## State of play — equitable mootness

The cross-circuit picture continues to fragment:

- **Fourth Circuit (April 27, 2026, NCBRC summary).** District court erred in dismissing a Chapter 13 appeal as equitably moot merely because a later plan was confirmed and payments began. Court emphasized the doctrine is "narrow" and "pragmatic," reserved for cases where effective relief is no longer practical or would be inequitable. This continues the trend of appellate courts narrowing the doctrine.
- **Fifth Circuit.** Has held that courts may bypass equitable mootness to rule on the merits — a meaningful procedural release valve.
- **Sixth Circuit.** Has held equitable mootness does not bar an appeal in a Chapter 7 case (Jones Day note).
- **Third Circuit.** Has continued to uphold equitable mootness in complex Chapter 11 cases where the plan is substantially consummated — Harvard Bankruptcy Roundtable's analysis remains the canonical summary.
- **Ninth Circuit.** Allowing broader exculpation clauses (Tonkon Torp note) — a separate but related plan-protection doctrine.

## Why this matters

For creditor-rights practitioners, the operational takeaway is unchanged: **file your stay motion early and pursue it aggressively at the bankruptcy-court level**, because:

1. The Fourth and Sixth Circuits have made it harder for debtors to invoke equitable mootness, but
2. The Third Circuit's substantial-consummation framework continues to bar appeals where the plan has gone effective — meaning a stay pending appeal remains the only reliable way to preserve appellate review of plan terms a creditor disputes.
3. Third-party releases remain the most aggressively litigated plan provision post-*Purdue*; the Third Circuit's "exceptional facts" framework (ABI summary) is now the leading template for what makes a non-debtor release survive appellate scrutiny.
