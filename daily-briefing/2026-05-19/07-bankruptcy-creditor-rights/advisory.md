## Lead Development

The U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware (Chief Judge Colm F. Connolly) issued a significant ruling on March 31, 2026, affirming a bankruptcy court order recognizing a Mexican concurso mercantil (bankruptcy-equivalent) proceeding as a foreign main proceeding under Chapter 15 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. Critically, the court held that nonconsensual third-party releases embedded in the foreign plan *survive* Harrington v. This is the first appellate-level ruling holding that Purdue's anti-release holding does not extend to Chapter 15 foreign-plan recognition. The decision has profound implications for multinational restructuring and mass-tort bankruptcy coordination.

## Key Developments (48-hour scan)

### 1. **District Court Rules Nonconsensual Third-Party Releases Survive Chapter 15 — March 31, 2026**
In the recognition proceeding for Crédito Real, S.A. (a Mexican corporate debtor undergoing concurso mercantil proceedings), the District Court of Delaware affirmed the bankruptcy court's order recognizing the foreign proceeding as a foreign main proceeding and granting full force and effect to the debtor's concurso plan, which included nonconsensual third-party releases (exculpations of parent companies, affiliates, and third-party lenders).

**Key holdings:**
- **Chapter 15 scope:** The court held that Chapter 15 (dealing with foreign bankruptcy recognition) is distinct from Chapter 11 (domestic corporate bankruptcies) and follows different statutory frameworks
- **Purdue limitation:** Harrington v. Purdue Pharma (2024 Supreme Court decision striking down nonconsensual third-party releases in Chapter 11 plans) does not apply to foreign plans recognized under Chapter 15
- **Reasoning:** Chapter 15's goal is to grant comity and enforcement effect to foreign proceedings; requiring Chapter 15 courts to impose stricter discharge/release standards than the foreign jurisdiction would contradict the comity framework
- **Practical holding:** When a foreign bankruptcy plan is recognized as a foreign main proceeding, the releases embedded in that plan are enforceable against U.S. creditors, notwithstanding Purdue

**Significance:** This is the first appellate-level authority on Purdue's extraterritorial scope. Previous Purdue commentary assumed its holding would apply to all federal bankruptcy contexts, but the Delaware District Court's ruling suggests a circuit split is forming.

### 2. **3M Aearo Technologies Bankruptcy Appeal — Status Update**
3M subsidiary Aearo Technologies continues appealing the federal judge's dismissal of its bankruptcy filing (originally filed June 2022; dismissed as "fatally premature" by the court in late 2023). Aearo filed for bankruptcy to address nearly 300,000 Combat Arms Earplug lawsuits from veterans and service members alleging defective earplugs sold to the U.S. military.

**Recent developments:**
- **$6 billion settlement reached (August 2024):** 3M agreed to pay $6 billion over six years to settle Aearo earplugs litigation
- **Ongoing appeal:** Aearo's appeal of the bankruptcy dismissal remains pending; outcome depends on whether bankruptcy court was correct in dismissing the case as premature
- **Practical resolution:** Despite bankruptcy case dismissal, the settlement provides class funding and claims administration, effectively resolving most underlying litigation

**Bankruptcy law issues:**
- Bankruptcy courts have discretion to dismiss cases filed in "bad faith" or deemed "premature" (when debtor is not truly insolvent)
- The 3M/Aearo case raised questions about whether mass-tort bankruptcies can be filed preemptively to achieve releases before insolvency is imminent
- Post-Purdue ruling (2024), the landscape for third-party releases in mass-tort bankruptcies has shifted significantly

### 3. **Purdue Pharma Docket Developments — Ongoing Plan Confirmation and Appeals**
The Supreme Court's Harrington v. Purdue Pharma decision (2024) struck down nonconsensual third-party releases in Chapter 11 plans as violating the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions. Post-Purdue, Purdue has modified its plan structure to eliminate certain third-party releases while preserving others (e.g., for Sackler family members as debtors, not non-debtors). The plan confirmation process has been delayed pending circuit-court guidance on the scope of permissible third-party exculpations.

**Outlook:** Expect Second Circuit and Third Circuit appellate guidance on Purdue's amended plan by Q3–Q4 2026.

## Strategic Commentary

The Crédito Real ruling represents a potential backdoor for multinational restructurings to preserve third-party releases despite Purdue's apparent prohibition. By filing a foreign bankruptcy (in Mexico, Cayman Islands, or BVI) and seeking U.S. recognition under Chapter 15, debtors may be able to import exculpations and releases that would be forbidden in a Chapter 11 plan. This creates regulatory arbitrage incentives and may trigger a response from Congress (potential amendment to Chapter 15) or appellate courts (potential Ninth/Eleventh Circuit limitation on Crédito Real's holding).

For mass-tort debtors (3M/Aearo, talc/asbestos, opioid manufacturers), the Crédito Real holding opens a path to achieve third-party releases in foreign proceedings without triggering Purdue strictures. However, the Sackler family's experience with Purdue shows that courts remain skeptical of releases for solvent non-debtor parties; expect continued litigation over the distinction between solvent and insolvent third-party releases.

**Bankruptcy creditor-rights impacts:**
- Creditors may find themselves bound by foreign-plan releases despite having no notice/opportunity to object (Chapter 15 comity framework)
- Indemnity claims against directors, officers, and parent companies may be precluded by foreign-plan releases even if the subsidiary is solvent
- Strategic debtors may forum-shop to foreign jurisdictions with relaxed third-party-release standards and seek U.S. Chapter 15 recognition
