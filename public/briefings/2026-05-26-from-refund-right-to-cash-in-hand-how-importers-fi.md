**IEEPA REFUND TRADE**

# From Refund Right to Cash in Hand: How Importers File and Sell an IEEPA Tariff Claim

*A Turnpage Digital Markets field guide for importers sitting on a federal refund right and deciding what to do with it.*

When the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariff regime in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* on February 20, 2026, it converted years of duty deposits into the largest involuntary federal receivable in modern customs history — by industry estimate, **over $175 billion across the importing community.** What it did not do is hand any importer cash. Between the SCOTUS opinion and a wire on Friday sits a filing process, a buyer's underwriting screen, and a closing set. This piece walks through all three, from the seller's chair.

The goal is simple: monetize a refund you have already earned, on a timeline you control, without giving away the upside or absorbing risks that belong to a buyer.

---

## Part One — File First. Everything Downstream Assumes It.

A refund right is not a tradable asset until it has been claimed. Buyers will not look at an undocumented exposure; lenders will not advance against it; even your auditor will treat it differently once a CAPE Declaration is on file.

**CAPE went live on April 20, 2026.** U.S. Customs and Border Protection deployed Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) inside the Automated Commercial Environment as the operational vehicle for IEEPA refunds, with the tool turning on at 8:00 a.m. EST that morning. The mechanics are straightforward: the Importer of Record — or a customs broker holding a valid power of attorney — uploads a single CSV listing up to **9,999 entry numbers per declaration**, through the importer's ACE Portal account. No invoices, no entry packages, no supporting attachments. CBP validates against its own records.

**Phase 1 is the fast lane.** It covers two populations: entries that are *unliquidated* on the filing date, and entries *liquidated within 80 days* of the filing date. The 80-day window mirrors the 90-day voluntary reliquidation authority under 19 U.S.C. § 1501 with a 10-day processing buffer baked in. CBP's published expectation is that **valid Phase 1 refunds will pay within 60 to 90 days** of declaration acceptance — wired electronically to the importer's banking instructions on file, with statutory interest from the original date of deposit. Phase 1 is projected to clear roughly **63%** of affected entries.

That leaves about **37% on the other side of the line** — entries that finally liquidated before the 80-day window opened. CBP's administrative authority to reopen those entries lapsed when the § 1501 clock ran out. The path forward is **Phase 2**, the planned operational lane CBP will use to execute Senior CIT Judge Richard K. Eaton's **March 27, 2026 amended order** in *V.O.S. Selections*, directing CBP to reliquidate *even those entries for which liquidation is already final.* As of mid-2026, Phase 2 has no announced launch date. That is its single most important feature for sellers — the **timing uncertainty is the trade.**

A third lane is still open in parallel: the **formal protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514**, with a 180-day window from liquidation. For entries that slipped past the 80-day window but remain inside the 180-day protest deadline, the protest route preserves rights even if Phase 2 stalls.

One more variable to calibrate before you file: the **November 10, 2025 rate cut.** Pre-Nov 10, IEEPA combined surcharges on China-origin goods ran 30% (20% fentanyl + 10% reciprocal). Post-Nov 10, the combined rate dropped to 20%. Your refund quantum will hinge on which side of that line each entry sat. Multi-line entries that mix both periods need to be reconciled at the entry level, not the line level — a common error in broker-prepared CAPE files that CBP will reject.

> *The headline number is what the refund could be. The defensible number is what survives validation. Filing accurately is the first place value walks out the door.*

## Part Two — What a Buyer Underwrites.

A claim purchaser is not paying for your hope. They are paying for a federal payable, less the time value of money, less every risk the buyer is taking on that you would otherwise hold. Understanding that arithmetic is how sellers stop leaving money on the table.

The buyer's process, compressed: **VALIDATE → SEGMENT → STRESS → PRICE.**

**Validate.** Every buyer starts with your CBP source data — typically the ES003 (line-level tariff detail) and ES701 (entry-level liquidation status), pulled from ACE or supplied by your broker. The first job is reconciling what your broker filed against what CBP actually shows: entry numbers, dates of import, duty paid by HTS, importer-of-record identity, payment status.

The fastest way to nuke a deal is **non-IEEPA contamination** — a CAPE file that mixes 9903.01.xx (IEEPA fentanyl + reciprocal) with 9903.88.xx (Section 301) or 9903.03.xx (Section 232). Section 301 and Section 232 duties are *not* refundable under the IEEPA ruling. Even small amounts of contamination can cause CBP to reject the entire entry, and a buyer underwriting a clean refund stream will haircut the file or walk.

A 2026 CBS News report on the CAPE program flagged that **CBP had already rejected roughly 15% of submitted refund claims** in the early months of the program — usually for the same handful of issues: wrong codes, broken bank instructions in ACE, IOR identity mismatches, or missing the 80-day window.

**Segment.** The buyer slots each entry into one of three pricing buckets. **Phase 1 eligible** (unliquidated or inside 80 days) is priced tightest — payment is near-term and CBP-administered. **Phase 2 candidates** (finally liquidated, dependent on Judge Eaton's order being implemented) are priced wider — timing unknown, operational risk real. **§ 1514 protest entries** (liquidated, still inside 180 days) are priced between the two — the path is clear but it requires affirmative filing, with its own deadline. Mix matters. A 100% Phase 1 file commands a materially different bid than a file weighted 60/40 toward Phase 2.

**Stress.** Beyond the CBP data, a buyer is underwriting **eight risk lanes** that sit on top of the refund right.

**1. ACE hygiene.** Is the importer's "Importer" sub-account current, with verified bank instructions? An expired or wrong wire path delays refunds by months.

**2. Surety and bond posture.** Did the surety pay any of the duty at issue? Were any entries subject to unpaid CBP bills, suspended protests, or AD/CVD holds?

**3. USMCA / drawback / reconciliation flags.** Entries with open drawback claims or USMCA duty-deferral elections may be excluded from CAPE altogether.

**4. Compliance posture.** Is the importer under a Focused Assessment, CF-28 inquiry, or open audit? CBP can hold a refund pending compliance review.

**5. Downstream consumer exposure.** Since early 2026, plaintiffs' firms have filed consumer class actions — including a high-profile case against Costco — pleading **unjust enrichment**, state UDAP statutes, and **"money had and received"** theories on the argument that retailers passed the tariff through and should disgorge the refund. Coverage notes from Reed Smith, Covington and Troutman Pepper Locke confirm the trend is accelerating.

**6. Contract pass-throughs.** Supplier indemnities, customer contracts with tariff pass-through clauses, and M&A purchase agreements signed during the IEEPA period may give downstream parties a contractual claim to part of the refund. Orrick and Mayer Brown both flag this as a deal point in current transactions.

**7. Solvency and lender consent.** Existing credit facilities frequently sweep "extraordinary receipts." Without lender consent, the refund proceeds may be trapped at the borrower level — a problem for any structure that pays a seller in cash at close.

**8. Assignment of Claims Act exposure.** The federal **Anti-Assignment of Claims Act (31 U.S.C. § 3727)** says an assignment of a claim against the United States is valid only after the claim has been allowed, the amount decided, and a warrant for payment issued. Its "assignment" definition is broad — it reaches not just transfers of the claim itself but **authorizations to receive payment.** Buyers structuring a transfer of an unliquidated refund have to navigate the ACA, not around it.

**Price.** After all of the above, the buyer applies a **discount rate** that accounts for two things together: the time value of money over an 18-to-36-month expected recovery (longer for Phase 2 and CIT-track entries), and the buyer's required return on capital deployed against this specific risk profile. The discount is not arbitrary — it is the explicit price of the risk lanes above.

> *A seller who shows up with a clean, segmented, reconciled file should expect a tighter bid than one who shows up with a broker spreadsheet. The diligence work is leverage.*

## Part Three — The Closing Set. What Papers Move the Trade.

A claim sale is not a wire against a handshake. The documents below are the standard closing set; the specific risk profile of any individual trade may add to or simplify the list.

**Confidentiality / NDA.** Mutual, narrow, with carve-outs for the buyer's deal team and financing sources. This goes out before any entry data moves.

**Term Sheet.** Identifies the claim universe (entries by importer-of-record number, date range, IEEPA codes), the purchase price formula (often expressed as a percentage of net refund proceeds, with segmentation by Phase 1 / Phase 2 / protest), the closing condition set, exclusivity, and the closing date.

**Claim Purchase Agreement (CPA).** The operative document. Its drafting tension is well-known to anyone who has bought federal claims: the ACA prevents a clean outright assignment of an unliquidated U.S. government claim, so the CPA has to combine (a) a present transfer of all economic and contractual rights the seller can transfer, (b) a covenant that the seller will hold any refund received in constructive trust for the buyer and remit on receipt, and (c) a true-up mechanism if CBP reduces, delays, or denies portions of the file. Reps and warranties usually cover entry-data accuracy, no prior assignments, no pledges or liens on the refund, no pending CBP audit on the entries, and disclosure of any consumer class action or supplier/customer claim that touches the refund.

**Limited Power of Attorney.** Authorizing the buyer (or its servicer) to communicate with CBP through the importer's broker, monitor CAPE status, and direct the broker on filing protests if needed. Drafted narrowly so it does not run afoul of the ACA.

**Servicing and Cooperation Covenants.** The seller commits to keeping the ACE Portal account active, maintaining valid bank instructions, signing any additional CBP forms required, and responding to CBP inquiries on a defined SLA. These covenants survive closing for the duration of the recovery.

**Direction Letter to Customs Broker.** Instructs the broker to honor the LPOA, to copy the buyer on CBP correspondence, and to forward CAPE acceptance/denial notices in real time.

**UCC-1 Financing Statement.** Even where a full assignment is impossible, the buyer typically perfects a security interest in the refund proceeds as a backstop against the seller's other creditors. UCC Article 9 § 9-408(d) means the buyer cannot compel CBP to redirect the wire — but it does establish priority against third parties post-receipt.

**Officer's Certificate, Good Standing, Authorizing Resolutions.** Standard corporate authority documentation.

**Tax Allocation Language.** Whether the refund is ordinary income, a reduction of prior cost of goods, or a § 1341 claim-of-right adjustment matters to both sides. CBIZ, Thomson Reuters, and the major accounting firms have published practitioner guidance on the trade-offs. Both the CPA and any side letters should be explicit about which party books which piece.

**Escrow or Holdback.** Where consumer class action risk, supplier indemnity exposure, or pre-existing CBP compliance issues are present, a portion of the purchase price is often held back in escrow for an agreed period to absorb claw-backs.

**W-9 and Wire Instructions.** Final, verified, and locked.
