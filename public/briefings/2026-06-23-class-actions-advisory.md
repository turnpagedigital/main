### I. *Maheshwari v. Hut 8 Corp.* — S.D.N.Y. | $2.35M Securities Act Settlement Filed June 22, 2026


The settlement memorandum of law was filed June 22, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York before Judge Victor Marrero; the original complaint had been filed February 7, 2024, and centered on Hut 8's all-stock merger with USBTC, which closed in November 2023.
 
Investors claimed that Hut 8 made materially misleading statements about the transaction's benefits while failing to disclose persistent energy curtailment and internet connectivity problems at the King Mountain joint venture, a Texas-based Bitcoin mining operation in which USBTC held a 50% interest.


The litigation posture heading into the settlement is worth unpacking. 
The litigation had already been narrowed before the settlement — Judge Marrero dismissed the investors' Exchange Act claims and also rejected Securities Act claims tied to alleged misstatements about USBTC's financial condition before the merger, but allowed part of the Securities Act case to proceed over alleged omissions related to risks at King Mountain.
 That narrowing to a Securities Act-only case is structurally significant: Exchange Act § 10(b) claims require scienter; Securities Act § 11 claims do not, but they carry strict traceability requirements that cut the other way. 
Defendants had indicated they planned to challenge traceability on the pleadings, arguing that unregistered shares issued to Legacy Hut shareholders were commingled with registered shares issued to USBTC shareholders, making it nearly impossible to trace aftermarket purchases back to the registration statement.
 That traceability argument — a recurring asymmetry in merger-registration securities cases — is what gave defendants meaningful leverage in mediation.


The proposed settlement was reached after mediation; the parties took part in a full-day virtual mediation session on May 7 before JAMS mediator Jed Melnick.
 
Melnick subsequently issued a mediator's proposal of $2,350,000, which both sides accepted on May 13, 2026.
 
Lead Plaintiff Abhishek Maheshwari is represented by Pomerantz LLP, which serves as Lead Counsel, with Bronstein, Gewirtz & Grossman, LLC as additional counsel.


On recovery math: 
plaintiff counsel estimated the $2.35 million settlement represents approximately 19.6% of the maximum recoverable damages of roughly $12.08 million — a recovery rate described in the filing as above recent averages for Securities Act-only settlements.
 
The settlement still requires preliminary and final approval from Judge Marrero.
 
Context on Hut 8's current financial position: the company signed a $7 billion, 15-year lease for an AI data center campus in Louisiana backed by Google in December 2025, and closed a $4.25 billion Texas data center bond in June 2026.
 
A related action on behalf of Canadian investors was also filed in the Ontario Superior Court of Justice in December 2025.


Claims-rate and distribution timing for this matter are not yet set — the preliminary approval motion is pending. Class members should monitor the court docket in S.D.N.Y. for the preliminary approval order, notice dissemination schedule, and claims deadline once set.

### II. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Pending; Distributions Imminent

This matter remains the single largest copyright settlement in U.S. history and the one with the most direct near-term distribution implications for rights-holders tracking this desk. The posture as of today: fairness hearing was held; final approval order has not yet entered; distributions are calendared for June 2026 or later, contingent on final approval and resolution of any appeals.

Recap of the settlement mechanics relevant to recovery value: 
Anthropic will pay $1.5 billion into a settlement fund.
 
Anthropic is funding the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The phased-payment structure matters for distribution timing — not all funds will be on hand at once, which creates a sequenced disbursement problem the administrator must work through post-approval.


The final approval fairness hearing took place on May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California.
 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work will be approximately $3,100; the class had a 92.77% participation rate for works on the works list.
 
The high rate of claims means the forecasts about a bigger payout based on the March 19 report of 54% participation will not materialize, and the actual payout will likely end up closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.
 That denominator dynamic — more claimants compressing per-unit recovery — is the central arithmetic lesson here for anyone tracking claims-rate strategy across similar copyright class actions.

On fee exposure: 
authors urged the court to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Separately, Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees that nonclass counsel requested is "far too high," with "scant justification" for the request.
 The fee contest — with two separate fee applications and an objection from the defendant as to one — means the court's final order on attorneys' fees could still move the net-to-class number meaningfully.


Per the Authors Guild timeline, June 2026 was designated for the settlement administrator to calculate distributions, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later.
 The June 11 calculation date has likely passed; distribution onset now turns on when Judge Martínez-Olguín enters the final approval order and whether any objector appeals.

Post-hearing procedure: 
following the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín issued a post-hearing order requiring Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages by May 21, 2026, addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 The docket's last recorded filing, per tracked-case ground truth, was June 4, 2026 (Entry No. 674 — order to withdraw as attorney). No final approval order has appeared on the CourtListener docket as of the June 21 sync. Rights-holders awaiting payment should treat July 2026 as the earliest plausible distribution-onset date, subject to that order and the absence of a Ninth Circuit notice of appeal.

### III. Keller Postman v. Google — Mass Arbitration | Clayton Act § 4 Treble-Damages Campaign

This campaign, formally announced May 11, 2026, is now the largest mass arbitration effort ever filed by claimed dollar value and warrants tracking alongside traditional class action mega-settlements for anyone in the advertiser or media-spend space.


In April 2026, Keller Postman's Ashley Keller began filing mass arbitration claims against Google/Alphabet on behalf of advertisers, seeking damages tied to two federal court rulings in 2024 that found Google illegally monopolized online search and advertising technology; potential claims are estimated at $218 billion or more.
 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist the firm retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability.


The structural predicate: 
federal judges in both the District of Columbia — Judge Amit P. Mehta — and the Eastern District of Virginia — Judge Leonie M. Brinkema — ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 The arbitration campaign is a private-enforcement action downstream of those liability findings, invoking Clayton Act § 4's treble-damage remedy. 
Google's advertiser contracts require mandatory arbitration, blocking court access and funneling claims into exactly the kind of mass arbitration process that has cost companies billions in prior campaigns.



According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade; experts in the federal antitrust cases estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs, creating the potential for substantial recovery under federal antitrust law.
 
Keller Postman's published position is that most qualifying businesses could recover up to 30% of their total Google Ads spend during the relevant period, with eligible businesses potentially receiving $10,000 or more depending on ad spend.


On remedies-phase exposure that will affect settlement pressure: 
Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad tech case remains expected in 2026; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines.
 
On the search side, Google filed an appeal in January 2026 seeking to pause data-sharing mandates imposed by the September 2025 remedies ruling in the Mehta case, adding further legal uncertainty to the regulatory picture.


For claimants evaluating this campaign: 
any business that purchased ads on Google in August 2016 or later may qualify for a claim.
 
Keller Postman cautions that deadlines vary by state, and that earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.
 The FAA governs the arbitral proceedings; the substantive rights arise under the Sherman Act §§ 1–2 and Clayton Act § 4. Because these are individual arbitrations — not a certified class — each claimant's recovery is computed on its own ad spend, without the dilution dynamic present in a claims-made class structure.


In addition to representing private claimants in arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state Attorneys General in a parallel case.
 That parallel track is meaningful: state AG enforcement signals institutional validation of the legal theory and creates additional settlement pressure independent of the mass arbitration volume.

### IV. Bartz Liability Architecture — Residual Risk to Other LLM Defendants

The *Bartz* settlement resolves the piracy-specific claims against Anthropic but leaves unresolved the broader liability question for AI companies whose training datasets include web-scraped content without obvious licensing. 
In June 2025, Judge William Alsup split the liability question — ruling that Anthropic's use of legally acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and protected as fair use, but simultaneously holding that downloading and keeping pirated copies was not fair use.
 
The massive payout for downloading and storing books from LibGen raises questions about websites scraped online without obvious permission; for AI companies outside the U.S., this case is a warning about American legal risk, and even if AI training ultimately proves legal, defending a class action can cost millions just to get to trial.
 
With statutory damages in play, the downside risk is catastrophic.


For claim-recovery counsel tracking downstream copyright class actions against OpenAI, Meta, and other LLM developers: *Bartz* effectively bifurcates the exposure universe into (a) legally-acquired training data — likely protected post-Alsup — and (b) piracy-origin training data — where statutory damages exposure per title, across millions of works, generates the kind of quantum that compels settlement at figures approaching $1.5 billion. The opt-out track from *Bartz* — with ClaimsHero Holdings LLC having been publicly rebuked twice by the district court for allegedly misleading authors to opt out in pursuit of a separate settlement — illustrates how third-party claim solicitation firms can create administrative friction and appellate exposure even in well-structured mega-settlements.
