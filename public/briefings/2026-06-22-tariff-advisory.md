# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Monday, June 22, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Three interlocking developments define the week**: the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal of Judge Eaton's IEEPA refund order is the most immediate threat to the $95B+ CAPE pipeline; the Federal Circuit's June 11 stay-pending-appeal of the Section 122 injunction resets the 19 U.S.C. § 2132 clock with 32 days left before July 24 expiration; and USTR Jamieson Greer's June 3 Section 301 "forced labor" tariff proposal — 10–12.5% on 60 trading partners — represents the administration's third attempt to rebuild a durable global tariff wall, with a public-comment deadline of July 6 and a hearing set for July 7. Today's new delta: India's Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal confirmed publicly this morning that New Delhi is pressing for a tariff advantage over ASEAN peers as a precondition to finalizing a bilateral trade agreement, and he would be "happy" if a deal closes before the July 24 § 122 expiration — a signal that bilateral negotiations and the § 122 litigation timeline are now directly linked.

### I. IEEPA Refund Pipeline: DOJ Federal Circuit Appeal & CAPE Phase 2 Launch (June 29)

The central refund mechanic remains the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system, administered by CBP under the supervision of CIT Judge Richard Eaton in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection* (the current lead case). 
CAPE Phase 1 is active, Phase 2 — covering reconciliation and antidumping/countervailing duty entries — is set to launch June 29, 2026, and Phase 3, covering finally liquidated entries and available only to importers who have filed at the CIT, is on track for late July 2026. Phase 1 claims accepted cover approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, with approximately $23 billion approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.
 
The government has represented that by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.
 
To underscore the scale: in all of last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


The DOJ's June 3 notice of appeal to the Federal Circuit is the most consequential litigation risk in the pipeline. 
On June 2–3, 2026, DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's Refund Order, contesting specifically the CIT's authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries that are both liquidated and past the 80-day window in which CBP can reprocess them.
 
DOJ previewed its arguments in its May 29 court filing, targeting two fronts: (1) CBP cannot reprocess older entries without a court order specific to each importer; and (2) the Refund Order is an impermissible "universal injunction" that exceeds the CIT's authority.
 The government leans heavily on *Trump v. CASA, 606 U.S. 831 (2025)*, arguing that 
Judge Eaton's orders are impermissible universal injunctions because they extend relief beyond the named plaintiffs.
 Plaintiffs will counter that CASA does not control because 
the CIT is not an ordinary district court, and its customs jurisdiction is closely tied to the administration of a uniform tariff regime.


At the CIT, 
at the June 9 hearing, DOJ reiterated its position that the CIT erred and the court cannot order the government to refund money to non-plaintiffs.
 Judge Eaton denied DOJ's attempt to excuse CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott from live testimony: 
Judge Eaton quickly denied DOJ's motion, acknowledging the separation of powers concerns but explaining that the Commissioner's testimony is necessary and that the Government's obligation to return all unlawfully collected duties is clear.


The structural risk for non-litigants is acute. 
For importers who have not filed suit, DOJ's position is that these importers must file a lawsuit at the CIT — and obtain their own court order — to secure refunds for entries past the 80-day window.
 
That issue is particularly important because the universe of finally liquidated entries is larger than many companies assume: CBP often liquidates entries more quickly than the outer statutory timeline, especially for lower-value or informal entries, and post-summary corrections can also accelerate liquidation.
 The two-year CIT filing statute is running. Section 1581(i) is the operative jurisdictional hook — 
importers that have filed Section 1581(i) protective actions are best positioned to recover their full IEEPA refunds; importers that have not filed are likely, at a minimum, to face significant delays; depending on the ultimate outcome, they may not recover certain refunds at all.

### II. Section 122 (19 U.S.C. § 2132): Federal Circuit Stay-Pending-Appeal Granted June 11; 32 Days to Expiration


On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge CIT panel, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606).
 
The CIT held that Proclamation 11012 exceeded the authority under Section 122, rejecting the administration's argument that the United States' goods trade deficit and current-account deficit satisfied the statutory predicate, reasoning that "balance-of-payments deficits" and "fundamental international payments problems" are distinct from a long-running trade imbalance.
 
The 2-1 decision resulted in a permanent injunction prohibiting the collection of Section 122 duties — but only with respect to Burlap & Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington.


The critical update this week: 
on June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motion for a stay pending appeal, pausing enforcement of the CIT injunction on collection of Section 122 duties against the State of Washington and the two private business plaintiffs.
 In granting the stay, the Federal Circuit applied the traditional four-factor test, implicitly crediting the government's likelihood of success on the merits — a meaningful signal about the appellate court's tentative statutory reading. 
The Federal Circuit consolidated two related appeals under a single briefing schedule and consolidated caption, with claims brought by the States of Oregon and Washington, along with Burlap & Barrel and Basic Fun.


The hard constraint: 
the Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026, in any event.
 
Given the overlap with the earlier IEEPA litigation, the case could reach the Supreme Court, which would significantly delay a final decision on the legality of the Section 122 tariffs; meanwhile, those tariffs are set to expire pursuant to the statutory limit on July 24, 2026, unless explicitly extended by Congress.
 With the stay in place through at least July 24, the litigation's practical refund value may be limited to the named plaintiffs unless either (a) the administration extends the § 122 authorization or (b) the Federal Circuit rules on the merits before expiration. Neither is likely. The more durable path for non-plaintiff importers remains preservation: 
importers that have paid Section 122 tariffs but have not initiated litigation should evaluate whether to file suit at the CIT, submit protests to CBP, or pursue other claim-preservation strategies; CAPE is currently limited to IEEPA tariffs and does not provide an administrative refund channel for Section 122.

### III. Section 301 "Forced Labor" Tariff Proposal: 10–12.5% on 60 Economies, Comment Period Through July 6

The administration's third tariff instrument entered a new phase on June 3. 
USTR proposed additional tariffs of up to 12.5% on imports from 60 economies over their failure to ban goods made with forced labor, with the determination made under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, finding that all 60 countries have failed to impose or effectively enforce a prohibition on forced labor-related imports.
 
Most of the 60 trading partners face a proposed 12.5% rate — including China, Japan, South Korea, and Brazil — while a lower 10% rate applies to 16 trading partners including the United Kingdom, Canada, Mexico, the EU, Taiwan, and Argentina, which the USTR characterizes as taking some steps toward enforcement.
 
The USTR said it would exempt from tariffs products including energy, rare earths and some other metals, beef, coffee, certain fruits and vegetables, pharmaceuticals, organic chemicals, and aircraft parts.



The USTR will accept public comments on the proposed tariffs and other remedies through July 6, with a public hearing scheduled for July 7.
 
The tariffs still need to go through the comment process before taking effect.
 This matters for refund math: unlike the IEEPA and § 122 regimes — both of which have been struck down at the CIT level — Section 301 authority is well-established and substantially insulated from the *Learning Resources* holding. 
The Supreme Court decision did not overturn duties assessed by CBP under Section 232 or Section 301.
 If these proposed Section 301 rates finalize, importers currently modeling refund recovery against a tariff-free post-IEEPA baseline will need to recalibrate: a 10–12.5% duty imposed under a legally durable authority replaces the struck-down IEEPA regime at comparable rate levels for most goods.

### IV. India–U.S. Bilateral: July 24 Clock Is Now a Trade-Deal Deadline

Today's Reuters/Business Standard reporting introduces a live political variable. 
New Delhi is seeking tariff concessions superior to those offered to competing Asian economies as it works toward finalizing a bilateral trade agreement with the United States; a final deal was delayed when the Supreme Court invalidated Trump's sweeping global tariffs.
 
Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal stated on Monday: "We are trying to work out with the US how they will ensure that we will get a comparative advantage, so that our exporters can benefit," adding that he would be "happy" if a deal is finalized before July 24, when Washington's temporary 10% tariff on trading partners expires.
 
Negotiations have been further complicated by the USTR's Section 301 investigations into India and other countries, which analysts say Washington is using to press India to open its markets for agricultural and other products.
 
New Delhi is specifically seeking a competitive tariff edge over regional peers, including ASEAN members such as Vietnam.


The refund implication is indirect but real. Any bilateral deal that fixes permanent tariff rates for India — or establishes a most-favored-nation-equivalent peg — could retroactively affect the refund math on entries from February 24 through July 24, 2026, depending on how agreement-specific provisions treat prior-period collections under § 122. Importers with Indian-origin supply chains should model both the refund-recovery scenario and the new-tariff scenario simultaneously.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and claims-recovery counsel should execute the following actions in priority sequence before the July 24 § 122 expiration: **(1) CAPE Phase 2 enrollment** — Phase 2 launches June 29 and will cover reconciliation entries and AD/CVD-affected entries that Phase 1 excluded; ensure ACE portal settings are current and broker authorizations are in place for CSV declaration submission under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest mechanics; **(2) CIT Section 1581(i) filing** — any importer with finally liquidated entries (past the 80-day CAPE window) who has not filed a protective action at the CIT should file before the two-year liquidation anniversary; the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal makes this non-optional for entries outside Phase 1 and Phase 3 coverage; **(3) Section 122 protest preservation** — file or amend protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 for all entries liquidated under Proclamation 11012 (HTSUS 9903.03.01), noting the *Burlap and Barrel* CIT ruling as the legal basis; the Federal Circuit stay does not extinguish protest rights, and the § 122 tariff expiration on July 24 does not resolve pending claims; **(4) Section 301 comment period** — clients with material exposure to proposed forced-labor tariffs should submit comments to USTR by July 6 and request hearing time on July 7; the scope of product exclusions and the textile mechanism are still subject to modification and respond to targeted advocacy; **(5) India supply-chain modeling** — importers sourcing from India should maintain dual scenario models for the refund-recovery and new-tariff outcomes given the live bilateral negotiation; do not liquidate Section 122 positions on Indian-origin entries until deal terms, if any, are published.
