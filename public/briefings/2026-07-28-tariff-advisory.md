## Analysis & Developments

### I. The Section 301 Successor Regime Is Already Under Attack — New CIT Filings, Day Four

The Section 122 global surcharge expired at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, exactly 150 days after the Supreme Court's February 20 IEEPA ruling — its statutory maximum under 19 U.S.C. § 2132. 
The new tariffs — ranging from 10% to 12.5% on imports from 60 countries accounting for roughly 99.4% of U.S. imports — took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET, replacing the temporary 10% global levy imposed under Section 122 that expired the same day.
 The administration did not pause. Within hours, two separate lawsuits landed at the U.S. Court of International Trade in New York.


The Liberty Justice Center filed suit in the CIT on July 24, 2026, challenging the new tariffs imposed under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, on behalf of Burlap & Barrel, Inc., a New York-based single-origin spice retailer, and Collective Horology, a California-based watch retailer and distributor.
 The caption is *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. et al. v. Greer et al.*, CIT No. 1:26-cv-03345 (__[Liberty Justice Center](https://libertyjusticecenter.org/cases/burlap-and-barrel-inc-v-greer-et-al/)__). 
The complaint asks the court to declare the new tariffs unlawful, halt their enforcement, and secure full refunds — with interest — for affected importers.
 
It was filed as a proposed class action covering all importers of record who will pay the new tariffs.



A second lawsuit challenging the Section 301 tariffs was filed on Friday on behalf of seven businesses, including two educational toymakers — Learning Resources Inc. and hand2mind Inc. — that were involved in the earlier IEEPA tariff litigation that reached the Supreme Court.
 
Learning Resources was a party to the consolidated Supreme Court litigation in which the justices invalidated Trump's emergency powers tariffs, though its own case was dismissed on jurisdictional grounds.


The statutory theory is distinct from — and meaningfully harder than — the IEEPA challenge. 
Section 301 authorizes USTR to investigate whether a particular foreign government's act, policy, or practice is unreasonable or discriminatory and burdens or restricts U.S. commerce; when the statutory requirements are met, USTR may take responsive action that is "appropriate and feasible" to obtain the elimination of the identified foreign practice. But Congress did not give USTR universal power to impose a predetermined global tariff policy.
 The complaint in *Burlap & Barrel* alleges that 
the new tariffs failed to reflect the "country-specific inquiry" that Congress expected when it adopted Section 301.
 
Jeffrey Schwab, a lawyer at the Liberty Justice Center representing the small businesses, said that Section 301 does not include any authority "to tax substantially all imports from substantially all countries at preestablished rates."


The government's litigation headroom here is wider. 
Experts say it might be tougher to successfully challenge the current round of tariffs than previous rounds — Trump used Section 301 to impose big tariffs on China in his first term, and they survived court challenges.
 Holland & Knight's Patrick Childress, a former U.S. trade official, was direct: 
"these tariffs will be with us for the long haul." Even if countries enact the precise policies the U.S. wants, he said, they will still need to prove that they're enforcing them to Washington's satisfaction before the tariffs are removed — "this suggests that no short-term path for country-wide relief from the new Section 301 tariffs will be available."
 (__[Bangor Daily News](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2026/07/25/nation/small-businesses-file-lawsuits-against-trumps-new-sweeping-tariffs/)__)

For Rewind Tariffs clients, the practical upshot is immediate: these challenges have been filed as class actions and the plaintiffs are expressly seeking refunds with interest, but the Section 301 regime does not carry the same jurisdictional clarity as the IEEPA framework. The refund math resets to zero for July 24-forward entries. The IEEPA refund pipeline — the one that actually moves money — is governed by a separate and still-contested track.

### II. CAPE Phase 3 Is Live (or Nearly So) — But DOJ's Federal Circuit Appeal Holds the Finally-Liquidated Pool Hostage

This is the most consequential refund-pipeline development since the June 9 CIT hearing, and it directly governs whether approximately 37% of the $166 billion IEEPA pool reaches importers without individual litigation.

**The current state of the pipeline.** 
CBP's updated declaration, filed with the Court of International Trade, fixes the total IEEPA duties collected at $166 billion — spanning April 2025 through February 2026 — across approximately 330,000 importers and 53 million entries; this is the full refundable pool.
 
As of July 10, $121.75 billion in claims had been accepted for processing, $104.29 billion had been authorized as of the June 29 filing, and $86.3 billion had actually been repaid to importers including statutory interest.
 (__[TariffsToolcom](https://www.tariffstool.com/guides/tariff-refunds-86-billion-paid-june-surge-2026)__) 
Two weeks before July 10, the figure stood at $71 billion; two weeks before that, roughly $40 billion.
 The velocity is real, though it is decelerating as the low-hanging unliquidated entries are processed.

**Phase 3 — the contested tranche.** 
CAPE Phase 3 addresses finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs. CBP confirmed at a June 9, 2026 hearing before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton in the CIT that programming for Phase 3 would be ready in late July 2026. Susan Thomas, executive assistant commissioner for CBP's trade office, delivered that update.
 The case docket is *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, CIT Court No. 25-00066. (__[Green Worldwide Shipping](https://www.greenworldwide.com/cape-phase-3-for-ieepa-tariff-refunds-on-track-for-end-of-july-as-federal-circuit-appeal-continues/)__)

Technical readiness and legal authority are two different things. 
On July 17, 2026, CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued an order directing CBP to "reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, any and all of Plaintiffs' entries that have been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which Plaintiffs made estimated deposits pursuant to IEEPA." The order followed the recent transfer to Judge Eaton's docket of more than 3,700 cases filed with the CIT seeking refunds of IEEPA duties.
 (__[Thompson Hine SmarTrade](https://www.thompsonhinesmartrade.com/2026/07/cit-orders-cbp-to-process-ieepa-tariff-refunds-for-phase-3-finally-liquidated-entries/)__) That is the carve: the order runs to *plaintiffs* — importers who filed CIT actions. Non-litigants remain exposed.


On June 2, 2026, DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's Refund Order. DOJ contests the CIT's authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries that are both liquidated and past the 80-day window in which CBP can reprocess them.
 (__[Jackson Walker](https://www.jw.com/news/insights-ieepa-refund-order/)__) 
DOJ's two main appellate arguments: first, CBP cannot reprocess older entries without a court order specific to each importer, meaning each importer seeking refunds must file a lawsuit at the CIT; and second, the Refund Order is an overly broad "universal injunction" that exceeds what the CIT is allowed to order.
 
The Federal Circuit appeal focuses on whether the CIT's exclusive jurisdiction and the Constitution's Uniformity Clause permit universal refund relief, while CBP continues processing CAPE refunds for unliquidated and nonfinal entries.
 (__[Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/government-plans-to-appeal-universal-ieepa-tariff-refund-order/)__)

The government's position carries a known internal tension. 
In December 2025, in *AGS Company Automotive Solutions v. CBP* (Slip Op. 25-154), DOJ affirmatively represented to the CIT that it "will not object to the [c]ourt ordering reliquidation of plaintiff's entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful." The CIT found this constituted an "unequivocal position" and held the government judicially estopped from reversing course.
 Whether that estoppel argument migrates successfully to the Federal Circuit briefing — and forecloses DOJ's universal-injunction theory — is the live appellate question. Briefing schedules have not been publicly confirmed as of this writing.

**The Phase 3 access point.** 
Critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 (__[Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/ieepa-tariff-refund-update-government-appeals)__) 
Importers who do not file a lawsuit at the CIT risk facing delays or permanent loss of refunds on finally liquidated entries and other categories outside CAPE Phase 1.
 The 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i) jurisdictional gateway — the residual CIT jurisdiction track — remains the vehicle; the statute of limitations has not expired, but the window is not unlimited.

### III. The Supreme Court Ruling and Its Structural Legacy

The predicate for all of the above. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed the Federal Circuit's en banc ruling that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the President to impose tariffs.
 
The Court affirmed 6-3 in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts.
 (__[SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/trump-v-v-o-s-selections/)__) The consolidated cases were *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 24-1287, 607 U.S. ___ (2026), and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, No. 25-250, 607 U.S. ___ (2026). (__[Supreme Court slip op.](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1287_4gcj.pdf)__)


The Federal Circuit, sitting en banc, held that IEEPA's authority to "regulate importation" did not authorize the tariffs because their scope, amount, and duration were unbounded.
 The CIT proceedings below — V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States, 772 F. Supp. 3d 1350 (2025) — remain the jurisdictional anchor for the 3,700+ consolidation cases now before Judge Eaton.


According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 (__[White & Williams](https://www.whiteandwilliams.com/resources-alerts-IEEPA-Tariff-Refunds-CBP-Launches-CAPE-Process)__) That pool — and the administrative architecture built to return it — is what CBP's CAPE system was designed to handle.

### IV. Section 122 Expiration: What It Changes and What It Doesn't


The Section 122 tariffs were designed as a temporary replacement for the IEEPA tariffs and expired after 150 days per the terms of the Trade Act.
 (__[ASI Central](https://members.asicentral.com/news/industry-news/july-2026/small-businesses-file-lawsuits-against-trump-administration-for-new-global-tariffs/)__) A challenge to the Section 122 surcharge was filed by Oregon and other plaintiffs; 
a separate challenge to the temporary replacement tariffs under Section 122 remains on appeal.
 (__[Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/27/trump-tariff-court-small-business)__) That appeal is not mooted by expiration — the question of whether Section 122 duties were validly imposed bears directly on whether those collections are subject to refund under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest procedures and the CAPE pipeline.

The Section 122 expiration also resets the rate environment going forward. 
The Trump administration announced July 23 that the U.S. will collect duties of between 10% and 12.5% on imports from most major trading partners, with the move under Section 301 following a probe into the alleged failure of around 60 economies to prevent forced labor in their supply chains.
 (__[Insurance Journal / Reuters](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/07/27/879045.htm)__) For importers who were paying 10% under Section 122, the headline rate is unchanged or marginally higher. The tariff wall remains up; only the statutory authority has shifted.


Collective Horology co-founder Asher Rapkin told Axios that the earlier tariff regimes cost the watch retailer more than $160,000, with refunds from the earlier invalidated tariffs still outstanding. "Section 301 tariffs are most definitely going to hamstring my business in the exact same way that the previous two phases of tariffs have," Rapkin said. The company imports watches from Switzerland, where there is virtually no U.S. manufacturing alternative.
 That anecdote is representative: for importers with no domestic substitute, the refund claim on IEEPA and potentially Section 122 entries is the only near-term financial lever.

## Recommended Actions

Clients with IEEPA refund exposure should act on two tracks simultaneously. First, confirm Phase 1 and Phase 2 CAPE declarations are complete and accepted in the ACE Portal — 
CAPE allows importers and customs brokers to submit IEEPA refund requests through the ACE portal, with Phase 1 covering most entries where IEEPA duties were paid
 (__[GHY Trade Compliance](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/cbp-cape-ieepa-refund-progress/)__) — and note that as of July 1, 
warehouse entries are no longer accepted on CAPE declarations
, so the filing window for certain entry types has closed. Second, and more urgently, clients with finally liquidated entries (liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period) who have not filed a CIT action should consult trade counsel immediately: 
for importers with finally liquidated entries who have not filed suit, this is where the DOJ appeal creates the most meaningful risk
 (__[Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/government-plans-to-appeal-universal-ieepa-tariff-refund-order/)__), and the July 17 Eaton order providing relief runs only to CIT plaintiffs. On the Section 301 front, clients who began importing after July 24, 2026 under the new forced-labor framework should document their entries meticulously and monitor *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. et al. v. Greer et al.*, CIT No. 1:26-cv-03345, for any preliminary injunction motion — the Liberty Justice Center's track record at the CIT and Federal Circuit suggests it will move quickly, and any TRO or preliminary injunction could create a protest-filing opportunity under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 for entries liquidated during the litigation window.
