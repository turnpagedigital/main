### I. Federal Circuit Stays the CIT's Section 122 Ruling — Pendency Now Runs Through July 24 Expiry Date

As of June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's request to stay the CIT injunction pending appeal, allowing the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 tariffs while the appeal proceeds. This is a decisive procedural escalation from the initial administrative hold that the court entered on May 12. In granting the full stay, the Federal Circuit found that the government showed a sufficient likelihood of success on appeal and that the CIT's interpretation of Section 122 may be incorrect.

The underlying CIT decision — *State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026) — was a 2-1 ruling in which a two-judge majority (Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly) granted summary judgment for the importer plaintiffs, holding that the duties imposed through Proclamation 11012 exceeded the President's statutory authority. The dispute boiled down to the definition of the phrase "balance-of-payments deficits," with the Court of International Trade rejecting the Trump administration's argument that the term in Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 is the same as a "trade deficit." Dissenting, Judge Stanceu disagreed with the majority's interpretation of the legislative history that liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance are the only methods to measure "balance-of-payments," explaining that had Congress intended to limit its measurement according to these factors, it would have been more explicit in doing so.

The practical consequence of the Federal Circuit's June 11 stay is stark: the practical result is continued tariff exposure. The Section 122 duties remain collectible for now, and the stay likely delays any plaintiff-specific refund relief that would have flowed from the CIT injunction. The permanent injunction extends only to the three importer plaintiffs who were found to have standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc. and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.

The statutory clock remains the overriding pressure point. The Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026, in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely. Given the stakes and the overlap with earlier IEEPA litigation, the case could reach the Supreme Court, which would significantly delay a final decision on the legality of the Section 122 tariffs. Another potential avenue for the administration is simply to issue a new proclamation grounded in the specific balance-of-payments metrics the majority identified as permissible, although it is unclear if this would retroactively "cure" the deficiencies of the proclamation for past entries.

Importers who have paid the Section 122 10% surcharge — which HTSUS Chapter 99 classifies under tariff item **9903.03.01** — must now decide whether to file their own CIT actions to obtain party-specific injunctive relief before July 24, 2026 renders the question moot. Importers considering such litigation must weigh the potential for obtaining an injunction against the possibility that follow-on litigation will be stayed pending the CAFC appeal and that the Section 122 tariffs are set to expire on July 24, 2026. Importers have paid an estimated $25 billion in these duties since February 24, just 72 days of collection — refund exposure for the government, if the CIT ruling is ultimately affirmed, is potentially enormous and runs parallel to the far larger IEEPA pool.

### II. Section 122 — Statutory Framework and the "Balance-of-Payments" Fault Line

The constitutional and statutory fault line exposed by *Burlap and Barrel* tracks directly from the February 20, 2026 Supreme Court IEEPA decision. The Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs absent clear congressional authorization. In a 6–3 decision, the Court concluded that although IEEPA permits the President to "regulate" importation during a declared national emergency, that language does not clearly authorize the imposition of tariffs. The Court emphasized that the Constitution assigns to Congress the authority to "lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises" and applied separation-of-powers principles in determining that tariff authority of significant economic and political magnitude requires explicit statutory delegation.

Following the February 20 ruling, President Trump issued an Executive Order terminating the additional ad valorem duties previously imposed under IEEPA, consistent with the Supreme Court's ruling. He then issued a Proclamation invoking Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 to impose a "temporary" 10% ad valorem duty on most goods entering the United States. Section 122 authorizes the President to impose a surcharge of up to 15% for no more than 150 days to address "fundamental international payments problems," including "large and serious United States balance-of-payments deficits."

The CIT majority found that Proclamation 11012 failed this statutory predicate. At the oral argument stage on April 10, 2026, the government acknowledged that balance-of-payments deficits are measured differently today than in 1974, and that basic balance and liquidity are no longer considered relevant measures. That concession became the sword on which the majority impaled the government's case: if the government's own economists no longer track the metrics Congress wrote into the statute, the proclamation cannot satisfy the statutory trigger.

For recovery counsel, the Section 122 analysis matters both on its own terms and as a preview of the Federal Circuit's appetite to police executive overreach on tariffs. The court of appeals may expedite the appeal similar to how it expedited the appeal that led to the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling against tariffs under IEEPA. An expedited briefing calendar would push a merits opinion before or shortly after the July 24 statutory sunset — and a Federal Circuit affirmance post-sunset would still preserve refund claims for the ~72-day collection window.

### III. IEEPA Refund Track: CBP's June 9 Hearing, CAPE Reconciliation, and Judge Eaton's Show-Cause Orders

While the Section 122 fight escalates at the Federal Circuit, the IEEPA refund administration track entered critical territory this week at the CIT. CBP claimed that its Commissioner (as a Senate-confirmed official) could not be required to testify on June 9 as CIT ordered absent "extraordinary circumstances," and requested that CBP's Executive Assistant Commissioner be allowed to testify instead of the Commissioner. Judge Eaton promptly denied CBP's motion, and the government filed an appeal on June 2, 2026.

At the June 9 hearing itself, CBP delivered significant programmatic commitments. CBP told the CIT on June 9 that it is expanding CAPE functionality so entries flagged for reconciliation can receive IEEPA tariff refunds. The reconciliation processing capability is expected to be available on June 29.

The overall CAPE throughput numbers, as of early June, are substantial but incomplete. Since the launch of CAPE Phase 1, more than 16 million entries have been processed. More than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA duty refunds have been accepted for processing. Approximately $22 billion in refunds have been completed and sent to the Treasury Department for disbursement. That figure must be read against Penn Wharton's projection of up to $175 billion in total refunds — with future tariff revenue collections falling by half unless replaced by another source. The gap between $89 billion accepted and $22 billion disbursed signals that Treasury transmission, ACH payment processing, and compliance review are each adding friction to the pipeline.

Following a closed conference on May 27, Judge Eaton issued two orders: the first addressed the progress of duty refund claims, including concerns about how CBP plans to address entries for which liquidation is final; the second requires CBP to "show cause" why he should not lift his suspension of the CIT's previous order requiring CBP to liquidate or reliquidate all entries on which IEEPA tariffs were paid without regard to liquidation status. The "finally liquidated entries" question is potentially the largest unresolved CAPE issue: CBP's own CSMS guidance initially excluded such entries from Phase 1, and the Department of Justice has asked the court not to lift the suspension of immediate compliance.

Of the roughly $166 billion in duties CBP has collected under IEEPA, approximately $85 billion in both potential and certified refunds have been accepted for processing in CAPE. The differential — approximately $81 billion in collected IEEPA duties not yet captured in the CAPE pipeline — represents the Phase 2+ universe: reconciliation entries, drawback claims, and finally-liquidated entries. Additional phases planned later in 2026 are intended to address more complex fact patterns, including reconciliation entries, drawback claims, and historically liquidated entries. CBP's June 29 commitment for reconciliation entry processing is therefore the single most important near-term milestone for importers whose refund exposure sits in that bucket.

### IV. Procedural Architecture — How the Two Tracks Interact

The IEEPA and Section 122 litigation streams share a procedural kinship that shapes recovery strategy. In both cases, the government has pressed for stays to delay adverse CIT orders while collecting duties; in both cases, the Federal Circuit has been the critical chokepoint. The difference is in the scope of the underlying relief: in March 2026, following the Supreme Court's ruling on the IEEPA tariffs, the CIT issued an order directing nationwide refunds of unlawful IEEPA tariffs, including through liquidation of unliquidated entries and reliquidation of certain liquidated entries — even for importers that were not parties to the litigation. No comparable nationwide refund order exists yet for Section 122. The fact that the CIT did not grant a universal injunction as part of its Section 122 opinion may indicate that — even if the CIT's decision is upheld on appeal — further litigation could be required for additional importers to obtain tariff refunds.

That asymmetry has direct implications for non-plaintiff Section 122 importers. Unlike the IEEPA track — where CBP's CAPE portal is open to all importers of record regardless of whether they filed suit — the Section 122 refund pathway currently has no analogous administrative mechanism. For non-plaintiff importers, there is no automatic refund mechanism at this stage. Any potential refunds depend on the final outcome of the appeal and whether relief is extended beyond the named plaintiffs. Absent a future universal injunction or congressional action, Section 122 refund claims will likely require individual protest filings under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, a process with strict 180-day deadlines that begin running on the date of liquidation.

The road to recovering illegally imposed and collected tariffs is clearer after a decision by CIT Judge Richard Eaton on March 6, 2026 — but that clarity applies to IEEPA refunds only. Section 122 claimants remain in a structurally distinct position and must not conflate the two recovery tracks.

## Recommended Actions

Importers, GCs, and recovery counsel should immediately triage their entry portfolios across both tracks. On the **IEEPA side**: file CAPE declarations promptly if not already submitted; entries flagged for reconciliation should be staged for the expected June 29 capability deployment. Monitor Judge Eaton's show-cause proceedings closely — if the court lifts its compliance suspension, CBP will face hard deadlines to reliquidate finally-liquidated entries, and importers with entries in that category should confirm their broker is positioned to respond. Once a submission is accepted, CBP anticipates refunds to be issued electronically within approximately 60–90 days, inclusive of statutory interest, subject to standard compliance review — meaning June-accepted declarations should generate payments by early September absent a compliance hold. On the **Section 122 side**: the Federal Circuit's June 11 stay forecloses operational relief for non-plaintiffs before July 24, 2026. Importers who have been paying the 10% ad valorem surcharge under HTSUS 9903.03.01 since February 24, 2026 should begin preserving refund rights now through § 1514 protests on liquidated entries (180-day deadline runs from liquidation date, not proclamation date). Counsel should also calendar the Federal Circuit's merits briefing schedule on the Section 122 appeal — an expedited timeline analogous to the IEEPA appeal could produce a ruling before year-end, at which point a universal-refund order on Section 122 duties becomes structurally viable. Do not assume the July 24 sunset moots the refund claim; the IEEPA precedent confirms that obligations paid under an invalidated tariff authority survive expiry of the underlying instrument. Finally, monitor any White House signals regarding a replacement proclamation that re-invokes Section 122 with corrected balance-of-payments language — such a new proclamation would need to be grounded in the specific balance-of-payments metrics the majority identified as permissible and its retroactive effect on prior entries remains legally unresolved.
