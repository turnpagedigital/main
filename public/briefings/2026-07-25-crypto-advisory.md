### I. DELTA — Poolin Technology, Ch. 11 (D.N.J. No. 26-18325): A Third-Generation Wallet-Freeze with Single-Digit Recovery Math


Poolin Technology filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on July 22, 2026 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of New Jersey under Case No. 26-18325, setting up an orderly wind-down and asset sale process tied to its West Texas mining operations.
 The judge has not yet been reported in available filings.


The debtors are Poolin Technology PTE. Ltd. and its U.S. affiliates, Lonestar Dream Inc. and Lonestar Taproot LLC. The filing details a $52 million stalking-horse bid from Thor CALAP LLC for the company's Pyote and Tarbush mining sites in West Texas.


The liability side of the ledger is structurally familiar and deeply unfavorable. 
Poolin's prepetition liabilities stand at $173.1 million, including $163.7 million in unsecured IOUs owed to roughly 11,700 Poolin Wallet users after withdrawals were frozen in 2022.
 
Court documents indicate total liabilities in the range of $100 million to $500 million, with assets valued between $1 million and $10 million, while Chief Restructuring Officer Michael DuFrayne's declaration specifies total prepetition obligations of approximately $173.1 million.


The gross recovery arithmetic is stark. 
The $52 million in signed offers covers Texas mining assets, not wallet cash, and equals just 31.8% of the IOUs before costs.
 
Moreover, the offers cover assets held by Lonestar affiliates rather than cash in Poolin Technology's wallet business
 — meaning the intercompany boundary between the Singapore parent and the Texas subsidiaries will be a central valuation and distribution question under the plan. 
With only $1.2 million in cash on hand, the $52 million stalking-horse covers only approximately 30% of debts before fees, implying real recovery will be materially lower.



The filing is a liquidating Chapter 11, not a reorganization — the company has no intention of emerging as a going concern.
 
Poolin permanently halted all mining and hosting operations on July 10, 2026, and has stated it will use proceeds from the bankruptcy sale to satisfy creditor claims under a liquidating plan.


The causal chain traces directly to the 2022 wallet-product design. 
Beginning in the second half of 2020, the Poolin Wallet added "time deposit" and "demand deposit" products carrying stated annual rates of roughly 2% to 8.8%.
 
In mid-2022, sharp declines in bitcoin prices triggered margin calls from lenders, forcing Poolin Wallet to pledge digital assets as collateral; in September 2022, the company declared a liquidity crisis and halted withdrawals, replacing customer deposits with IOUs; a further price drop in late 2022 led to collateral liquidations by lenders.
 The IOU-for-deposit swap is the same mechanism that courts in *In re Celsius Network LLC* analyzed under § 541 — the critical unresolved question being whether Poolin's wallet TOS created a trust or bailment over deposited tokens, or simply a debtor-creditor relationship that pulls those assets into the estate.


Claim valuation is a separate problem, and it turns on what the claim is denominated in — the original bitcoin, ether or USDT, or the IOU token that replaced it. Section 502(b) requires claims to be fixed in U.S. dollars as of the petition date, here July 22, 2026, nearly four years after the tokens were issued.
 The distance between the 2022 freeze date and the 2026 petition date is consequential: bitcoin's price trajectory over that period means that petition-date dollar valuation likely exceeds the dollar value at the time of freeze — a dynamic that compresses distributable value if the estate is measured in fiat while creditors hold expectations calibrated to peak token prices.

On the asset side, upside optionality exists but is modest. 
The debtors' prepetition marketing outreach focused on AI and high-performance computing operators, hyperscalers, REITs, private equity groups, and other cryptocurrency miners, generating 28 nondisclosure agreements and seven letters of intent covering both individual sites and the full portfolio, with company representatives highlighting that rising interest in AI infrastructure could elevate the worth of the sites' power capacity and electrical systems.
 
The Texas entities reported combined losses of approximately $45.9 million since inception.


Creditors holding wallet IOUs should track: (1) whether the debtors seek to consolidate the Singapore parent and Texas subsidiary estates; (2) the auction process timeline and whether AI/HPC interest lifts the floor bid above $52 million; (3) the intercompany claim structure between Poolin Technology and Lonestar Dream/Taproot; and (4) whether the official committee (once formed) pursues a § 541 / trust argument on the wallet deposits.

### III. BACKGROUND — Genesis/DCG Avoidance Litigation: The $2.1 Billion Clawback in Flight

The Genesis/DCG avoidance complex remains the beat's highest-dollar active recovery effort. 
Crypto lender Genesis Global Capital launched legal action against its parent Digital Currency Group (DCG) following bankruptcy proceedings that concluded in August 2024, seeking to recover approximately $2.1 billion in disputed transfers to benefit creditors.
 
The lawsuit filed in Delaware Chancery Court contains serious allegations against DCG, its founder and CEO Barry Silbert, and other company insiders, requesting recovery of approximately one million coins worth around $2.1 billion.



The disputed crypto payments exceed $582 million, with total preferential transfers in excess of $1.2 billion as of March 31, according to the court filing; the lawsuit also mentions $101 million to the DCG subsidiary HQ Enhanced Yield Fund, $136 million to DCG International, $448 million to DCG itself, and $34 million in tax payments to DCG.


The FTX estate's separate pursuit of Genesis Digital Assets adds a second recovery vector into the same pool. 
FTX is pursuing litigation including a $1 billion lawsuit against Genesis Digital Assets as part of its broader clawback strategy, with the estate's next creditor distribution having been scheduled for March 31, 2026.
 The cross-claim exposure — Genesis as both plaintiff (against DCG) and defendant (against FTX) — creates a triangulated recovery posture where each estate's eventual payout depends in part on the other's success rate in avoidance litigation.

The statutory theory common to both: § 547 preferential transfers and § 548 fraudulent transfers under the Bankruptcy Code, with the critical lookback period questions turning on when each counterparty was insolvent. DCG's defense on the preference count — that transfers were made in the ordinary course of business or pursuant to a contemporaneous exchange — has not been tested at summary judgment as of this run.

### IV. BACKGROUND — Poolin Structural Read-Across: The § 541 Customer-Property Question Across Open Dockets

This week's Poolin filing crystallizes a pattern worth naming: three open dockets — Poolin (D.N.J. 26-18325), BlockFills/Reliz (D. Del. 26-10371), and the longer-running Celsius precedent — all share the same threshold dispute. When a crypto platform takes customer deposits, offers yield, pledges those assets as collateral, and then freezes withdrawals during a market stress event, does the resulting claim against the estate sound in contract (unsecured creditor) or in property (trust or bailment)?

The Celsius court (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., J. Glenn) held, in the core wallet product, that the terms of service transferred title to the estate — making wallet depositors unsecured creditors, not beneficial owners. That holding has not been followed uniformly across jurisdictions. The HKCFI's analysis in the *Gatecoin* liquidation (HKCFI 914) applied a different framework under Hong Kong law, treating certain deposit arrangements as trust structures. Neither Poolin's D.N.J. filing nor BlockFills' D. Del. case has produced a judicial ruling on the § 541 question as of this run. In Poolin, the question will arise when creditors argue that IOU-denominated claims should be treated as trust beneficiary interests rather than general unsecured claims. In BlockFills, the dual-track structure — NewCo option plus § 363 sale — has so far sidestepped a direct ruling, but the confirmation release dispute and the two prefiling customer-fraud actions (Dominion Capital, 1:26-cv-01672 S.D.N.Y.; Alberta Ltd., 26-cv-02451 N.D. Ill.) push the question toward the confirmation docket.

Recovery counsel tracking either matter should file requests for special notice and monitor the committee's position on estate-vs.-trust treatment — that determination, more than the sale price or plan haircut, controls the distributable universe.

## Recommended Actions

Creditors and recovery counsel holding wallet-freeze claims across the Poolin and BlockFills estates should take the following steps immediately: in the Poolin case (D.N.J. 26-18325), file a request for special notice under Bankruptcy Rule 2002 and monitor court filings for (i) the U.S. Trustee's appointment of an official committee, (ii) any motion to consolidate the Singapore parent and Texas subsidiary estates, (iii) the debtors' § 363 bidding procedures motion (which will set the auction timeline and overbid increment above Thor CALAP's $52 million floor), and (iv) the plan's treatment of wallet IOUs as unsecured debt versus trust-beneficiary interests under § 541 — the latter argument is worth asserting in a committee or ad hoc capacity given the Celsius precedent's limited geographic reach; in BlockFills (D. Del. 26-10371), the September 11 confirmation hearing is the next hard deadline — creditors holding disputed, contingent, or unliquidated claims should have resolved temporary allowance for voting purposes per the May 28 DS order [Dkt. 388], and any party intending to object to the opt-out release mechanism (flagged by Judge Horan at the DS hearing) should prepare its objection now, calibrating it to the customer-fraud allegations in Dominion Capital (S.D.N.Y. 1:26-cv-01672) and Alberta Ltd. (N.D. Ill. 26-cv-02451); on the Genesis/DCG front, claimants who received distributions in the concluded Genesis case should monitor the avoidance litigation's progress, as any material recovery against DCG could fund supplemental distributions, and the interplay with FTX's $1 billion Genesis Digital Assets clawback action may compress or delay those distributions depending on litigation sequencing.
