### I. Bartz v. Anthropic — Final Approval Still Pending; Late Opt-Out Dispute Now the Controlling Variable


The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place on May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín conducted the hearing and, by signed order the same day, directed Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than 2 pages by May 21, 2026 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, while also directing plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections — with a redline to the April 30, 2026 proposed order.



As of the June 2026 reporting period, Judge Martínez-Olguín had declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement. In a supplemental filing ordered after the fairness hearing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any supporting record. Final approval remains pending.
 The CourtListener docket (ID 69058235) reflects a last-filing date of July 1, 2026, with no new activity in the 72 hours preceding this advisory — meaning the final approval order had not been entered as of the last confirmed docket refresh.

The claims posture is remarkable. 
Class counsel filed papers showing that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% participation rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the Motion for Final Approval filed March 19, 2026, eleven days before the claims deadline of March 30, 2026.
 
The high claim rate means earlier forecasts of a larger per-work payout will not materialize; the actual disbursement will likely remain near the original $3,000-per-work figure.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed at the May 14 hearing that the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100.


The fee structure demands attention. 
Authors urged Judge Martínez-Olguín to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million.
 
Separately, Anthropic argued that $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel is far too high, citing "scant justification."
 The fee contest is live and unresolved — a meaningful appellate risk vector if the court awards at the high end and any objector pursues a Ninth Circuit appeal.

The payment waterfall, once approval is granted, runs on a multi-installment schedule. 
Anthropic will fund the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
The settlement administrator is scheduled to issue a second wave of checks on October 9, 2026, and Anthropic's Schedule Payment 1 of $450 million is due September 25, 2026.



If approved, the settlement would rank among the largest copyright class action settlements on record and could become an early benchmark for how courts value mass claims against AI companies over the use of copyrighted books.
 The underlying liability was clean: 
Judge Alsup held that Anthropic's use of lawfully acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and thus protected by fair use, but simultaneously ruled that Anthropic's creation and retention of a "central library" comprised in part of pirated works was not transformative and constituted infringement.
 The settlement resolves only the piracy-side liability; the fair-use-for-training holding survives and governs the broader AI copyright landscape.

### II. Visa/Mastercard Interchange — MDL 1720, Second Distribution Approved for September 2026

The $5.54 billion Damages Class settlement in *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation* (MDL 1720, E.D.N.Y.) has moved past first-wave distribution and into second-round proceedings. 
On May 26, 2026, class counsel filed a motion requesting court approval of a second initial distribution of settlement funds; on June 15, 2026, the court approved the motion, finding the proposed distribution to be in the best interests of eligible claimants while protecting those whose claims are not yet ready. The second initial distribution is anticipated to occur in September 2026.


Simultaneously, a new chapter opened in the same court. 
On April 21, 2026, a putative class action was filed in the Southern District of New York against Visa and Mastercard by merchants asserting violations of federal antitrust law consistent with MDL 1720 allegations, brought on behalf of merchants that have accepted Visa and/or Mastercard credit cards since January 25, 2019 — the day the Damages Class settlement's release period ends.
 
In April 2026, the named plaintiffs concurrently filed a motion for summary judgment seeking a declaration that the prospective release in the Damages Class Settlement Agreement — which runs through August 2028 — does not bar their damages claims. Briefing on this motion has not yet been scheduled.
 The release-scope motion is the key structural issue: if the release bars post-January 2019 claims until August 2028, the new class action is effectively stayed; if not, the next litigation cycle opens immediately. Clayton Act §4 treble-damage exposure on a decade of interchange volume is substantial.


On June 9, 2026, U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan granted preliminary approval to the revised Visa and Mastercard injunctive relief settlement — a meaningful update because prior versions of the settlement have been rejected.
 
The agreement "provides more extensive relief" than the prior settlement the court rejected in June 2024, Cogan found, with the pact covering approximately 12 million merchants party to the class action.
 
Under the proposed injunctive settlement, Visa and Mastercard agreed to lower swipe fees by 0.1 percentage point for five years, with standard consumer card fees capped at 1.25% for eight years.
 
NACS has indicated it will appeal to the Second Circuit if Cogan grants final approval.
 A final approval fight and likely Second Circuit appeal make this a 12-to-18-month process before injunctive relief becomes operative.

### III. BCBS Subscriber Antitrust — MDL 2406, N.D. Ala. — Distribution Underway

The *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation* (MDL 2406, Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP, N.D. Ala.) crossed into active distribution in May 2026 after years of post-final-approval appellate delay. 
The settlement administrator began issuing payments to approved claimants on May 11, 2026. A motion for distribution of settlement funds was filed on April 23, 2026 and approved by the court on April 24, 2026.
 
Approximately $1.9 billion will be distributed to roughly six million claimants, with individual payouts varying based on coverage history and premiums paid.


The broader BCBS litigation ecosystem is still active. 
In October 2024, the BCBS Association and its affiliates separately agreed to pay $2.8 billion to settle antitrust claims from hospitals, physician practices, and other providers — the largest settlement of its kind in the healthcare industry — with a federal judge approving the settlement in August 2025. Nearly 6,500 providers opted out, however, including facilities owned by Mayo Clinic, Providence, University of Michigan Health, and AdventHealth, and dozens of health systems subsequently filed new antitrust lawsuits alleging anticompetitive practices are ongoing.
 
In February 2026, physician staffing groups filed a new federal antitrust lawsuit in California against the BCBS Association and its affiliates, alleging market allocation, reimbursement suppression, and price fixing.
 The opt-out litigation wave from the provider-side settlement is the live risk and recovery opportunity for health system claimants who preserved their claims.

### IV. Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Claims Filing Underway, $218B Claimed Exposure


Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist the firm has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability.


The structure is FAA mass arbitration, not FRCP 23 class action. 
Due to contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, these claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation.
 
Federal antitrust law permits treble damages, meaning a 10% overcharge claim could translate into a recovery of up to 30% of the advertiser's ad spend.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.



Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. DOJ, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling is expected in 2026; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines.
 The remedies ruling in the E.D. Va. ad-tech case is the proximate catalyst for the scale of these claims: a divestiture-plus-behavioral remedies order would sharpen the damages narrative before arbitrators. 
Keller has estimated that similar mass arbitrations have lasted 12 to 24 months between the filing of claims and resolution.



While many companies that displayed ads purchased through Google — including USA Today Co. and Advance Publications Inc. — have sued for damages since the rulings in 2024, advertiser contracts with the search giant require mandatory arbitration over legal disputes.
 The FAA arbitration clause dynamic inverts the usual corporate playbook: what was designed as a liability shield becomes, at sufficient scale, a fee-cost pressure mechanism that historically forces global settlement. 
Ashley Keller has previously used this approach to drive the Amazon Alexa campaign, which prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands, and the TurboTax campaign that resolved in a confidential settlement alongside a $141 million FTC fund.

Cal. docket weekly. BCBS subscriber claimants (MDL 2406) with valid claims on file should confirm payment receipt from JND Legal Administration given the May 11, 2026 distribution launch; those who missed the November 2021 claims deadline have no recourse in the subscriber class. Merchants in the Visa/Mastercard Damages Class (MDL 1720) should confirm their claims are in the second-distribution population ahead of the September 2026 payment wave, and separately evaluate whether post-January 2019 interchange-volume positions warrant a new filing given the April 2026 putative class action and the pending release-scope summary judgment motion. U.S. businesses with material Google ad spend since August 2016 should evaluate Keller Postman's advertiser mass arbitration intake (adsrefund.com) against their applicable state statutes of limitations before Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling narrows the damages-certainty window; the per-business economics are favorable when spend exceeds $300K given the 30%-of-spend treble-damages ceiling, but engagement ahead of the 12-to-24-month resolution timeline matters for SOL compliance.
