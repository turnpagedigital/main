## Analysis & Developments

**The central question this week: when does Bartz close, and what does Concord I's summary judgment ruling mean for every other AI-training case in the system?**

The $1.5 billion *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* settlement, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.), remains the dominant event in the AI-copyright docket — but it is not yet closed. 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined to grant final approval at the May 14 fairness hearing, demanding more detail on how the money will actually reach claimants and how much will go to plaintiffs' attorneys; her pause left the settlement in legal limbo just weeks before payouts were expected to begin.
 That order post-dates the May 14 hearing and is the last public action of record. As of today — July 1, 2026 — no final approval order has been publicly docketed. 
The CourtListener docket (ID 69058235) was last updated June 25, 2026, with no new entries flagged in the prior 72 hours.
 The settlement remains under submission.

The structural posture has been clear since the hearing. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín's post-hearing order required Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than 2 pages on late opt-outs by May 21, 2026, and required plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026; the court stated it would not consider any further submissions from objectors.
 Those supplemental filings are now on the record. The open question is whether the judge will issue a final approval order as written, require material modifications to the fee structure or distribution mechanism, or hold another conference. Each path has distinct implications for the Bartz template's value to other pending AI-training defendants.

### Bartz — Settlement Economics and the Fee Dispute

The headline number is $1.5 billion, but the distribution economics are more granular. 
Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate — vastly surpassing the 54 percent rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval.
 
At the May 14 hearing, lead class counsel Justin Nelson reported the payout per work will be approximately $3,100, with a 92.77 percent participation rate for works in the works list.
 That participation rate is far outside any class action norm: 
the high claim rate effectively eliminated earlier forecasts of a larger per-work payout, pulling the figure back toward the original $3,000-per-work estimate.



Class counsel are seeking attorneys' fees equal to 25 percent of the fund — $375 million — with $50,000 service awards per named plaintiff ($150,000 total for the three class representatives), plus administration fees and Special Master fees for resolving disputes between class members.
 
According to the Authors Alliance summary of the May 14 hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín's questions focused on attorneys' fees and the structure of the cost reserve, rather than the merits of the settlement itself.
 That framing suggests the substantive deal is not in jeopardy — the judge's skepticism is directed at the fee and administrative architecture, not liability resolution.


Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The April 30 installment has already been paid. The September 25, 2026 installment becomes due before this case is likely to be fully resolved if any objector pursues a Ninth Circuit appeal after final approval issues. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.


One objector-posture note worth flagging for rights-holder counsel: 
Professor Lea Victoria Bishop raised concerns about transparency — specifically that certain concerns raised by the original judge were allegedly not communicated to his replacement — prompting Judge Martínez-Olguín to order that her objection and those of other objectors be unsealed before the court decides on final approval.
 That unsealing creates a public record that future objectors in analogous AI settlements can cite as procedural leverage.

For context on why this settlement carries systemic weight: 
the Bartz settlement has been treated by the industry as a template — a number other AI labs and rights holders are quietly using as a benchmark in their own negotiations — and if Martínez-Olguín requires material changes, the leverage in every pending AI training case shifts toward plaintiffs.

### Concord I — Cross-MSJ Fully Briefed; Fair-Use Ruling Imminent

*Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee, Mag. Judge Susan G. Van Keulen), is the most consequential fair-use proceeding currently live in the AI-copyright system. 
The CourtListener docket was last updated June 22, 2026, with Judge Eumi K. Lee assigned and Magistrate Judge Van Keulen handling discovery.



Concord I completed fact and expert discovery and is now at summary judgment, with the threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — positioned to determine the viability of most or all of the publishers' claims.
 
Per Anthropic's March 2026 motion to stay Concord II, dispositive motions in Concord I were to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026
 — meaning the cross-MSJ record is now closed and Judge Lee can rule at any time.


The publishers moved for partial summary judgment that fair use does not excuse Anthropic's conduct, arguing the company used their lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 The § 107 four-factor framing here is aggressive: the publishers contend that the fourth factor — market harm under *Harper & Row* — is dispositive because Anthropic used commercially licensed lyrics to build a direct market competitor, not a transformative work. Anthropic's counter-position tracks the *Google v. Oracle* transformativeness framework: 
Anthropic claims fair use when used to train the AI model, citing judgments where courts have held that making "intermediate" copies of copyrighted materials to develop new technologies does not violate copyright law.


The expert-declaration episode adds procedural texture. 
A California federal magistrate judge partially stricken an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged she used Claude.ai to help draft the expert's declaration that included the erroneous citation, characterizing it as "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 That episode, resolved at the magistrate level, nonetheless sits on the MSJ record and gives plaintiffs' counsel a credibility data point if the court weighs competing expert testimony.

### Concord II — $3B Follow-On Suit; Stay Motion Outcome Pending


Major music publishers already suing Anthropic filed a second, $3 billion suit against the company, asserting newly discovered mass infringement of sheet music and songbooks.
 
Anthropic moved to stay *Concord II* (No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK) pending final resolution of Concord I, or in the alternative, pending resolution of the Concord I summary judgment motions and any subsequent appeal.
 
The stay hearing was set for May 20, 2026, at 10:00 a.m. in Courtroom 7, San Jose Division.
 No order on that stay motion is publicly available in the search record as of this run.


Anthropic's stay argument is that if it prevails on summary judgment in Concord I, the training- and CMI-based claims in Concord II would be resolved or substantially narrowed, and allowing parallel proceedings would require duplicative discovery for over 20,000 newly identified works-in-suit, including ownership, chain of title, and deposit copies.
 Judge Lee's ruling on the stay — whenever it issues — is itself a leading indicator: a grant signals the court views Concord I's MSJ as a genuine threshold event; a denial signals the court thinks the claims are sufficiently distinct to proceed simultaneously.

### In Re OpenAI Copyright MDL, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein)


The CourtListener docket for the OpenAI MDL (No. 1:25-md-03143, S.D.N.Y.) was last updated June 26, 2026, with Judge Sidney H. Stein presiding and Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang handling referred matters.
 The MDL consolidates at least sixteen copyright lawsuits across news organizations, authors, and publishers. The two major discovery rulings from earlier this year remain the controlling data points for this period.


On January 5, 2026, Judge Stein affirmed a magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20 million-log anonymized ChatGPT sample — not the cherry-picked conversations OpenAI had proposed — marking a significant discovery victory for news organizations and authors suing OpenAI.
 That production is in progress; its evidentiary implications for the § 504 statutory damages calculus and the § 107(4) market-harm analysis are substantial. Every log that shows verbatim regurgitation of a registered work strengthens the output-based infringement theory that Judge Stein declined to dismiss in October 2025.


On October 27, 2025, Judge Stein rejected OpenAI's motion to dismiss output-based infringement claims, finding that ChatGPT-generated summaries of George R.R. Martin's novels were "substantially similar" to the originals because they "parrot[] the plot, characters, and themes"; fair use remains unresolved.
 
OpenAI's motion to dismiss was granted as to claims 4 and 5, granted in part and denied in part as to claim 8, and denied as to claims 3, 6, and 7; OpenAI's motion to stay was granted as to newer models — o1, o1-mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 — not already within the MDL's substantive scope.
 That stay of the newer-model scope is significant: it insulates OpenAI's most commercially relevant current products from discovery while the core GPT-4 era fair-use questions are litigated first.

The May 2025 preservation order issued by Magistrate Judge Wang — requiring OpenAI to retain all ChatGPT conversation logs affecting over 400 million users worldwide — remains operative. 
That order, affirmed by the presiding judge, represents the first time a court has mandated mass preservation of AI-generated content on such a scale, forcing immediate reconsideration of privacy commitments, international compliance obligations, and the fundamental relationship between AI systems and user data.

### System-Level Fair-Use Watch: What Concord I's Ruling Will Anchor

The three matters above — Bartz final approval, Concord I MSJ, and OpenAI MDL discovery — are not independent proceedings. They form an interdependent framework around a single unresolved legal question: whether AI training on commercially licensed or pirated copyrighted text constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107, and in particular whether the § 107(4) market-harm inquiry is satisfied when the AI product competes in the same market as the licensed original.

*Bartz* resolved by piracy, not training, and Anthropic admitted no liability on the training claim. 
The scope of the settlement has been widely misunderstood: Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training, and if Judge Martínez-Olguín grants approval it will resolve one of the last major procedural hurdles for the settlement — but it will not bind any other AI developer or future case.
 The training fair-use question migrates to Concord I (lyrics, J. Lee), the OpenAI MDL (books and journalism, J. Stein), and eventually to whichever circuit court takes the first appeal on the merits.

*Concord I* is structurally the closest to a first-mover ruling on training fair use. Judge Lee denied the preliminary injunction on training in March 2025 on overbreadth and irreparable harm grounds — she did not reach the merits of the § 107 factors. 
On March 25, 2025, Judge Lee denied the publishers' application for an interim injunction restraining Anthropic's use of their lyrics in AI training — a decision described as an early victory for AI developers, though the merits remained open.
 The cross-MSJ briefing now puts the merits directly before her. A ruling that Anthropic's use is not fair use — even on a partial record — would be the most consequential copyright ruling in the generative AI era to date, more durable than the Bartz settlement because it would be a merits adjudication, not a negotiated release.
