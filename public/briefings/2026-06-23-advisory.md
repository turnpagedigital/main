## Analysis & Developments

### BARTZ v. ANTHROPIC — Final Approval in Limbo, Distribution Clock Running


After a 75-minute fairness hearing on May 14, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined to approve the $1.5 billion settlement from the bench
 in *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal., Courtroom 12 – 19th Floor, 450 Golden Gate Ave., San Francisco). 
Her post-hearing order — signed May 14, 2026 — directed Anthropic to file a supplemental brief by May 21 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and directed plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order with a redline to the April 30 proposed order; the court stated it would not consider any further submissions from objectors.
 As of today, June 23, no public docket entry reflecting the final approval order itself has been confirmed through available sources. The matter remains under submission.

The practical consequence: 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved, and payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest; Anthropic is paying the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.
 Per the settlement agreement, 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The June 11 distribution-calculation milestone listed in the settlement's plan of allocation — keyed to "70 days after the Claims Deadline" under ¶ 3(c)(ii)(2) — is now past, but it was expressly conditioned on the absence of an appeal and on final approval having been issued. 
After the fairness hearing, the settlement administrator was to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement expected to begin in June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed.
 The continued submission posture means that timeline is now effectively suspended pending the final approval order and the expiration of any appeal window.

What is confirmed: the claims posture is remarkable. 
Class counsel filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate
, against a typical class action claim rate of roughly 10 percent. 
Class counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work will be approximately $3,100.
 
Plaintiffs' lawyers are seeking 25% of the fund — $375 million — as attorneys' fees; service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for three class representatives) are also sought.


The structural point that rights-holder counsel should keep front of mind: 
*Bartz* is not a settlement about AI training. Judge Alsup ruled in June 2025 that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and concluded that Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired. Nothing about this settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
What the court left unresolved — and what the settlement addresses — was Anthropic's potential liability for downloading and storage of files from shadow libraries LibGen and PiLiMi.
 The settlement therefore resolves piracy-predicate infringement under 17 U.S.C. § 504, not training-phase fair use under § 107. That bifurcation is the jurisprudential frame for every downstream case.

On the **objector risk**, seven objectors each presented at the May 14 hearing, with attorney James Bartolomei appearing for several. 
The hearing was one of the last major procedural steps before the court decides whether to grant final approval; Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission and indicated that short orders may follow.
 The absence of a final approval order roughly five weeks after the hearing is notable but not disqualifying — the court may be working through objections to the proposed order with care. Any objector who preserves standing could appeal to the Ninth Circuit, which would further delay the distribution timeline.

### CONCORD / UMG v. ANTHROPIC — Two Active Dockets, Piracy Theory Migrates to Music

The Concord music-publisher litigation against Anthropic is now best understood as two parallel actions before Judge Eumi K. Lee in the Northern District of California, with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen referred on both.

**Concord I** — *Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.) — is the original October 2023 action by Concord, Universal Music Corp., ABKCO Music, and affiliated publishers asserting direct and secondary infringement and CMI removal arising from Claude's lyric outputs. 
The publisher plaintiffs allege Anthropic infringes their exclusive rights to musical lyrics by using their works to train Claude and by distributing lyrics in Claude's outputs when prompted.
 The docket was last updated June 10, 2026. Cross-motions for summary judgment on fair use (training and output) remain pending; the February 2026 scheduling adjustment pushed the next fair-use ruling into the fall 2026 window.


The court previously found in Anthropic's favor on two separate motions: it denied the motion for a preliminary injunction as overbroad and failing to establish irreparable harm, and it granted Anthropic's motion to dismiss the contributory and vicarious infringement claims.
 
Prior to those rulings, the parties entered a joint stipulation by which Anthropic agreed to maintain already-implemented guardrails as to its current AI models and products; the court noted the stipulation resolved plaintiffs' motion for injunctive relief to the extent it concerned Claude's output.
 What remains alive in Concord I is the direct copyright infringement claim on training-phase copying — the core § 107 four-factor fight.

**Concord II** — *Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal., filed Jan. 28, 2026) — is a second, separate action filed by Concord and Capitol CMG asserting that Anthropic torrented pirated copies of music-publisher works from LibGen and PiLiMi. 
The complaint alleges Anthropic concealed during discovery in Concord I that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books — including hundreds or more that plainly contain publishers' musical compositions — from pirate library websites, and that publishers did not learn of the torrenting until July 2025, when Judge Alsup's ruling in *Bartz* publicly revealed it.
 
Publishers therefore bring the second action to address what they characterize as egregious and willful infringement by downloading and uploading unauthorized copies from illegal websites.



Anthropic filed a motion to stay Concord II, with the motion hearing set for May 20, 2026, before Judge Lee in San Jose, Courtroom 7, 4th Floor.
 That hearing date has now passed; no public order on the stay motion has been confirmed through available sources. If the stay was denied, Concord II moves to answer and discovery; if granted, it waits on Concord I's summary judgment outcome. This matters: a stay pending Concord I's fair-use ruling would create a sequencing in which the training/output question is resolved before the piracy theory is tried — which structurally mirrors how *Bartz* developed.

The Concord II complaint covers 20,517 additional works per Exhibit B and names Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann individually alongside Anthropic PBC. A separate UMG-led coalition complaint making comparable piracy-theory allegations was filed January 28, 2026 as well. 
Music publishers' subsequent $3.1 billion lawsuit against Anthropic using the same piracy theory suggests plaintiff attorneys view *Bartz* as a replicable playbook.

### THOMSON REUTERS v. ROSS — Third Circuit Argument: The First Appellate Test of AI Fair Use


The first appellate ruling on fair use in an AI training case is in the Third Circuit, which heard oral argument on June 11, 2026 in *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence*. The case involves non-generative AI — Ross's system used Westlaw headnotes to train a legal search tool — but the fair-use analysis it will produce is expected to influence every generative AI training case in the pipeline.



On February 11, 2025, the district court granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and that Ross Intelligence's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use.
 
On May 23, 2025, the court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting the questions were "hard"; in its appeal brief filed in September 2025, Ross argued the decision would have "sweeping consequences for innovation" in both legal research and AI.


The § 107 analysis at the district level tracked all four factors. 
Factors two (nature of the work) and three (amount used) weighed in Ross's favor, given the low degree of creativity in the headnotes and the fact that they did not appear in Ross's end outputs.
 
Factor four — "undoubtedly the single most important element of fair use" — favored Thomson Reuters because of the potential impact on Thomson Reuters' ability to sell its own data for use in AI training if Ross's use was permissible.
 The Third Circuit's treatment of factor four will be the critical data point: if the appellate court affirms that an emerging licensing market for training data triggers adverse § 107(4) analysis, that ruling transfers directly to generative AI defendants who face music, news, and book-licensing market-harm arguments in every pending case. 
The ruling is expected to influence every generative AI copyright case in the pipeline, even though Ross involved non-generative AI.
 A decision is possible within three to six months of the June 11 argument date.

### SYSTEM-WIDE: Doctrinal Architecture and Market-Harm Pricing

Three reference points for structuring any recovery or licensing negotiation posture across the AI copyright system:

**1. The Alsup bifurcation governs.** 
Judge Alsup's June 2025 decision in *Bartz* established that AI training on copyrighted works can constitute fair use — but that storing pirated copies of those works does not. This bifurcation is now the framework that other U.S. AI copyright cases are navigating.
 Cases premised solely on training-phase copying against defendants who sourced content legally face a structurally weaker § 107 argument after *Bartz*. Cases premised on shadow-library torrenting — including Concord II and the music-publisher coalition complaint — have a cleaner path.

**2. The Bartz per-work benchmark.** 
The $1.5 billion *Bartz* settlement establishes the first concrete per-work pricing benchmark — approximately $3,113 per book — for AI training on pirated content. Norton Rose Fulbright's March 2026 analysis notes that statutory damages under § 504 can reach $150,000 per work, meaning the settlement resolved at approximately 2.1% of theoretical maximum exposure. This ratio gives plaintiff attorneys in other AI copyright cases a credible floor for settlement negotiations.


**3. AI authorship is foreclosed at the Supreme Court level.** 
On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable.
 
The denial does not necessarily prohibit copyright protection for works created with AI assistance; human contributions with AI assistive use may be protectable. But because Thaler had explicitly and repeatedly disclaimed any human creative input, the court did not address how much human involvement is necessary to support copyright registration for AI-assisted works — that issue remains unresolved.


**4. Parallel matters (no new verified filings in 72h).** *In re OpenAI Copyright MDL*, No. 3:24-md-03143 (N.D. Cal., Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín) — 
the OpenAI MDL in the Southern District of New York alone consolidates 16 separate suits
 — continues pre-trial coordination. *Disney, et al. v. Midjourney* (N.D. Cal.): 
the parties recently filed their joint Rule 26(f) report; Disney requested a non-expert discovery cutoff for August 17, 2026 with all motions filed by December 21, 2026, but Midjourney asked for dates approximately three months later.
 *Getty Images v. Stability AI* (UK): 
Stability AI prevailed in the UK High Court in November 2025 after Getty abandoned its primary copyright infringement claims including training-phase copying; the court also rejected the remaining secondary infringement claims; Getty received narrow trademark infringement findings on early versions of Stable Diffusion; permission to appeal has been granted.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims-side investors should treat June 23 as a waiting point, not a settled one. The *Bartz* final approval order has not been confirmed as entered; if objectors preserved adequate standing and appeal the approval once signed, the distribution timeline shifts from "late fall 2026" to something materially longer, and Anthropic's installment schedule — $450 million due September 25, 2026 — may not reach claimants for well over a year. Monitor the CourtListener docket (ID 69058235) for entry of the final approval order and the 30-day appeal window that follows; any Ninth Circuit notice of appeal filed by a *Bartz* objector is the single most consequential near-term event for recovery timing. On Concord II (5:26-cv-00880), the May 20 stay-motion ruling is overdue for public confirmation — if the stay was denied, discovery posture and the Anthropic/Amodei/Mann individual liability exposure become active variables. For the Third Circuit's *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* decision (argument June 11), flag the § 107(4) market-harm holding as the first appellate signal on AI training economics: an affirmance favoring Thomson Reuters substantially strengthens the § 107(4) arm of every pending music, news, and book training case. Rights-holders with unregistered works should confirm timely registration status under 17 U.S.C. § 411 before any new piracy-theory complaint is filed; the *Bartz* class required registration with the Copyright Office within five years of publication and before or within three months of the piracy event.
