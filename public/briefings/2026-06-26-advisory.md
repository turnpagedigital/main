**What is confirmed from the May 14 hearing record:** 
After a 75-minute hearing in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement, but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson reported a per-work payout of $3,100, with a 92.77% participation rate for works on the class list.
 
The judge heard from seven objectors, each given two minutes, and questioned lead class counsel Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman on service awards, the cost reserve, attorneys' fees, and the fee multiplier.


**The fee dispute is the residual friction point.** 
Authors urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Separately, Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees that non-class counsel requested is "far too high," asserting there is "scant justification" for the amount.
 How the court resolves those competing figures will determine the net distribution per work. At the $3,100 gross figure reported by class counsel, and assuming fees and administration consume a portion of the $1.5 billion fund, the arithmetic is sensitive to where the court lands on the fee award.

**Post-hearing order mechanics:** 
By no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic was permitted to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; Judge Martínez-Olguín held the final fairness hearing, which was also live-streamed.
 
Plaintiffs were also required by May 21 to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026. The court stated it would not require anything more from the objectors, nor consider further submissions from them.

### Settlement Administration: Distribution Timeline and Payment Structure


Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The plan of allocation, if the final approval order was entered around the expected window, maps the next milestones as follows: 
the settlement administrator is scheduled to calculate per-work and per-claimant distributions as of June 11, 2026 — with June 2026 described as the earliest possible payment date if the settlement is not appealed.
 
Summer 2026 is the earliest payment window if there are objectors but no appeals; Anthropic's third scheduled installment of $450 million falls due September 25, 2026, followed by a second wave of checks around October 9, 2026, and a fourth installment of $450 million on September 25, 2027.


The appeal risk is the variable that matters most for claims investors and rights-holders tracking liquidity. 
The California federal judge already gave a terse response to a Canadian publisher's counsel who contacted Anthropic seeking a better deal, stating the publisher could opt out but could not "seek a sweeter deal than other class members."
 That signal — plus the near-elimination of further objector submissions — suggests the court is not inclined to hold proceedings open. Still, any 9th Circuit objector appeal would toll the distribution timeline and push first payments past the September installment window.


For Anthropic, the settlement releases liability for past conduct in exchange for the $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies. It does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme and does not bind any other AI developer or future case.
 That structural feature — no license granted, no forward-looking injunction on Claude training — is the principal reason the settlement's collateral value for claimants ends at the per-work payment.

### Opt-Out Track: *Cruz v. Anthropic* and *Kwon v. Anthropic* — The Parallel Dockets

The opt-out litigation deserves separate treatment. 
Twenty-eight authors and rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. (3:26-cv-04482) demanding a jury trial on statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504, plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works.
 
A follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.
 These are not class actions; they are individual-plaintiff statutory damages cases. Statutory damages under § 504(c)(2) for willful infringement run up to $150,000 per registered work. With 28 plaintiffs in *Cruz* and 34 in *Kwon*, the aggregate exposure — before any fair-use defense is tested — is substantial. The shadow-library piracy framing that drove the Bartz settlement is directly applicable here: 
in June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of legally acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and protected as fair use, but simultaneously held that downloading and keeping pirated copies was not fair use.
 The opt-out plaintiffs are litigating precisely the piracy-liability half of that ruling, without a class settlement ceiling to cap exposure.

### *Concord Music Group et al. v. Anthropic PBC* — The "Concord II" Complaint and Escalation to Named Individuals

The music-publisher track against Anthropic has materially escalated since last reported. 
A January 28, 2026 complaint was filed as a second action by Concord, Universal, and ABKCO against Anthropic PBC and — unusually — two named individuals, Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann, explicitly positioned as "distinct and separate" from the earlier Concord I case, and centered on two categories of alleged wrongdoing: (1) mass piracy via BitTorrent from LibGen and PiLiMi involving at least 714 publisher-controlled musical works (Exhibit A), and (2) post–Concord I scraping and use of physical books and other datasets to train and operate newer Claude models, allegedly involving 20,517 publisher works (Exhibit B), plus output-side infringement and DMCA § 1202 CMI-removal theories.


The case posture on the original Concord I docket is clarified by CourtListener: 
the docket was last updated June 22, 2026, and is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, with referral to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen, in the Northern District of California at 5:24-cv-03811.
 
The original complaint was filed by Universal Music Corp., Songs of Universal, Inc., ABKCO Music, Inc., Polygram Publishing, Inc., Universal Music – MGB NA LLC, Concord Music Group, Inc., Universal Music – Z Tunes LLC, and Capitol CMG, Inc.


The Concord II complaint's theory of individual liability is unusually direct. 
The complaint alleges scale (approximately 5 million books from LibGen and 2 million from PiLiMi), leadership awareness (internal descriptions of LibGen as a "blatant violation of copyright" and "sketchy"), and intent — with Amodei allegedly acknowledging legal purchase options but choosing torrenting to avoid a "legal/practice/business slog."
 
The filing relies on evidence surfaced in the Bartz proceedings showing that Benjamin Mann personally engaged in illegal torrenting, with Dario Amodei personally discussing and authorizing the conduct.
 
As of early February 2026, the court was considering a motion to consolidate the two Concord actions.
 Whether that consolidation motion has been resolved — and whether Concord II has been formally joined with the N.D. Cal. Concord I docket under Judge Lee — is not confirmed in the public record as of this writing.

The Concord II monetization theory has downstream significance for the broader AI copyright system. 
The API pay-per-word revenue theory — tying Claude's lyric outputs to vicarious-liability "direct financial benefit" allegations — is unusually concrete and, if sustained, reshapes negotiating power far beyond this single case.
 
The requested relief includes an accounting of training data and methods, which, if granted, would function as a discovery and transparency lever with implications well beyond the Concord plaintiffs.

### *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation* — MDL 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.) — Discovery Front


In April 2025, the U.S. Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated twelve cases originating in the S.D.N.Y. and N.D. Cal. The MDL encompasses class actions by authors, suits by news organizations, DMCA-focused suits, and a case by an online video creator, all involving common questions about the defendants' use of copyrighted works without consent or compensation. The consolidated matter was assigned to the S.D.N.Y. to streamline infringement claims against OpenAI.


The most consequential development in MDL 3143 through the first quarter of 2026 is the ChatGPT log production order. 
On January 5, 2026, U.S. District Judge Sidney Stein affirmed a magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20 million-log anonymized sample — not just the cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' works — marking a significant discovery victory for the news organizations and authors suing OpenAI.
 
On relevance, the court found that even output logs without reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's fair use defense: logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal patterns relevant to whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works — directly implicating the § 107(4) market-harm factor.
 
Active discovery continues within MDL 3143. Magistrate Judge Wang ordered OpenAI on May 13, 2025 to preserve and segregate all output log data; Judge Stein affirmed the preservation order on June 26, 2025 over OpenAI's privacy objections; a September 26, 2025 modification released OpenAI from preserving newly created logs going forward but required retention of everything already saved; and on January 5, 2026, Judge Stein affirmed Wang's order compelling production of the entire 20-million-log anonymized sample, rejecting OpenAI's hand-picked subset.


The Kadrey v. Meta parallel deserves a brief note. 
In *Kadrey et al. v. Meta Platforms*, plaintiffs sued Meta alleging copyright infringement in training its Llama LLM on their books. The court granted a partial motion to dismiss on fair use grounds as to training. Claims related to alleged reproduction of pirated works during "seeding" in the torrenting process remain active in the N.D. Cal.
 The seeding/torrenting liability theory is the same thread running through Bartz, Concord II, and Kadrey — and the weight of authority is now clearly focused there rather than on the training-as-fair-use question, which Judges Alsup and Chhabria have both largely resolved in favor of defendants on legally-acquired data.

### System-Level Framework: What the Jurisprudence Has Settled and What Remains Open

The two structural conclusions that are now sufficiently established to brief against:

1. **Training on legally acquired works = fair use (N.D. Cal.):** 
In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of books to train its LLMs was fair use, and concluded Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired. Nothing in the Bartz settlement disturbs those rulings.
 Judge Vince Chhabria reached a parallel conclusion in Kadrey. Both rulings weight the § 107(1) "transformative use" factor heavily toward AI developers on legally acquired data.

2. **Piracy = separate, non-fair-use liability:** 
Courts have consistently found that the source of training data is significant. Courts may permit AI companies to train on copyrighted works if obtained lawfully, as Bartz demonstrates. But, as Bartz and Kadrey show, the retention and/or distribution of pirated copies can create separate liability independent of training.


What remains open: the S.D.N.Y.'s position on the training-data question in MDL 3143, output-side infringement liability (the Disney/Universal v. Midjourney docket targets this directly), DMCA § 1202 CMI-removal claims (now pled in Concord II and Raw Story), and the question of whether individual officers can be held personally liable for torrenting decisions — the Concord II Amodei/Mann theory is untested at the motion-to-dismiss stage.


On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming that AI-generated material must have human authorship to be copyrightable — locking in the Copyright Office's registration refusal standard.
 That ruling affects output-side claims: content generated autonomously by Claude or ChatGPT cannot itself be registered, which forecloses one category of countersuit by AI companies against downstream uses of their outputs.
