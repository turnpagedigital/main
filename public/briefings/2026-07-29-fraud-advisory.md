### 2. BLMIS / Madoff (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., No. 08-01789 (LGB)) — Seventeenth Distribution Closed; Fee Application Signal


On February 27, 2026, Irving H. Picard, SIPA Trustee for the liquidation of Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities LLC (BLMIS), announced that the seventeenth pro rata interim distribution from the Customer Fund to eligible BLMIS customers commenced. The SIPA Trustee is distributing more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims. Including that distribution, the aggregate amount sent to eligible BLMIS customers totals nearly $15.38 billion, which includes approximately $850.9 million in advances made by SIPC.


The fee-creep vector warrants attention. 
On February 13, 2026, the SIPA Trustee and his counsel at BakerHostetler filed the Fiftieth Interim Fee Application with the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York for their work on the global liquidation of BLMIS from August 1, 2025 through November 30, 2025. The Application seeks approval of fees representing approximately 63,774.40 hours of professional and paraprofessional services, billed at an average public interest discounted rate of approximately $588.88 per hour.
 At that blended rate, the four-month application period represents approximately $37.6 million in requested fees for the August–November 2025 window alone — a figure that crystallizes the administrative-cost trajectory for a recovery program now 17+ years running. Critically, 
subject to Court approval, 100 percent of the SIPA Trustee's recoveries are allocated to the Customer Fund for distribution to BLMIS customers with allowed claims; none of the money recovered is used to pay administrative costs — all Trustee, legal and accounting fees, as well as administrative expenses, are paid by SIPC.
 That structural feature — SIPC bearing the fee burden rather than the Customer Fund — means the 50th fee application, while large, does not directly impair the pro-rata recovery percentage for allowed claimants. The distinction matters for claims-pricing models that incorrectly netted fee applications against distributable assets.


As of July 24, 2026, SIPC has committed approximately $850.9 million to the BLMIS liquidation.
 The trustee has described the recovery initiative as having recovered approximately 75 cents on the dollar of the stolen money. The next distribution motion has not yet been filed as of today's date; the cadence over recent cycles has been roughly semi-annual.

The plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million. A key component involves the appointment of a disinterested Liquidating Trustee to manage the company's assets, including pursuing potential legal claims and overseeing distributions to creditors.



The Celsius-related objecting creditors have asserted over $133 million in preference claims against the debtor in connection with the pending bankruptcy case of Celsius Network LLC et al. (Case No. 22-10964) pending in the Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York.
 That cross-docket preference exposure is the structural overhang on any distribution waterfall: resolution of the Celsius preference claim is a prerequisite to accurate claims sizing, and a successful preference recovery by the Celsius estate would increase Celsius's allowed claim in this case while simultaneously subjecting any net-winner transferees to avoidance risk under 11 U.S.C. § 548 and the applicable Ponzi presumption.

The committee's plan filing flagged concern about pre-bankruptcy conduct. 
The committee's filing raises concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions, including loans made to affiliated entities.
 Those insider transactions are the seed of potential § 548 and state UFTA/UVTA avoidance claims — the very avoidance-action pipeline that will ultimately define whether the Liquidating Trust generates any material recovery beyond the current asset base.  The court rejected that election given the actual claims profile, which is how the case converted to a standard Chapter 11 with a committee — and why the $192 million claims pool is now driving the process.

### 4. SEC Enforcement Pipeline — FY 2025 Results and the Disgorgement Question

The enforcement pipeline that generates tomorrow's receiverships bears watching. 
Notable FY 2025 actions included charges in connection with several large-scale Ponzi schemes, including one involving approximately 2,700 investors and an alleged $400 million in losses and another involving approximately 300 investors and more than $140 million in alleged losses.
 Those cases — Paramount Management Group / Daryl Heller ($400 million, ~2,700 investors) and First Liberty Building & Loan / Edwin Frost IV ($140 million, ~300 investors) — are both in the early-stage receivership pipeline. 
During fiscal year 2025, the Commission returned approximately $262 million to harmed investors and awarded approximately $60 million to 48 individual whistleblowers.


A structural risk to recovery economics hangs on a pending Supreme Court decision. 
The U.S. Supreme Court will decide *SEC v. Sripetch* on whether the SEC has the ability to seek disgorgement as a penalty without showing investors suffered financial harm.
 A ruling adverse to the SEC would narrow the disgorgement toolkit — potentially reducing receiver-estate size in future enforcement cases and, by extension, the assets available for victim distribution. Claims investors pricing positions in SEC-receivership cases should treat that decision as a binary risk factor.


Enforcement actions have targeted fraudsters who preyed on veterans, seniors, and members of religious communities — themes observed in the program's current FY 2026 reported cases.
 Affinity-fraud cases of this type tend to involve diffuse retail victims, slow aggregation of proof of claim submissions, and extended distribution timelines — characteristics that compress secondary-market claim prices relative to institutional-claimant cases where claim amounts are crisply liquidated.

### 2. OneCoin — DOJ Remission Window Has Closed; Administration Phase Begins


On April 13, 2026, the Department of Justice announced the beginning of the remission compensation process to provide recovery for victims of the international investment scheme involving OneCoin Ltd.
 
Victims of the $4 billion OneCoin fraud could apply for compensation from a $40 million fund of seized assets; OneCoin was co-founded by fugitive Ruja Ignatova and Karl Sebastian Greenwood, and defrauded as many as 3.4 million investors worldwide through a fake cryptocurrency that never operated on a blockchain.


The petition deadline was June 30, 2026. 
The Criminal Division's Money Laundering, Narcotics, and Forfeiture Section managed the process; Kroll Settlement Administration LLC serves as the designated administrator.
 
Eligible victims included those who purchased OneCoin or invested in the system between 2014 and 2019 and suffered a net loss, accounting for any withdrawals or recovered amounts; the programme had international scope, with non-U.S. investors permitted to apply.


The $40 million remission pool is materially disproportionate to the $4 billion aggregate fraud — a recovery rate below 1% against total losses — which underscores the structural limitations of DOJ forfeiture-based remission relative to civil receivership or bankruptcy distribution mechanisms. The forfeiture pool reflects only what has been seized and forfeited to date; Ignatova remains a fugitive. 
Co-founder Ruja Ignatova remains on the FBI's wanted list.
 Parallel asset-recovery proceedings continue in other jurisdictions: 
on January 16, 2026, the Royal Court of Guernsey ordered the registration and enforcement of an overseas forfeiture order against funds totaling GBP 8,590,200.92, which had been found in German legal proceedings to be the proceeds of Ignatova's criminal conduct.
 
The money was held with a Guernsey financial institution for the benefit of two Guernsey-registered companies, through which Ignatova commissioned the purchase of shares to acquire two freehold flats in Kensington, London.


For claims investors and victim-advocates: the June 30 deadline has lapsed. Anyone who missed the window should monitor the Kroll administration process for any late-petition exception notices. The Guernsey forfeiture and ongoing German proceedings suggest the asset-recovery perimeter continues to expand, but given the fugitive status of the primary defendant and the multi-jurisdictional complexity, the conversion of seized assets to victim distributions will take time.
