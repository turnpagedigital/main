# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Friday, July 17, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Two clocks are running simultaneously and both expire next week.** On Thursday evening USTR Jamieson Greer announced a 25% tariff on thousands of Brazilian imports effective July 22 under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974—the first major new tariff authority deployed since the Supreme Court invalidated IEEPA in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 24-1287 (Feb. 20, 2026). Twenty-four hours later, 
the 10% global import surcharge President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2132, is scheduled to expire by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026—150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 The structural question for importers tracking refund exposure is whether the administration uses the Section 122 sunset as a hard backstop or bridges to replacement Section 301 authority without a gap. The Brazil action answers that: Section 301 is the chosen successor vehicle, and the administration is moving aggressively.

---

### I. Brazil: Section 301 Tariffs at 25%, Effective July 22 — With a Second Tranche Coming


The U.S. will begin charging a 25% tariff on imports of certain goods from Brazil following an investigation alleging unfair trade practices. USTR characterized the year-long inquiry as finding "a number of Brazil's practices to be unreasonable and discriminatory, restricting the competitive position of American farmers, workers, innovators, and exporters," in a post issued Wednesday night.



The USTR announcement made Brazil the first country targeted under the Trump administration's new tariff strategy, which relies on Section 301 of U.S. trade law to justify new levies on dozens of countries.
 That is the operative legal frame: where IEEPA is gone, Section 301—which survived *Learning Resources* untouched—is now the instrument of first resort.


The tariffs will apply to thousands of Brazilian imports including sugar, agricultural machinery, apparel, electrical machinery, paper, and steel, though the U.S. exempted beef, coffee, rare earths, energy products, aircraft, aircraft parts, organic honey, pig iron, and unflavored instant coffee.
 
The American Chamber of Commerce for Brazil reported that the expanded exemptions list covers some $11 billion in annual trade—$2 billion less than expected—though the tariff "places Brazil among the countries facing the most restrictive conditions for access to the U.S. market."


This is not the end of Brazil's exposure. 
A separate U.S. investigation into forced-labor enforcement could add an additional 12.5% duty on Brazilian goods, with a decision due next week that would bring Brazil's total tariff burden to 37.5%.
 
Brazil has also been included in a separate Section 301 investigation by the USTR, due to conclude on July 24,
 the same date the Section 122 surcharge expires—a coincidence the administration appears to have engineered deliberately. 
Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Lula's government had "not negotiated in good faith," while Lula rejected the tariff decision as groundless and vowed countermeasures.


For importers and counsel tracking refund eligibility: the Brazil tariffs announced this week are Section 301, not IEEPA. They are prospective, not subject to the CAPE refund process, and carry no statutory sunset. They are also immune from the *Learning Resources* holding. Clients with Brazilian supply chains face a fundamentally different legal exposure than the IEEPA refund pool.

### II. Section 122 Surcharge: Hard Expiration July 24, Refund Posture Uncertain


Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending. Absent congressional action, the surcharge terminates on its own terms.
 
Current Section 122 tariffs are limited to a 150-day window and are set to expire on July 24, 2026, and cannot be immediately reimposed under the same provision.


The underlying litigation is *State of Oregon et al. v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. Trump* (CIT Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606, respectively). 
On May 7, 2026, the CIT held in those consolidated cases that the Trump administration's imposition of a 10% tariff pursuant to Section 122, to address "fundamental international payment problems," was unlawful because the government's interpretation of the statute was overly expansive.
 
The court found the administration exceeded its authority by citing general trade deficits rather than the specific "balance-of-payments deficits" Congress had in mind when it wrote Section 122 in 1974. However, the court limited its permanent injunction to the three named plaintiffs: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. Every other importer remains obligated to pay Section 122 duties unless they file their own case or a higher court grants broader relief.


On appeal: 
the government appealed on May 8. On May 12, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay, suspending the CIT's injunction while the appellate court considered a more durable stay pending appeal.
 
The CIT separately denied the government's own motion for a stay pending appeal on May 20, finding the government had failed to demonstrate irreparable harm or likelihood of success on the merits. The Federal Circuit's administrative stay nonetheless remained in place.
 On June 11, 
the Federal Circuit issued a stay of the CIT's injunction, which had prevented CBP from collecting duties pursuant to Section 122.
 
The government's initial brief is due to the Federal Circuit in July, making it unlikely this litigation is resolved prior to the July 24, 2026 expiration for the Section 122 duties. Because the CIT's injunction is stayed, CBP will continue to collect Section 122 duties until July 24, 2026.


The practical result: Section 122 refund exposure remains live but unresolved. The statutory sunset moots injunctive relief prospectively; the refund question for duties collected between February 24 and July 24 turns on whether the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's merits ruling. 
Section 122 duties are not refundable through CAPE.
 Importers who paid the surcharge and want to preserve a refund claim must file individual 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests or separate CIT actions under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i)—the same protective filing logic that applies to IEEPA's finally liquidated category.

### III. CAPE Refund Pipeline: $104 Billion Authorized, $71 Billion Paid, Phase 3 Imminent

The headline number from CBP's July 1, 2026 status report to Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton (CIT) is material: 
CBP has authorized $104.29 billion in refunds and paid out $71.06 billion (including interest). The July 1 update shows that the agency has made significant headway in returning the billions of dollars owed to U.S. importers based on the Supreme Court's invalidation of the emergency tariffs.
 
However, the filing also shows that frictions built into the process continue to slow the issuance of refunds, and an entire class of importers—collectively owed tens of billions of dollars—might be unable to get their money back without having to sue.


Phase architecture: 
CBP's CAPE refund system is progressing—Phase 1 is active, Phase 2 (reconciliation and antidumping/countervailing duty entries) was set to launch June 29, 2026, and Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries, available only to importers who filed at the CIT) is on track for late July 2026.
 
According to declarations filed with the CIT, around 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties, spread across more than 53 million entries.


The Phase 3 problem is structural, not operational. 
The government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 
Approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected, if CBP proceeds as planned, to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2 and 3 combined, regardless of liquidation status.
 For the remaining ~326,000 importers who have not sued, the finally liquidated category is in genuine jeopardy.

Reconciliation entries present a further cutoff risk: 
entries filed before roughly May 31, 2025 that liquidated on a standard 314-day cycle will have liquidated more than 80 days before June 29, 2026, and fall outside Phase 2's scope. Importers with reconciliation entries from the earliest months of the IEEPA tariff period should not assume Phase 2 will capture those entries.

### IV. Federal Circuit Appeal: The Universal Injunction Question


On June 2, 2026, the DOJ on behalf of the Trump administration formally appealed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit the order of Senior Judge Richard Eaton of the CIT compelling CBP to refund importers of record $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs that the Supreme Court declared unlawful in February.


The government's two-front challenge: 
the DOJ contests the CIT's authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries both liquidated and past the 80-day reprocessing window. The DOJ intends to challenge the refund order on two main fronts: (1) CBP cannot reprocess older entries without a court order specific to each importer; and (2) the refund order is an overly broad "universal injunction" that exceeds what the CIT is allowed to order.


The constitutional hook is *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, 606 U.S. 831 (2025). 
On an anticipated appeal, the government is expected to challenge the CIT's authority to issue a refund order applying nationwide to all importers, rather than only to the parties before the court, citing the Supreme Court's decision in CASA.
 CIT Judge Eaton distinguished CASA by reasoning that 
the prohibition on universal injunctions in CASA does not apply to the CIT because the court's statutory authority differs from that of federal district courts created under the Judiciary Act of 1789.
 
In related filings, the government characterized the refund orders as impermissible universal injunctions and argued that the CIT lacks authority to grant relief extending beyond parties before the court.



The DOJ intends to seek a stay of the refund order pending appeal, and if granted, refunds for finally liquidated entries could be halted for non-litigants entirely.
 The Federal Circuit has not yet ruled on the stay motion as of this writing; that ruling is the single most consequential near-term event for the ~$60+ billion in finally liquidated entries held by importers who have not yet filed suit. Any indication the court is leaning toward a stay should be treated as a filing trigger, not a wait-and-see signal.

### V. Section 301 as IEEPA's Successor: The Broader Architecture

The Brazil action is the administration's proof-of-concept for a Section 301–anchored tariff regime. 
The U.S. launched the initial investigation into Brazil last July under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, designed to address foreign practices affecting U.S. commerce and competitiveness.
 
Many expect new Section 301 tariffs to be announced by July 24, 2026, the date the Section 122 tariffs expire (unless Congress authorizes a continuation or modification).
 
USTR's Section 301 investigation, with a determination issued June 2 and a public hearing held July 7, proposes replacement duties of 10 to 12.5 percent that carry no statutory expiration, unlike Section 122's built-in 150-day sunset.
 Section 301 tariffs are also beyond the reach of *Learning Resources*—they are not IEEPA tariffs, they survive indefinitely, and they do not trigger CAPE. For companies that restructured supply chains or priced contracts on the assumption that the post-IEEPA trade environment would be materially lighter, the Brazil precedent should be read as a template, not an outlier.


Disputes over the allocation of IEEPA refunds are already emerging among importers of record, downstream purchasers and end-users, including class actions seeking to recover pass-through tariffs.
 
Similar disputes are conceivable with respect to Section 122 refunds.
 Any commercial party—retailer, distributor, manufacturer—that absorbed Section 122 or IEEPA costs through supply-chain pricing should now be mapping whether those pass-through amounts are potentially recoverable and from whom.

## Recommended Actions

GCs and CFOs should treat next week as a hard deadline week on two separate tracks. **Track one — Section 122 sunset (July 24):** The surcharge expires Thursday by operation of 19 U.S.C. § 2132 with no extension legislation pending; CBP will stop collecting after midnight, but importers who paid between February 24 and July 24 and want to preserve a refund claim if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's merits ruling in *Burlap and Barrel* and *State of Oregon* must file 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests or 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) CIT complaints now—Section 122 duties do not flow through CAPE. **Track two — IEEPA finally liquidated entries:** The Federal Circuit stay motion on the universal refund order is the most consequential pending ruling in this space; if a stay issues, non-litigant importers holding finally liquidated entries lose Phase 3 access entirely, and the two-year CIT filing clock is already running; any importer not yet in suit should file a protective action this week regardless of Phase 3's technical readiness in late July. **Track three — Brazil / Section 301:** Clients importing sugar, steel, apparel, paper, or electrical machinery from Brazil face a 25% duty effective July 22, with a potential additional 12.5% forced-labor tranche due as early as next week—compliance teams should flag affected HTS classifications immediately and model total landed-cost impact at the 37.5% worst-case rate. For all three tracks, document internal duty allocations and counterparty pass-through mechanics in writing now; the class-action exposure on IEEPA and Section 122 pass-through disputes is active and expanding.
