# ⚖️ Tariff Refunds | Saturday, August 01, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The dominant development this week is the completion of the IEEPA-to-Section-301 handoff: 
the new Section 301 forced-labor duties took effect at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, immediately after the 150-day Section 122 surcharge ended, imposing tariffs of 10% or 12.5% on products from 80 countries covering 99.4% of imports into the United States.
 The CAPE pipeline moves to its most consequential week yet: 
CBP is required to file a short CAPE progress report with the court by 5:00 p.m. EDT on August 4, 2026, followed by a closed settlement conference on August 5.
 
Together with the class certification hearing for IEEPA duty refund plaintiffs set for August 6, 2026, these proceedings may provide important additional guidance regarding CAPE's continued rollout and the potential treatment of importers that have not filed individual actions.
 Three threads warrant close attention heading into that cluster: (1) the Section 301 legal challenges just filed at the CIT, (2) the Phase 3 reliquidation order Judge Eaton entered July 17 and what it requires of counsel in the roughly 3,700 pending cases, and (3) the Holland & Knight "File Now" alert — published July 27 — confirming that the window for protective actions has narrowed materially.

### 1. Section 301 Replacement Tariffs: Seamless Handoff, Immediate Legal Challenge


On July 23, 2026, USTR introduced new Section 301 tariffs affecting imports from 60 trading partners, entering into force at 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on July 24, 2026, following USTR investigations into whether trading partners had adopted and effectively enforced prohibitions on imports produced using forced labor. The new measures replace the temporary Section 122 tariffs that expired July 24 and establish a permanent legal framework for these additional duties, with additional duties of 10% or 12.5% now applying to most imports from the 60 economies covered.
 
The final action covered roughly 60 economies — about 99.4% of U.S. imports — wider in scope than the single-rate, 46-country proposal that had been on the table days earlier. EU-origin goods are unaffected, remaining on the U.S.-EU trade deal's 15% ceiling.


The practical tariff arithmetic for most importers is close to a wash from the Section 122 baseline. 
The forced-labor action is simpler to model than feared: 10% or 12.5% depending on origin, with MFN caps for certain major partners and Section 232 and USMCA exemptions. For many importers, the move from the flat 10% Section 122 surcharge to a 10% or 12.5% Section 301 duty is nearly revenue-neutral, and for those whose goods fall in the exemption annexes it is a reduction. The larger cost risk sits in a pending excess-capacity investigation, which remains unresolved.


The critical legal distinction: 
Section 301 tariffs do not expire the way Section 122 did — once in place, they can run for years.
 That permanence is precisely what drew an immediate lawsuit. 
The Liberty Justice Center filed a complaint captioned *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. et al. v. Greer et al.* in the U.S. Court of International Trade on July 24, 2026,
 the same day the duties took effect. 
The lawsuit argues that the government cannot preserve a predetermined global tariff policy simply by moving from one statute to another.
 
The core theory is that USTR converted separate investigations into a largely standardized global tariff program without adequately establishing, economically or procedurally, the predicate findings that Section 301 requires.
 
As a practical matter, however, importers should expect the Section 301 tariffs to remain in place until either the investigated economy meaningfully addresses the underlying forced-labor enforcement failures, USTR modifies the action, or a court intervenes.


For the IEEPA refund desk, the Section 301 transition matters in one specific way: 
only tariffs paid under the invalidated IEEPA authority are eligible for refunds — duties paid under Section 122, 232, 301, or other authorities are not subject to the CIT's refund orders.
 The five-month Section 122 window (February 24–July 24) is closed as a collection matter; whether it generates a separate refund pool depends entirely on how the Burlap & Barrel challenge and any related CIT proceedings resolve. That is a second-order risk to monitor, not a live refund event today.

### 2. CAPE Phase 3: The July 17 Eaton Order and What It Requires This Week

The operative Phase 3 instrument is Judge Eaton's order of July 17, 2026, in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066. 
On July 17, 2026, Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued an order directing CBP to "reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, any and all of Plaintiffs' entries that have been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which Plaintiffs made estimated deposits pursuant to IEEPA." This followed the transfer to Judge Eaton's docket of more than 3,700 cases seeking IEEPA refunds.



The order provides the government with the legal authority it had said it needed before it would voluntarily reliquidate finally liquidated entries — the government's position had consistently been that a court order directing reliquidation of entries whose liquidation had become final would be necessary before such refunds could flow.
 The relief is not universal: 
the order applies only to companies that have filed cases seeking IEEPA tariff refunds at the CIT, and it directs reliquidation of all entries liquidated for more than 80 days, which includes entries that may continue to become final during the CAPE process itself.



To successfully submit a CAPE declaration for finally liquidated entries, plaintiffs must first submit to CBP certain information, including their importer of record identification numbers.
 CBP will issue separate instructions to plaintiffs' counsel. This is a procedural predicate — firms that have filed but have not yet submitted importer IDs are sitting on a Phase 3 delay of their own making.


The CIT's order further states that the court will issue case-specific orders in each of the approximately 3,700 individual IEEPA cases directing Customs to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries — and separately confirms that the new lead case is *Freestyle World Inc. v. United States*, as the plaintiff in *Euro-Notions* intends to voluntarily dismiss.


On the volume side, the scale of what has already moved through CAPE is significant. 
As of CBP's July 13 filing, CAPE has accepted 24.4 million entries for IEEPA-duty removal, with approximately $86.3 billion completed, certified, and sent to Treasury.
 That figure sits against a total pool 
of roughly $166 billion in collected IEEPA tariffs
 — meaning approximately 52% of the pool has been certified for refund, with Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries) and the protest-track entries constituting the bulk of the remainder.


There is currently no procedure for requesting a refund of IEEPA tariffs on an entry that has been protested. Expanding CAPE's capabilities to cover protested entries would allow CBP to process a broader range of refund claims through a single administrative platform
 — CBP indicated at the July 15 webinar that it is evaluating that expansion, but no order has issued and no timeline has been confirmed.

### 3. August 4–6 Proceedings: The Consequential Cluster

Three successive proceedings next week will define the Phase 3 operational timeline and the class-action path for non-filers:

**August 4 — CAPE Progress Report (5:00 p.m. EDT).** 
CBP is required to file a short progress report on CAPE with the court by 5:00 p.m. EDT on August 4, 2026.
 The report will confirm whether Phase 3 infrastructure is operational following the July 17 reliquidation order and whether CBP has begun accepting importer-ID submissions from the 3,700-case population. This is the first post-Phase-3-order data point.

**August 5 — Closed Settlement Conference.** 
A closed settlement conference follows on August 5.
 No public filings will emerge from this session, but its outcome could affect the government's posture in the Federal Circuit appeal — DOJ's June 2 notices of appeal remain pending, and a negotiated administrative resolution of the finally-liquidated-entry question would moot the appeal's sharpest edge.

**August 6 — Class Certification Hearing.** 
The class certification hearing for IEEPA duty refund plaintiffs is set for August 6, 2026,
 in connection with the pending motion filed by Terry Precision Cycling. 
Plaintiffs argued at the June 9 hearing that if the government is committed to refunds but worried about *CASA* (the Supreme Court's universal-injunction decision), it should not oppose class certification — though Judge Eaton expressed reservations about that path.
 A class certification ruling, even a favorable one, would face immediate government opposition and likely a Federal Circuit interlocutory challenge; its primary value is as leverage in the settlement conference the day before.

### 4. The Refund Gap for Non-Filers: Holland & Knight "File Now" Alert (July 27)


For importers that have not filed suit, the path to refunds on finally liquidated entries remains unresolved. Potential relief depends on pending proceedings before the Federal Circuit and unresolved class certification motions at the CIT, neither of which presently provides a certain outcome or timeline.



On June 2, 2026, DOJ formally appealed the CIT's order requiring refunds of all IEEPA tariffs. In related filings, the government characterized the refund orders as impermissible universal injunctions and argued that the CIT lacks authority to grant relief extending beyond parties before the court. Although the appeal encompasses the broader refund orders, recent government filings suggest the principal dispute concerns finally liquidated entries, with the government arguing that importers seeking refunds for finally liquidated entries must pursue importer-specific remedies.


The practical consequence: 
one of the most consequential remaining issues concerns entries that became "finally liquidated" before refunds could be processed — and for those entries, the distinction between importers that filed CIT actions and those that did not has become increasingly important.
 The July 17 Eaton order resolved that distinction in favor of filers. It does nothing for non-filers.


Importers that have not filed should evaluate promptly whether a protective action is appropriate.
 The Holland & Knight alert, published July 27, treats the August 6 class certification hearing as a near-term data point but does not treat it as a substitute for individual action — the message is that class relief is uncertain and the filing window is narrowing with each day that more entries pass the 80-day liquidation mark.
