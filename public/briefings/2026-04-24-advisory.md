# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 24, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Anthropic's cross-motion for summary judgment in Concord Music Group v. Anthropic, filed on April 22, represents the most consequential fair-use motion in any AI music copyright case to date — the company is asking Judge Eumi Lee to rule as a matter of law that training Claude on the publishers' 499-song catalog was categorically transformative fair use, while simultaneously arguing that the publishers' DMCA § 1202 claim for removal of copyright management information fails because "even after almost two years of discovery, Publishers have not identified a single work in suit from which Anthropic removed CMI." ([Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/04/22/anthropic-music-publishers-lawsuit-summary-judgment-motion/)) The motion's legal architecture splits the dispute into inputs and outputs — training is transformative because it teaches Claude language patterns rather than reproducing expressive content, while the rare occasions when Claude generates lyrics are user-provoked artifacts that should not give rise to direct liability against Anthropic — a framing that directly engages the output-side infringement theories that Concord and its cross-industry amici (RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, AAP, News/Media Alliance, STM, and Authors Guild) have argued undermine the fourth fair-use factor by demonstrating market substitution at scale. ([Noah News](https://noah-news.com/anthropic-advances-fair-use-defence-in-high-stakes-ai-music-copyright-case/)) The summary judgment hearing is scheduled for July 15, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. before Judge Lee in San Jose, and the ruling — whether it embraces or rejects the input/output distinction — will set the doctrinal template for every pending AI training case, including the OpenAI MDL's fair-use briefing expected later this summer and the parallel Concord/UMG v. Anthropic II action covering 20,517 torrented works. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/))

Meanwhile, the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement enters a critical countdown phase: Anthropic's second $300 million installment is due in six days on April 30, bringing total funded commitments to $600 million of the $1.5 billion fund, and the rescheduled fairness hearing before Judge Martínez-Olguín is now twenty days away on May 14, 2026 at 2:00 p.m. PT. ([anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com](https://www.anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com/dates)) The 440,490 titles claimed by the March 30 deadline — a 91.3% claim rate that the Authors Guild describes as unprecedented in class action practice — translate to an estimated base payout of approximately $2,931 per work after the revised fee petition of $187.5 million (12.5% of the fund, down from $300 million/20%) and administration costs, though the actual per-title figure depends on the publisher-versus-author allocation formula and whether the court orders any cy pres distribution for residual unclaimed funds under Rule 23(e)(2). ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/anthropic-settlement-update-91-percent-of-books-claimed/)) ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/digital/copyright/article/100208-anthropic-settlement-hearing-comes-into-focus.html)) The six-plus unsealed objections — including Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's challenge to per-title valuation methodology and allegations of publisher-over-author favoritism in the allocation framework — will be front and center at the May 14 hearing, and class counsel's response briefs, expected in early May, will need to address these structural concerns with specificity if the court is to make the Rule 23(e) fairness findings necessary for final approval.

On the output-side liability front, a New York Law Journal analysis published April 20 examines how two recent developments are fundamentally reshaping copyright liability theories for AI platforms: Judge Colleen McMahon's ruling in Advance Local Media v. Cohere that "substitutive summaries" — non-verbatim AI outputs that mirror the expressive structure and journalistic storytelling choices of copyrighted originals — may plausibly infringe copyright even without literal copying, and the Supreme Court's unanimous Cox Communications reversal (March 25) that narrowed contributory copyright liability to circumstances involving inducement or services not capable of substantial noninfringing uses. ([New York Law Journal](https://www.law.com/newyorklawjournal/2026/04/20/recent-decisions-reshape-copyright-liability-for-ai-platforms-and-their-users/)) ([Copyright Lately](https://copyrightlately.com/court-rules-ai-news-summaries-may-infringe-copyright/)) Together, these decisions create a doctrinal pincer: direct liability theories are expanding (substitutive summaries need not be verbatim to infringe), while contributory liability theories are contracting (Cox requires inducement or a service with no substantial noninfringing use), and the net effect is to push the AI copyright battlefield toward output-side direct infringement — precisely the theory that Anthropic's Concord MSJ is attempting to foreclose and that the OpenAI MDL's 108 million log production (20M + 78M + 10M reservoirs) is designed to prove.

Today, the Mozilla Foundation's Copyforward symposium opens at USC Gould School of Law in Los Angeles, bringing together legal scholars, filmmakers, guild representatives, and entertainment lawyers for a two-day examination of how AI is reshaping creative authorship, intellectual property, and copyright — a conversation that unfolds against the backdrop of the White House's March 20 National AI Policy Framework, which acknowledged that "training of AI models on copyrighted material does not violate copyright laws" while simultaneously recommending that Congress consider licensing frameworks and collective rights systems for creator compensation. ([USC Gould](https://events.law.usc.edu/event/copyforward)) ([White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/03/president-donald-j-trump-unveils-national-ai-legislative-framework/)) On Capitol Hill, the three-bill legislative package championed at last week's GRAMMYS on the Hill — the TRAIN Act, the CLEAR Act (S. 3813), and the NO FAKES Act — continues to build bipartisan momentum, though the White House Framework's studied neutrality on the fair-use question suggests that any enacted legislation will likely focus on disclosure and transparency obligations rather than a legislative resolution of whether AI training constitutes infringement, leaving the pivotal doctrinal questions to the Concord PSJ ruling and the OpenAI MDL motions expected this summer. ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/blogs/in-the-know/5843379-lawmakers-honor-music-protections/))

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and class members monitoring the Bartz settlement should calendar two imminent milestones: Anthropic's $300 million second installment on April 30, which will serve as a tangible marker of the defendant's financial commitment under Rule 23(e)(2)(D) at the fairness hearing, and the May 14 hearing itself, accessible via Zoom, at which the court will address the unsealed objections, the revised fee petition, and the publisher-versus-author allocation formula — claimants who submitted by the March 30 deadline should verify their confirmation through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and review whether the objections raise issues material to their individual claim positions. Music publishers and AI developers alike should study Anthropic's April 22 Concord MSJ filing closely, as the input/output distinction it advances will define the fair-use framework for the July 15 hearing and, by extension, for every AI training case in the pipeline — publishers with works in the 499-song catalog should prepare for the possibility that an adverse ruling on training-as-fair-use would force a strategic pivot to output-side theories, while AI developers should evaluate whether their own training practices and output-guardrail architectures would withstand scrutiny under the same analytical framework. Practitioners tracking output-side liability should integrate the Advance Local v. Cohere "substitutive summaries" doctrine into their compliance assessments immediately, as Judge McMahon's rejection of a bright-line copying threshold means that AI-generated content need not reproduce verbatim text to create infringement exposure — any output that mirrors the expressive structure of a copyrighted original could be actionable, a standard that implicates summarization, paraphrasing, and retrieval-augmented generation workflows across the industry. AI developers subject to the proposed CLEAR Act's $5,000 per-work penalty structure should accelerate training-data provenance audits and prepare for the possibility that retroactive disclosure mandates — applied to models already in production — could create compliance obligations that dwarf the cost of prospective licensing deals.
