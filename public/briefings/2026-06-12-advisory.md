## Analysis & Developments

**Today's Delta:** The Third Circuit held oral argument yesterday — June 11, 2026 — in *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Rights-holders, opt-out plaintiffs, and counsel tracking the macro AI copyright liability arc face a convergent moment: the appellate fair-use standard will likely be set by the Third Circuit within the next four to six months, while the Bartz fund distribution timeline is now gating on judicial signature alone.

### I. *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence*, No. 25-8018 (3d Cir.) — Oral Argument, June 11, 2026

Yesterday's oral argument at the Third Circuit was the most consequential AI-copyright proceeding to date in U.S. courts. Counsel for Ross Intelligence encountered a barrage of probing questions from Third Circuit judges who expressed skepticism over the company's bid to overturn an AI-training copyright fair use decision against the company in a case over copied Westlaw headnotes. Although the attorney for Westlaw owner Thomson Reuters Enterprise Center GmbH also faced critical questions, the panel jumped right into questions about the impact of Ross's use of Westlaw headnotes on the market for the legal search engine's work.

The interlocutory appeal — certified under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b) — turns on two discrete controlling questions: (1) whether Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System satisfy the originality threshold under 17 U.S.C. § 102(a) and *Feist Publications v. Rural Tel. Serv. Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991); and (2) whether Ross's wholesale ingestion of those headnotes to train a competing AI legal search tool qualifies as fair use under the four-factor framework of 17 U.S.C. § 107. Judges spent much of Thursday's oral argument probing whether Ross Intelligence built a market substitute for Westlaw and whether its use of Thomson Reuters' copyrighted materials was transformative — a focus that could signal a willingness to confront the AI-training fair-use question head on, rather than decide the appeal on narrower copyrightability grounds.

The underlying district court ruling came from Circuit Judge Stephanos Bibas, sitting by designation in the District of Delaware, who in early 2025 reversed his own 2023 summary judgment decision. The court emphasized that Ross's tool is not generative AI — it does not write legal research memos or opinions and merely identifies cases in response to user queries — and found that the nature-of-the-work and amount-used factors weighed in Ross's favor given the narrow scope of protection afforded the headnotes. However, the effect on the market heavily favored Thomson Reuters, as Ross's tool was directly competitive and undercut Westlaw's market. On May 23, 2025, the district court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard."

The Third Circuit's eventual ruling — expected within the coming months — will be the first appellate decision in the United States on fair use as applied to AI training data. It is the first appeal of a decision addressing whether the use of copyrighted works to train an AI model is a fair use. Ross has argued throughout that the decision will have sweeping consequences for AI innovation, while Thomson Reuters has framed the issue as straightforward commercial copying by a market competitor. Thomson Reuters framed the question as: whether a legal-research company that copied original editorial content from its competitor to build its own commercial product escapes copyright liability because the copied content is allegedly not copyrightable or its copying was fair use.

Pre-argument, the Third Circuit asked both parties for supplemental briefing on how its own recent fair use decision in *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes* applies, adding a parallel fair-use data point that may cut in Ross's favor. The Third Circuit asked the parties in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* for supplemental briefs on how its own fair use decision in *ASTM v. UpCodes* applies to their case. Ross's counsel before the Third Circuit argued that if the district court ruling stands, it will have sweeping consequences for innovation in both legal research and artificial intelligence. In its appeal brief filed in September 2025, Ross Intelligence argued that if the decision stands, it will have "sweeping consequences for innovation" in both legal research and artificial intelligence.

The panel composition was established at grant: the order was signed by Judge Cindy K. Chung, and joined by Judges Peter J. Phipps and D. Brooks Smith. That same panel heard yesterday's argument. A decision could issue as early as September 2026 and would directly set precedent for every pending AI-training copyright dispute in the Third Circuit, while carrying persuasive authority nationwide — particularly in the Ninth Circuit, which is managing the consolidated OpenAI, Meta, and Anthropic follow-on matters.

**Practitioner note:** The Third Circuit's doctrinal signal yesterday was unmistakably focused on the fourth fair-use factor. That framing is materially adverse to AI defendants across the board: if the appellate standard anchors on whether an AI training corpus displaces the licensing market for the underlying work, nearly every LLM defendant faces elevated risk in pending cases — regardless of whether the trained model is generative or merely retrieval-based.

### II. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Pending; Distribution Calculations Complete

The $1.5 billion Bartz settlement — administered by JND Legal Administration at AnthropicCopyrightSettlement.com — remains under submission before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín following the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing. After a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly.

At the hearing, class counsel Justin Nelson (Susman Godfrey LLP) reported updated distribution economics. Class counsel stated the payout per work in the class will be $3,100, with the class having a 92.77% participation rate for the works on the works list. Per Authors Alliance and Publishing Perspectives, the hearing went smoothly with the claims rate updated to 92.77% — 447,576 of 482,460 works — and roughly 53 objections logged.

Judge Martínez-Olguín took the motions under submission and then issued an order requiring Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages by May 21, 2026 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; Plaintiffs were also required to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026. As of the June 9, 2026 CourtListener docket update, no final approval order had been entered. Per the settlement timeline, the Settlement Administrator was scheduled to calculate distributions as of June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later subject to whether appeals are filed.

The settlement fund mechanics are structured across four installments. Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion into a settlement fund distributed in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027 — at approximately $3,000 per covered work, with an additional $3,000 per work if the number of affected works exceeds 500,000. The fee request from co-lead counsel (Susman Godfrey and Lieff Cabraser Heimann & Bernstein) was revised downward from $300 million to $187.5 million. Authors who allege Anthropic pirated their work urged the court to grant final approval to the $1.5 billion settlement, along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.

Opt-out plaintiffs are already organizing parallel litigation. Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. (No. 3:26-cv-04482) demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works; a follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors. Because statutory copyright damages can reach $150,000 per infringed work, the exposure theoretically extends into the hundreds of billions of dollars, raising existential risk for Anthropic.

A critical scope limitation carries strategic significance for the broader AI copyright docket: Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy, not the act of AI training itself, and as such the fair use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers. This means the training-data fair-use question was deliberately carved out of the Bartz settlement and remains live — setting up the Ross/Third Circuit ruling and the OpenAI consolidated proceedings as the primary doctrinal vehicles going forward.

### III. *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal.) — Assigned to Judge Van Keulen; Docket Quiet

*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880, assigned to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen, remains in early posture with no significant docket activity reported in the 72-hour monitoring window. This action presents a distinct theory from Bartz: Concord Music Group alleges that Anthropic's Claude models reproduce lyrics and other musical content in their outputs — an output-generation theory, not a training-data ingestion theory — which directly implicates 17 U.S.C. § 106(1) (reproduction) and § 106(3) (distribution) rather than the § 107 fair-use analysis at the center of Bartz and Ross. The case is thus not covered by Bartz's settlement release, and Anthropic has not settled or resolved any output-liability claim on a class basis. Counsel and rights-holders in the music-publisher vertical should track Judge Van Keulen's forthcoming scheduling order, which will define the discovery and briefing posture that governs this non-Bartz track.

### IV. Broader AI Copyright Docket — Key Macro Signals

The Third Circuit argument and the Bartz near-final-approval moment are occurring against a backdrop of related proceedings that rights-holders must monitor as systemic risk indicators.

There are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages — including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others. In the Meta shadow-library track, Judge Vince Chhabria (N.D. Cal.) issued a summary judgment ruling in 2025 finding that Meta's downloading of books from LibGen did not constitute fair use, and plaintiffs in *Kadrey v. Meta* have since sought to certify that question for interlocutory appeal to the Ninth Circuit — a procedural move that, if granted, would create a Ninth Circuit parallel to the Third Circuit's Ross appeal. The Supreme Court's March 2, 2026 denial of certiorari in the Thaler human-authorship case (the U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 2, 2026, thereby reaffirming human authorship as a foundational requirement of U.S. copyright law, even in this era of rapid AI advancement) meanwhile closed off one alternative doctrinal avenue — AI-generated works — while leaving the training-data fair-use question entirely to the circuits.

For any case theory anchored on the fourth fair-use factor (market harm), yesterday's Ross argument is a forward indicator: a Third Circuit panel skeptical of market-substitution arguments from AI defendants will have a chilling effect on settlement-hold-out strategies by LLM developers whose training datasets overlap with commercially licensed content markets.
