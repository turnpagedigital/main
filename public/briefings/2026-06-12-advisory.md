### I. *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence*, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.) — Oral Argument Recap, June 11, 2026

Judges on the Third Circuit spent much of Thursday's oral argument probing whether ROSS Intelligence built a market substitute for Westlaw and whether its use of Thomson Reuters' copyrighted materials was transformative — a focus that could signal willingness to confront the AI-training fair-use question head-on, rather than deciding the appeal on narrower copyrightability grounds.

Counsel for ROSS Intelligence encountered a barrage of probing questions from the Third Circuit judges, who expressed skepticism over the company's bid to overturn an AI training copyright fair-use decision against the company in a case over copied Westlaw headnotes. Although Thomson Reuters' attorney also faced critical questions, the panel jumped right into questions about the impact of ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes on the market for the legal search engine's work.

The procedural posture is important. The Third Circuit appeal, docketed as No. 25-2153, was appealed from the District of Delaware, No. 1:20-cv-00613, on the district court's February 11, 2025 judgment. On February 11, 2025, Circuit Judge Stephanos Bibas — sitting by designation — granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, holding that Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and that ROSS's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use. The court found that ROSS had infringed 2,243 Westlaw headnotes and upheld the validity of Thomson Reuters's copyrights, with the exception of those that may have expired.

The interlocutory appeal was certified on two controlling questions: how much "creative spark" is required to meet the legal threshold of originality, and whether ROSS has a fair-use defense — issues Judge Bibas acknowledged are both "hard under existing precedent," leaving substantial ground for difference of opinion.

ROSS framed its appellate question as: "Is a short quote or paraphrase of judicial holding copyrightable and does the fair use doctrine protect ROSS's internal use of Westlaw's headnotes in memos that served as training data for an AI legal search engine?" Thomson Reuters countered: "Whether a legal-research company that copied original editorial content from its competitor to build its own commercial product is not liable for copyright infringement because the copied content is not copyrightable or its copying was fair use."

Pre-argument briefing surfaced a direct clash over the Third Circuit's own recent fair-use decision in *ASTM v. UpCodes*. Both parties, following the Third Circuit's April 27 text order directing supplemental briefing "addressing what impact, if any" the UpCodes decision has on the appeal, filed supplemental briefs on May 11 — each arguing that UpCodes supports its position. ROSS argued that the UpCodes framework requires courts to examine the market for headnotes specifically, not legal research platforms broadly, and that Thomson Reuters "conceded it has no evidence of actual market harm — no documented lost subscribers, no lost revenue traceable to ROSS — and that UpCodes requires courts to examine the record closely rather than inferring harm from the mere fact of competition." Thomson Reuters responded that, unlike UpCodes, ROSS was fully commercial, explicitly marketing its product as a direct Westlaw replacement.

The panel that sat for yesterday's argument — Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves, and Bové, Circuit Judges — thus had both a fully developed merits record and fresh supplemental briefing. For rights-holders and AI developers alike, this is "the first U.S. appeal to consider the intersection of copyright law and AI," and the decision "will have sweeping consequences for AI innovation," ROSS co-founder Andrew Arruda stated. The case is non-generative AI — the court emphasized that ROSS's tool does not write legal research memos or opinions and is merely a search tool; factors regarding the nature of the work and the amount used weighed in ROSS's favor, given the narrow scope of protection and the fact that headnotes did not appear to end users. That distinction matters: a panel ruling that intermediate copying of low-creativity, non-consumer-facing content is not fair use would be considerably more restrictive than the generative-AI cases require.

### II. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Pending; Distribution Calculation Begins

The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement. After a 75-minute hearing in San Francisco, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined for the moment to approve the settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect final approval relatively quickly.

The judge took the motions under submission and then issued a post-hearing order requiring: by May 21, 2026, Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; and, by the same date, plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.

The claims posture is remarkable. Class counsel filed papers showing that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed on March 19, 2026, indicating that a large number of claims came in at the very end of the claims process. Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed that the payout per work will be $3,100, with a class participation rate of 92.77%. That figure reflects attorneys' fees sought by plaintiff lawyers at 25% of the fund, or $375 million — later revised per Law360 to $187.5 million — plus service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff ($150,000 total for the three class representatives). Authors urged final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million.

The settlement's scope limitation is critical for downstream litigation strategy. *Bartz* is not really a settlement about AI training — per Authors Alliance executive director Dave Hansen. Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy, not the act of AI training, and as such the fair use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers. This means the piracy-first litigation template — which drove the $1.5 billion resolution — does not settle the underlying AI-training fair-use question that the Third Circuit is now deciding for the first time on appeal. The litigation around AI training is not ending but shifting to other cases; there are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others.

Distribution mechanics: per the Authors Guild, the settlement administrator was scheduled to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed. Anthropic is funding the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027. Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.

Opt-out and follow-on litigation has already materialized. Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the *Bartz* settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal., No. 3:26-cv-04482, demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works. A follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.

*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal., Judge Susan G. Van Keulen), remains in active discovery. The case targets AI training on song lyrics and reproduction of lyrics in Claude outputs under 17 U.S.C. §§ 106(1) and 106(3), advancing music-publisher copyright claims that are structurally distinct from the book-piracy theory that drove the *Bartz* settlement. No new docket activity has been logged in the 72-hour scan window, but the case's outcome on intermediate-copying and lyrics-reproduction issues will track closely to the Third Circuit's eventual ruling in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS*. Practitioners should watch for any scheduling order or motion practice setting the discovery cutoff or dispositive motion briefing.

### IV. Broader AI Copyright Litigation Landscape — Structural Themes

The *Ross* oral argument and *Bartz* disbursement cycle crystallize three structural tensions dominating this docket cycle:

**Market-harm circularity.** The third circuit panel's focus on market substitution echoes a recurring tension in the AI cases: the effect on the market heavily favored Thomson Reuters, as ROSS's tool was directly competitive and undercut Westlaw's market. But ROSS's supplemental brief argued that the district court made a categorical error by treating "legal research platforms" as the relevant market, rather than examining what West actually derives from the specific works at issue — its headnotes — and the percentage of Westlaw revenues attributable to them. If the Third Circuit adopts ROSS's narrower market-framing, that standard would significantly liberalize fair-use analysis in generative-AI cases where the training data is just one input into a multi-modal model.

**Non-generative vs. generative AI.** The *Thomson Reuters* case differs from the more sprawling copyright lawsuits against Anthropic, Meta, and OpenAI in a number of ways: its AI is non-generative, uses relatively low-creativity legal summaries, and the headnotes did not appear to end users. A ruling against ROSS on facts most favorable to fair use could be read broadly as foreclosing fair use in generative-AI training cases — a concern that has drawn amicus briefs from law professors.

**Piracy-first vs. training-first theories.** The *Bartz* settlement's limitation to piracy claims — with the fair-use question on AI training expressly unsettled — leaves GCs and IP counsel advising AI developers in precisely the posture where training-data provenance hygiene matters most. If a developer acquired training data through channels that can be characterized as unauthorized download or shadow-library ingestion, the *Bartz* template suggests eight-figure to ten-figure exposure even before the training fair-use question is resolved.
