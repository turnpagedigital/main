## Analysis & Developments

**Today's controlling delta:** As of today, the Section 122 surcharge is eighteen days from statutory extinction. 
The Section 122 duties under Presidential Proclamation 11012 — a 10% ad valorem surcharge on virtually all imports effective February 24, 2026 — are scheduled to expire on July 24, 2026.
 The Federal Circuit's June 11 stay has locked CBP's collection apparatus through that date, but the administration's Section 301 investigative runway is almost gone: 
USTR is conducting two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations, which were expected to conclude before the Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026.
 Whether those determinations land before July 24 is the week's most consequential open question. Simultaneously, the IEEPA refund pipeline reached a structural inflection point: 
as of June 29, 2026, CBP reported that CAPE declarations had cleared file validation covering 18.1 million entries, with roughly $71.06 billion in refunds certified and sent to Treasury — but 4.36 million entries failed CAPE's entry-level checks.
 And CAPE Phase 3 — the tranche covering finally liquidated entries and an estimated $30+ billion in contested refunds — is on track for late July but blocked by an active Federal Circuit appeal, No. 26-1898.

### I. The IEEPA Refund Pipeline: $71 Billion Processed, $30+ Billion Still Contested

The IEEPA refund machinery that CIT Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton has been supervising since March 2026 is now running at an industrial scale with no precedent in customs administration. 
Approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 CBP's Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) platform — built inside the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) portal — is the mechanism Congress and the courts have de facto authorized CBP to use for bulk refund processing.


In all of the last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type. In just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.
 The scale is staggering, and the system is still in the middle of its phased rollout:

**Phase 1** (launched April 20, 2026): 
Phase 1 applies to unliquidated entries and entries liquidated within 80 days of the CAPE submission date.
 As of June 29, 2026, 
CBP reported that CAPE declarations had cleared file validation covering 18.1 million entries, with 15.92 million liquidated or reliquidated without IEEPA duties, and roughly $71.06 billion in refunds certified and sent to Treasury for disbursement.


**Phase 2** (live as of June 29, 2026): 
Effective June 29, 2026, CAPE accepted reconciliation-flagged entries (entry types 01, 02, and 06) where no reconciliation entry (type 09) has been filed.
 A parallel procedural note for practitioners: 
effective July 7, 2026 — today — warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) will no longer be accepted on a CAPE Declaration. Warehouse withdrawals (Entry Types 31, 32, 34, 38) continue to be accepted, with refunds processed upon (re)liquidation of the associated warehouse entry.
 Filers who submitted warehouse entries (Type 21 or 22) in the April 20–July 6 transition window without corresponding warehouse withdrawal submissions face a gap: 
those entries will not be (re)liquidated with an IEEPA duty refund; filers will need to submit separate CAPE Declarations covering the warehouse withdrawals on which IEEPA duties were paid.


**Phase 3** (targeted late July 2026): 
Phase 3 of CAPE will be ready by the end of July 2026; CBP confirmed that timeline at the June 9 CIT hearing before Senior Judge Eaton, through Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas.
 Phase 3 covers the contested category: finally liquidated entries. 
A large category of entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund clock started — remains contested, with the government estimating the dollar exposure at potentially more than $30 billion.


The Phase 3 problem is not technical — it is legal. 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion asserting, for the first time since the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, that it lacks the legal authority to issue IEEPA refunds on finally liquidated entries without an importer-specific court judgment.
 The government's stated position, now on appeal: 
DOJ stated that "Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final," and that to make refunds available for such entries, "an importer must file suit in this Court, and the Court must enter an importer-specific order."



Judge Eaton promptly denied CBP's motion, and the government filed an appeal on June 2, 2026.
 That appeal is now docketed at the Federal Circuit as 
No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026,
 in the consolidated refund administration proceedings. 
The government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT; approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined.
 For the remaining universe of importers without a CIT filing: 
the risk is no longer theoretical — the government's current position is that importers who did not file at the CIT may wait indefinitely — and perhaps permanently — for refunds of finally liquidated entries.


The bottom line on CAPE entry failures: 
three fixable failure reasons — importer/filer mismatch, entry-number errors, and CSV template misalignment — are costing importers refunds they are otherwise entitled to.
 Correct and resubmit before phases close.

The governing CIT case for refund administration is Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States, 
CIT No. 25-00595, with CBP filing its latest status declaration on July 1, 2026.
 V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States, 
Court No. 25-00066,
 remains the companion proceeding before Judge Eaton.

### II. Section 122 — Eighteen Days to Statutory Expiry; Federal Circuit Stay Holds; Section 301 Replacement Clock Running

The Section 122 litigation track has reached a temporary equilibrium that will be broken, one way or another, before the end of the month — either by the surcharge's own expiration or by Section 301 replacement authority.

**The CIT decision:** 
On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the CIT, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 The case docket numbers are 
State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026).



The 2-1 CIT majority — comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly — held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted, and instead improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 The dissent, authored by Judge Stanceu, 
disagreed with the majority's interpretation of the legislative history that liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance are the only methods to measure "balance-of-payments," explaining that had Congress intended to limit its measurement to those factors, it would have been more explicit.


**The Federal Circuit stay:** 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion to stay the CIT's injunction, allowing continued collection of the 10% Section 122 surcharge from all importers — including the three named plaintiff-importers who had won relief at the CIT: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.
 The stay rationale is significant: 
the Federal Circuit concluded the government had made a sufficient showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits, explaining that the CIT's interpretation limiting "balance-of-payments deficit" to deficits measured by liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance "may be incorrect."


That language — "likely to succeed on the merits" — carries real weight for refund exposure. 
The government's initial brief is due to the Federal Circuit in July, making it unlikely that this litigation is resolved prior to the July 24, 2026 expiration of the Section 122 duties.
 Practically: 
because the CIT's injunction is now stayed, CBP will continue to collect these duties from all importers until July 24, 2026.


**The remedy gap:** 
The permanent injunction issued against the government's collection of the Section 122 tariffs extends only to the three importer plaintiffs who were found to have standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.
 Total Section 122 collections now in the pipeline: 
importers have already paid roughly $25 billion in Section 122 duties since late February, and that meter keeps running while the case is argued.


**The replacement question:** The administration is running a parallel track to ensure tariff continuity after July 24. 
The administration initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices; the administration reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.
 USTR is conducting 
two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations.
 If those determinations issue before July 24, the Section 122 litigation becomes largely academic for forward-looking compliance — though the refund claim for entries paid between February 24 and the expiry date remains live regardless.

### III. The IEEPA Ruling's Structural Legacy — What the Supreme Court Actually Held and What It Did Not

The foundation underlying everything above: 
in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, argued November 5, 2025 and decided February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's August 2025 ruling.
 The vote was 6-3: 
the majority opinion, written by Chief Justice John Roberts, was joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Ketanji Brown Jackson.
 
Justices Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Alito dissented, concluding that IEEPA authorizes tariffs and that the President acted within delegated authority.


Case numbers: 
Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, No. 24-1287; Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc., No. 25-250, decided 6-3 that IEEPA does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs.
 The Federal Circuit's en banc mandate in V.O.S. Selections, Nos. 2025-1812 and 2025-1813, issued forthwith on 
March 2, 2026 (en banc per curiam).


On the merits: 
Chief Justice Roberts concluded that Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution vests the power to lay and collect taxes, duties, and imposts exclusively in Congress; the Framers deliberately gave Congress alone control over the taxing power and did not vest any part of that authority in the executive branch.
 The Court held that 
while the statute authorizes the President to "regulate importation," it contains no reference to tariffs or duties; Congress has consistently used explicit language when delegating tariff authority, imposing defined limits on scope, duration, and procedure; and in that context, the absence of tariff-specific language in IEEPA was decisive.


Two implementation points that practitioners must keep in mind:

First, 
the Court did not address whether refunds must be issued or how any reimbursement should operate.
 The refund obligation flows from the CIT's separate order, not the Supreme Court opinion itself — which explains why DOJ is contesting the universality of Judge Eaton's order on appeal.

Second, the ruling did not disable all tariff authority. 
Congress has delegated tariff authority to the President through other statutes, such as Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, but each of those delegations is explicit, comes with rate caps, time limits, and procedural preconditions.
 The effective tariff landscape as of today: 
China faces a 10% Section 122 + 25–100% Section 301 effective rate of 35–110%; Canada and Mexico face 0% if USMCA-qualifying (89% of goods), otherwise 10% Section 122.

### IV. Framework Agreements and the Refund Math Complication

One operational detail that surfaces repeatedly in CAPE failure filings deserves focused attention. 
The Framework Agreements with certain countries — the European Union, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland/Liechtenstein — required that the Column 1/General Duty be paid along with IEEPA duties, such that the combined duty rate equaled 15%; the total 15% could be reported on a single line on the entry summary. Importers will not receive a refund of the total 15% because a portion of that 15% accounts for the Column 1/General Duty rate which remains in effect.


This means importers whose entries involved EU, Japanese, Korean, or Swiss goods, and who reported a blended 15% rate, may have overclaimed or miscalculated their expected refund — and should reconcile their CAPE declarations against the actual IEEPA-only component before reliquidation locks the figure. The error is structural and will not be self-corrected by CBP; it requires a proactive post-summary correction or CAPE amendment.

## Recommended Actions

**For importers with unliquidated or Phase 1/2-eligible entries:** Verify today that warehouse entries (Types 21/22) submitted to CAPE before July 7 have corresponding warehouse withdrawal entries in the system; as of today, new warehouse entry submissions are rejected outright, and the transition-period exposure identified in CSMS #69066837 is not self-curing. Correct importer/filer mismatches, entry-number errors, and CSV template misalignments identified in CAPE rejection reports — these are fixable failures costing real recoveries. For EU-, Japan-, South Korea-, and Switzerland/Liechtenstein-sourced goods reported at a blended 15% rate, extract the IEEPA-only component before submitting or amending CAPE Declarations to avoid overclaim exposure. **For importers with finally liquidated entries who have not filed at the CIT:** File a Section 1581(i) action before the Federal Circuit resolves No. 26-1898 (the DOJ appeal of Judge Eaton's universal refund order). The government's stated position is that non-filing importers receive no Phase 3 refund; filing preserves standing while the appeal is pending — it is not a guarantee, but waiting is the one strategy with no upside, as Judge Eaton's own proceedings have confirmed. **On Section 122:** The 10% surcharge expires in eighteen days absent congressional extension, which is considered unlikely; CBP continues to collect it from all importers under the Federal Circuit stay. File a Section 1581(i) protest if you are not among the three named plaintiffs and wish to preserve a refund claim in the event the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's May 7 ruling. Monitor USTR's Section 301 determination schedule: if 76 determinations issue before July 24, the tariff rate and classification landscape resets materially, and landed-cost models will need immediate revision.
