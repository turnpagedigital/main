### Power Block Coin (SmartFi) — Bankr. D. Utah No. 24-23041-CDP: Conversion Trajectory and Claims Pricing Signal


Power Block Coin LLC, operating under the SmartFi brand, requested conversion of its Chapter 11 case to Chapter 7 liquidation, citing administrative insolvency and failed reorganization efforts stemming from the 2022 cryptocurrency market collapse. The case is before Judge Cathleen D. Parker in the Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah under Case No. 24-bk-23041-CDP.



The conversion motion was filed November 7, 2025, with the debtor arguing that approximately $2 million in unpaid professional fees had rendered the estate "grossly administratively insolvent" and unable to confirm any reorganization plan.
 The tracked-case ground truth confirms three new docket entries filed on or before July 6, 2026 (including entries at Docket Nos. 529 and 531), suggesting active motion practice is continuing. The substantive content of those specific filings could not be independently verified through publicly available sources as of this run.

The claims universe here is unusually concentrated. 
The plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million.
 That one creditor — the Celsius litigation administrator — holds or controls more than 69% of the total claim pool by dollar value. 
Annette W. Jarvis of Greenberg Traurig appears as counsel of record for Celsius Network LLC, the Litigation Administrator and Complex Asset Recovery Manager, the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors, and the Blockchain Recovery Investment Consortium LLC
 — a degree of creditor-side consolidation that significantly constrains the debtor's negotiating posture on any plan or conversion.


The committee's filings raise concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions. A significant asset under scrutiny is a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC, an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah. The committee expressed concerns about this loan's unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property.


That $17.4 million Solara loan is the recovery-grade issue to watch. 
Additional affiliate notes include $1.07 million owed by parent company Blue Castle Holdings Inc. and $1.79 million from SmartFi Lending, both carrying 4% interest rates and maturing in 2028. The debtor noted that none of these affiliate loans were secured by any collateral.
 Unsecured affiliate loans to an insider-controlled real estate entity, post-encumbrance, squarely implicate 11 U.S.C. § 548 constructive fraudulent transfer analysis and, depending on Utah choice-of-law, UVTA § 25-6-202. A Chapter 7 trustee stepping into this case would inherit those avoidance theories. Claims buyers pricing positions in this estate must discount heavily for the pending conversion question, the depth of administrative insolvency, and the Celsius creditor's ability to direct recoveries toward its own preference and fraudulent transfer claims rather than pro rata distribution to retail claimants.


Prior to filing bankruptcy, SmartFi faced significant operational challenges when its banking institutions closed the company's accounts in December 2023. The company subsequently entered into a services agreement with parent company Blue Castle Holdings, allowing it to use Blue Castle's bank account for dollar-denominated transactions.
 That cash management arrangement — debtor funds flowing through an insider affiliate's account post-bank closure — is itself a potential preference or improper transfer target under § 547 and § 548, and any Chapter 7 trustee will scrutinize the pre-petition period closely.

### BLMIS / Madoff — Bankr. S.D.N.Y. No. 08-01789 (LGB), SIPA Trustee Irving H. Picard: Seventeenth Distribution Status


The SIPA Trustee commenced the seventeenth pro rata interim distribution on February 27, 2026, distributing more than $253 million to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims.
 
Including the seventeenth distribution, the aggregate amount sent to eligible BLMIS customers totals nearly $15.38 billion. The seventeenth distribution represents 1.302% of each allowed claim and will be paid on claims relating to 768 accounts. Approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim will have been paid when combined with the prior sixteen distributions, unless that claim has been fully satisfied.



As of June 26, 2026, the SIPA Trustee has recovered or reached agreements to recover approximately $15.456 billion.
 
As of June 26, 2026, SIPC has committed approximately $850.9 million to the BLMIS liquidation.
 
SIPC has received $272.4 million in reimbursement from the Customer Fund for advances paid on fully satisfied accounts, leaving $578.5 million in SIPC advances outstanding.


The distribution velocity matters for claims pricing. 
The Madoff recovery initiative has returned approximately 75 cents on the dollar — many times the usual rate in comparable cases. The seventeenth distribution commenced February 27, 2026, totaling more than $253 million.
 At 72.848% aggregate recovery against admitted claims of approximately $20.3 billion, the remaining 27-plus cents on the dollar is the contested question: how much further litigation yield remains, and at what timeline? 
The SIPA advances, combined with seventeen interim distributions, have returned approximately $15.377 billion to BLMIS customers with allowed claims. The Trustee continues to move forward aggressively to resolve litigation and appeals that are delaying further distributions.
 For claims traders, the open questions are (a) the size of any eighteenth distribution, and (b) the timing, which has historically been annual to biennial depending on settlement flow. The February cadence has been consistent — the last six distributions all commenced in late February — which provides useful predictability for discount-rate modeling.

The fee structure in BLMIS is an outlier relative to all other Ponzi receiverships: 
no funds recovered in the Madoff Recovery Initiative are used to pay costs associated with the recovery; all trustee, legal, and accounting fees, as well as administrative expenses, are paid by SIPC.
 That insulation from fee drag is a material structural advantage for BLMIS claimants compared to, e.g., Stanford or crypto-era receiverships where receiver/counsel fees erode distributable principal.

### SEC Enforcement — Retail Fraud Working Group: Forward Signal for Receiver Pipeline


On July 7, 2026, the SEC announced the creation of the Retail Fraud Working Group, designed to strengthen the Division of Enforcement's efforts to identify and combat fraud targeting everyday investors. The working group will leverage staff and resources across the Commission to identify offering frauds, pump-and-dump schemes, market manipulation, and breaches of duties to customers by investment advisers and broker-dealers.



The working group will serve as a dedicated resource for proactive case generation and will coordinate with regulatory partners and foreign counterparts. It will be led by Kate Zoladz, Deputy Director, West, and Kim Frederick, Assistant Director, Asset Management Unit.


For recovery-desk purposes, this announcement is a pipeline signal, not an immediate docket event. New enforcement actions of any scale typically precede receiver appointments by two to six months. 
In April 2026, David Woodcock took the reins as SEC Director of Enforcement and delivered a keynote at the MFA Legal & Compliance 2026 Conference outlining a traditional enforcement agenda focused on high-impact cases involving investor harm, market integrity, and core fraud priorities — with specific focus areas including offering fraud, accounting and disclosure fraud, market manipulation, insider trading, private funds, and cross-border fraud.



Crypto and digital assets have resulted in the most significant penalties and settlements. Digital assets created many large-scale offering fraud cases because promoters could rapidly raise money from thousands of retail investors worldwide. The SEC has characterized many token sales, lending programs, and crypto investment products as securities offerings when investors contributed money expecting profits from others' efforts.
 The combination of a newly constituted Retail Fraud Working Group and a Director of Enforcement with a stated crypto-and-private-fund enforcement priority substantially increases the probability of new receiver appointments in the crypto-lending and alternative investment spaces in the next twelve to eighteen months. For claims buyers, that means the current pipeline is incomplete — the next round of early-stage claims will likely emerge from enforcement targets not yet publicly identified.

Cross-border coordination is a separate enforcement lever. 
Since the announcement of the SEC's Cross Border Task Force, the Commission has issued over a dozen orders for trading suspensions against Asia-based companies because of potential manipulation of foreign issuers' securities made to investors via social media. The thirteen trading suspensions announced in the last four months is almost twice the number of trading suspensions instituted in the three years 2022–2024 combined.
 That acceleration in cross-border activity is consistent with broader SEC rhetoric about international investor harm and points toward potential 28 U.S.C. § 1782 discovery proceedings and, in UK-adjacent matters, Norwich Pharmacal orders, as enforcement-adjacent asset tracing tools.

### Structural Note: Administrative Insolvency Risk in Crypto-Era Receiverships and Bankruptcies

The Power Block Coin conversion motion — premised on $2 million in unpaid professional fees rendering the estate administratively insolvent — illustrates a recurring structural hazard across crypto-era proceedings. Unlike BLMIS, where SIPC absorbs all professional costs, SEC-appointed receivers and Chapter 11 debtors-in-possession in crypto cases must fund operations from estate assets that are often illiquid, price-volatile, or encumbered. Fee priority claims under 11 U.S.C. § 503(b)(1) sit ahead of all prepetition general unsecured creditors; administrative insolvency effectively subordinates every retail claimant below the professional fee stack. In the Power Block Coin estate, the $2 million in unpaid fees is not large in absolute terms but is large relative to an estate whose platform was generating 
just over $50,000 in transaction fee income through September 2024, against more than $500,000 in costs during the same period.
 That burn rate is structurally determinative of conversion timing. Claims buyers entering this estate at elevated prices are taking basis risk on the administrative insolvency question resolving unfavorably.
