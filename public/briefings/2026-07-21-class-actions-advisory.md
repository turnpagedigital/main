# 💰 $1B+ Class Actions & Mass Arb | Tuesday, July 21, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's delta is singular and consequential:** 
the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the class settlement of certain infringement claims in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, requiring Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to the authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from pirate sites.
 That ruling closed one chapter of the AI copyright litigation cycle — and immediately set the terms for the next one.

---

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Entered July 21, 2026

The facts on the ground: 
Judge William Alsup has since retired, and Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín signed off on the settlement Monday. The payout will deliver $3,000 per work across an estimated 500,000 works, shared among the authors and publishers who hold rights to them.



The agreement requires Anthropic to destroy all original files of works torrented from pirate sites and any copies that originate from those torrented copies. In aggregate, this marks the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77 percent.


That 92.77% figure is the operative lens for claimants still assessing recovery math. 
Class counsel apprised the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% rate (updated to 92.77% by the final approval stage). The final claims rate vastly surpassed the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed March 19, 2026, eleven days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 The high participation rate means per-claim recovery converges on the original headline number: 
the high rate of claim means that forecasts about a bigger payout based on the March 19 report will not happen, and the actual payout will likely end up being closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.


On distribution mechanics, Anthropic is not writing a single check. 
Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved. There is currently no exact timeline, but payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest.
 Rights-holders should not assume the September installment date equals a distribution date — claims administration, any appellate exposure from objectors, and the fund-to-claimant allocation calculation all intervene.


As defined by the court, the class includes legal or beneficial copyright owners — specifically the owners of the exclusive right to reproduce copies — of any book downloaded by Anthropic through Library Genesis (LibGen) in June 2021 or from Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi) in July 2022, provided the work has an ISBN or ASIN, was registered with the Copyright Office within five years of first publication, and was registered before being downloaded by Anthropic or within three months of first publication.


On precedential value: 
Alsup found the second method (the piracy track) illegal on its own terms and said that piracy question could go to trial; Anthropic agreed to a settlement soon after to avoid trial and whatever damages a jury might have awarded. While final approval closes out this case, it does not settle the legal question industrywide because Alsup's ruling was a single district court decision, and Anthropic's decision to settle means the case will never reach an appeals court to become binding precedent.
 That is the precise gap the Hachette-Google complaint is now designed to exploit.

### II. *Hachette Book Group, Inc. et al. v. Google LLC*, No. 1:26-cv-05870 (S.D.N.Y.) — New Filing, Upstream Risk for Gemini

The ink on the *Bartz* final approval order was barely dry when the downstream litigation moved. 
The case is *Hachette Book Group, Inc. et al. v. Google LLC*, case number 1:26-cv-05870 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. Although the publishers publicly announced it on July 13 and news reports followed on July 14, the federal docket records the complaint as filed on July 10, 2026.



The group of plaintiffs — Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier, author Scott Turow, and S.C.R.I.B.E. — also alleges that Google intentionally removed or changed copyright information on these works to "conceal… that its Gemini Models were trained on stolen materials."
 This is a materially different allegation from the *Bartz* piracy theory: it introduces a 17 U.S.C. § 1202 copyright management information (CMI) removal claim layered on top of standard infringement.


The Southern District of New York, which is entirely free to reach the opposite conclusion on whether AI training constitutes transformative fair use from the California courts, is now hearing this case. That matters because the Hachette-Google complaint contains details the *Bartz* case did not: Google allegedly removed or altered copyright information on ingested works to "conceal that its Gemini Models were trained on stolen materials" — a claim that goes beyond careless data sourcing toward alleged intentional concealment.


The venue choice is deliberate and strategically significant. 
The plaintiffs filed in the Southern District of New York rather than the Northern District of California, which had handed down a run of AI-friendly rulings. It reads as a venue choice aimed at a different outcome on different precedent terrain.
 SDNY's fair use jurisprudence under the Second Circuit is distinct from the Ninth Circuit framework that governed *Bartz*.


The proposed class would include owners of registered copyrights in books carrying ISBNs, as well as journal articles identified through Digital Object Identifiers or International Standard Serial Numbers. Potential class members would need to show that Google copied their works from one of its services, obtained them through web scraping, or used them in connection with Gemini training.
 The class has not been certified; no judge assignment has been publicly confirmed. No Google response has been filed as of this advisory.


The plaintiffs seek statutory damages or compensation based on their claimed losses and Google's alleged profits, along with legal fees and other relief available under federal copyright law.
 Statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) — up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement — applied across a class of millions of registered works would, in theory, produce exposure orders of magnitude beyond the *Bartz* settlement. That number is theoretical; no damages discovery has occurred.


Separately, the same group of publishers sued Meta in May 2026 over allegations involving its Llama models. Google is also facing a separate consolidated case, *In re Google Generative AI Copyright Litigation*, in Northern California.
 The Hachette-Google New York action is thus one node in a multi-front campaign by institutional publishers against every major LLM developer simultaneously.

### III. *In re: Fidelity Investments Data Breach Litigation*, No. 1:24-CV-12601-LTS (D. Mass.) — Claims Deadline: July 27, 2026

This settlement falls below the $1B threshold tracked here as a primary anchor but is included because the claims deadline is imminent and the class pool is larger than initially reported. 
A settlement has been reached with FMR LLC d/b/a Fidelity Investments and Fidelity Brokerage Services LLC in a class action lawsuit relating to an incident between August 17 and August 19, 2024, where a third party accessed and obtained certain information without authorization. The lawsuit is *In re: Fidelity Investments Data Breach Litigation*, Case No. 1:24-CV-12601-LTS, pending in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts.



The court heard final approval of the settlement on July 9, 2026.
 
While initial reports said that 77,099 customers were impacted, the settlement acknowledges 155,000 individuals or joint accountholders.
 
Those affected have until July 27, 2026 to submit a claim for payment of up to $5,000.


Structure: 
Fidelity has agreed to establish a $2.5 million settlement fund. It provides $100 cash payments to eligible claimants, subject to the number of claims filed. California residents can receive an additional $50 payment. Eligible customers impacted by the breach can also receive reimbursement for documented losses and fees up to $5,000.
 
Class members who do not file a claim by July 27, 2026 will not receive benefits or payments and will give up the right to be part of another lawsuit related to the legal claims in this case.
 This is a hard cutoff.

### IV. Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Campaign Posture Update

The *Bartz* final approval and *Hachette v. Google* filing are not the only Google exposure events in the ecosystem this week. The Keller Postman mass arbitration campaign against Google on advertiser overcharge claims — announced May 11, 2026 — continues to build its claimant pool.


Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade. Experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs, creating the potential for substantial recovery under federal antitrust law.


The damages framing: 
potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law.
 
The $218 billion figure is an estimate, not a filed demand amount. In mass arbitration, the leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger. The scale of the estimate signals that the advertiser claimant pool is potentially enormous: every business that purchased Google search ads or display advertising during the monopoly period at inflated prices is a potential claimant.


Mechanics: 
Google's advertiser contracts require mandatory arbitration, blocking court access and funneling claims into exactly the kind of mass arbitration process that has cost companies billions in prior campaigns.
 
The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most of the group legal proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.


The remedies backdrop matters for settlement pressure. 
Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad tech case is expected in 2026; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines. Closing arguments concluded in the remedies phase in November 2025; Judge Brinkema confirmed she is actively drafting her remedies opinion.
 A structural divestiture order in *United States v. Google* (E.D. Va.) would substantially increase Google's incentive to settle the mass arbitration campaign rather than fight simultaneously on multiple fronts. Watch for that ruling as the primary settlement catalyst.

Google updated its Ads Terms of Service effective July 1, 2026 — 
for the first time since 2018, including arbitration updates and liability changes.
 
The update came in the wake of Keller Postman's May 2026 mass arbitration effort targeting $728 billion in U.S. ad spend over the period after 2016. Google described the update as being proactive about settling things with advertisers.
 Advertisers who accepted the July 1 ToS update without opting out of its arbitration provisions within the contractually-specified window should assess their standing in the Keller Postman campaign immediately.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders whose registered works were downloaded by Anthropic from LibGen (June 2021) or PiLiMi (July 2022) and who have not yet confirmed their claim status with the Anthropic Copyright Settlement Administrator should do so now — the fund is fully capitalized by installment and distribution begins in fall 2026, but unclaimed shares do not revert to claimants who filed late; the structure is non-reversionary — (__[Anthropic Copyright Settlement Administrator](https://www.anthropiccopyrightsettle.com/)__). Publishers and authors with works also supplied to Google Books, Google Play Books, or Google Scholar for snippet or ebook purposes should evaluate their potential class membership in *Hachette Book Group, Inc. et al. v. Google LLC*, No. 1:26-cv-05870 (S.D.N.Y., filed July 10, 2026), particularly given the CMI-removal claim under 17 U.S.C. § 1202, which carries its own damages floor and does not require proof of market harm in the same way fair use analysis does. Any business that purchased Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016 and accepted the revised Google Ads ToS effective July 1, 2026 without opting out of its arbitration provisions should consult with antitrust counsel on their standing in the Keller Postman mass arbitration campaign before the state-law filing deadlines begin to close — (__[Keller Postman Google Ads Claim](https://www.kellerpostman.com/cases/google-ads-overcharges/)__). Finally, Fidelity Investments data breach class members have a hard deadline of **July 27, 2026** to file claims in *In re: Fidelity Investments Data Breach Litigation*, No. 1:24-CV-12601-LTS (D. Mass.) — (__[Fidelity Data Settlement](https://www.fidelitydatasettlement.com/)__) — after which all relief is forfeited and future claims are barred.
