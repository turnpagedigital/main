### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Fee Structure, Claims Rate, and Distribution Mechanics

The case before Judge Martínez-Olguín represents the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history. 
Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion into a settlement fund; class counsel (Susman Godfrey's Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman) are seeking 25% of the fund — $375 million — plus administrative costs to JND Legal Administration and $50,000 service awards per named plaintiff ($150,000 total for the three class representatives).


The claims rate is the material variable for per-work recovery. 
Class counsel reported to the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the motion for final approval filed March 19, 2026, eleven days before the claims deadline.
 At the May 14 hearing, 
class counsel Justin Nelson represented that the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100, with the class reaching a 92.77% participation rate for works on the works list.
 The practical implication: the high claims density compresses per-work recovery toward the original $3,000 floor. 
The high rate of claim means that earlier forecasts projecting a larger payout based on the March 19 report of 54% claims participation will not materialize.



Anthropic will fund the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The staggered payment structure is material for distribution timing: rights-holders will not receive the full fund at once. 
Per the settlement agreement, Anthropic's third scheduled payment of $450 million is due September 25, 2026, twelve months after preliminary approval, with a second wave of distribution checks calendared for October 9, 2026; the fourth payment of $450 million follows September 25, 2027.


The scope caveat bears repeating for any party treating Bartz as a broad AI-training precedent. 
The settlement is not about AI training: in June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of books to train its models was fair use and that Anthropic was entitled to scan lawfully acquired books, and nothing in the settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
What the court left unresolved — and what the settlement resolves — is Anthropic's potential liability for downloading and storing files from shadow libraries LibGen and PiLiMi.
 
For many authors, the litigation is shifting rather than ending: dozens of AI copyright suits remain at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, and Adobe.


The objector campaign at the May 14 hearing was narrow. 
Seven objectors each spoke for two minutes, with attorney James Bartolomei given five minutes to address the court on behalf of several objectors.
 
Attorneys told the court the settlement claims rate had grown to nearly 93%, while the opt-out and objection rates were described as "minuscule," a strong sign the deal meets the standard of being fair, reasonable, and adequate.
 The court's post-hearing order — setting a May 21 supplemental-brief deadline and expressly declining further submissions from objectors — is consistent with a judge closing the record rather than reopening it. The outstanding question is timing, not outcome.

The Bartz settlement does not set binding precedent on the fair use question but does set a market anchor: 
the Bartz settlement has given plaintiffs' counsel a roadmap, and most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies now allege that defendants downloaded from shadow libraries, attempting to skip the fair use debate on training and proceed directly on the piracy theory.

### In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation, MDL 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.) — Distribution Now Active, Tier 2 Lagging

The BCBS subscriber settlement crossed its most critical operational threshold last month. 
The initial distribution of payments to damages class members with valid claims began on May 11, 2026.
 
The case is *In re: Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL 2406, Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP, N.D. Ala. — a $2.67 billion gross settlement (approximately $1.9 billion net after fees and costs), one of the largest antitrust settlements in U.S. healthcare history.



Class counsel filed the motion for distribution of the net settlement fund on April 23, 2026; Judge Manasco approved the distribution order on April 24, 2026, with a target start date of May 11, 2026.
 The mechanics: 
the settlement administrator distributed funds on a pro rata basis proportional to each claimant's recognized claim; claimants below the $5 de minimis threshold receive no initial distribution; claimants below $200 receive the full amount in the first round; and claimants at or above $200 receive 90% now, with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution.


The Tier structure is material: 
Tier 2 (employee) notification emails began June 1 for the first batch of approximately 15% of validated Tier 2 claims, with payments arriving four to eight weeks after notification; Tier 3 (employer) payments have not yet started.
 
BCBS settlement payments are still being distributed in June 2026, in waves rather than on a single day.
 The residual and second-distribution mechanics remain live; 
the case was filed in 2013, preliminarily approved November 2020, finally approved August 2022, and held up by appellate review through October 2024 before claim allocation, fraud screening, and payment-method processing cleared the way for distribution.
 Claimants on the Tier 3 employer track should not expect receipts until late Q3 at the earliest given how far back the queue sits.

### Keller Postman v. Google — Mass Arbitration Campaign (AAA), Est. $218B Aggregate Claim

The most structurally significant mass arbitration development in the period is Keller Postman's formal launch of Google advertiser claims. 
Keller Postman, a Chicago-based litigation firm, announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem.


The liability predicate is already established in two separate courts. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) ruled in favor of the U.S.
 government on Google's antitrust liability — the search monopoly (D.C.) and the ad tech monopoly (E.D. Va.) respectively. 
Due to contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, these claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation; Keller Postman is coordinating thousands of individual claims on behalf of businesses nationwide.



The $218 billion aggregate figure is based on calculations from an economist the firm has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly.
 The FAA mechanics here are favorable to claimants in a way the classic consumer-facing arbitration clause was not: unlike consumer disputes where defendants weaponized *AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion* to avoid class exposure, this campaign inverts the structure — 
for major advertisers with hundreds of millions of dollars in Google ad spend per year, anonymous mass arbitration allows large brands to pursue settlements without publicly taking an adversarial posture toward a critical media partner.
 Google's counter-options are constrained: 
retaliating against an advertiser for pursuing an antitrust-related settlement would only invite further claims regarding monopoly power.


The damages ceiling under Clayton Act § 4 is treble: 
potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade; experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs.
 The $218 billion figure is the pre-treble economist estimate. Applied treble damages would, in theory, reach into the $650 billion range — a figure that will never be paid but anchors settlement leverage considerably. The remedies ruling from Judge Brinkema in the ad tech case remains pending, which will determine how much operational disruption accompanies the damages exposure and may accelerate Google's appetite to resolve private claims before appellate proceedings on the remedies order run their course.


As of May 11, 2026, Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines, with Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling still expected.
 Statutes of limitations vary by state and by antitrust theory; businesses that purchased Google ads on or after August 2016 are within the window. 
In addition to representing private claimants in arbitration, Keller Postman also represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel case.

### Downstream Precedent Watch — Shadow Library Theory and Next-Wave AI Copyright Actions

The Bartz settlement's most durable effect may be its impact on litigation posture in cases still in early stages. 
As observers have noted, law firms including Susman Godfrey have developed what one commentator calls "The Shadow Library Strategy" — attempting to bypass the AI training fair use debate, on which judges have so far been skeptical of plaintiffs, and proceeding directly on the piracy theory.
 The Bartz resolution at $1.5 billion on a piracy-only theory — without any ruling on training-based liability — gives the strategy both a dollar benchmark and a structural template. Remaining defendants (OpenAI, Meta, Apple) will face class counsel armed with a proven settlement value and a claims-administration playbook. Watch for motions to certify piracy-specific subclasses in those cases on an accelerated schedule now that the Bartz Works List methodology has been tested and a court has found it workable.
