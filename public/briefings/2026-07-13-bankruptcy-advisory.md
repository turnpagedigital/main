pursuant to the Stalking Horse APA dated June 12, 2026, SNBR Inc., an affiliate of Sleep Country Canada Inc., has committed to acquire substantially all of the Company's assets for $415 million in cash plus the assumption of certain liabilities.


The capital structure context matters for recovery analysis. 
Substantially all of the Company's debt — approximately $672.5 million in aggregate principal as of the petition date — is in default and accelerated, but subject to the automatic stay under the Bankruptcy Code.
 At the stalking horse floor, the bid covers roughly 62 cents on the dollar of that funded debt. 
The bid is well short of the full payment the prepetition milestones contemplated. As of April 30, 2026, the debtors reported consolidated assets of roughly $642.3 million against consolidated debts of roughly $1.281 billion, a negative net worth near $639 million.
 
The filing indicates that there will be funds available for distribution to unsecured creditors
 — though the size of that pool depends entirely on whether today's auction clears a price meaningfully above $415 million.


The case was compounded by the Company's pandemic-era overexpansion of its store footprint, manufacturing capabilities, and debt levels, which heightened leverage and impaired its ability to absorb prolonged headwinds, and by the April 2025 wave of U.S. tariffs — including IEEPA-based levies on certain direct-import products, followed by a February 2026 U.S. Supreme Court ruling that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs — leaving the Company to navigate continuing regulatory uncertainty.
 That IEEPA ruling downstream: 
management monetized liquidity through an IEEPA tariff-refund claim sale as part of its pre-petition capital actions.

 — the day before the sale hearing.

 Landlords in particular face a narrow window: any party that filed a sale objection by the July 10 deadline and wants to preserve it post-auction must file a supplemental objection by 4:00 p.m. tomorrow. 
The case is docketed at 1:26-bk-11399 in the New York Southern Bankruptcy Court, filed June 12, 2026.
 Debtor counsel is Brian M. Resnick of Davis Polk & Wardwell LLP. 
Claims administration is through Kroll.


The bid process was compressed but well-telegraphed. 
The filing aims to effectuate a going-concern sale of substantially all of the Debtors' assets under section 363, backstopped by a stalking horse bid from SNBR Inc., an affiliate of Sleep Country Canada Inc., supported by the lenders and agent under the Company's Prepetition Secured Credit Facilities.

 The question for distressed investors: whether any of those five preliminary bidders converted to a qualified competing bid by the July 8 deadline, and whether today's auction is contested. ### BlockFills (Reliz Technology Group Holdings): Post-Sale Plan Track Now Operative; Bar Date September 11


A Delaware bankruptcy judge on June 16, 2026 agreed to approve the $3.25 million sale of cryptocurrency financial technology firm BlockFills to a Belgian digital asset investment group, as BlockFills prepares for a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.
 That confirmation track is now the operative path in *In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.*, Case No. 26-10371 (TMH), Bankr. D. Del., before Judge Thomas M. Horan.

The sale itself is modest against the liability stack. 
According to the bankruptcy filing, Brussels-based Keyrock SA agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property.
 
Reliz Technology Group Holdings reports $50 million to $100 million in assets and $100 million to $500 million in liabilities, with the filing indicating that there will be funds available for distribution to unsecured creditors.
 
The Debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations.


The critical customer-property question — whether digital assets held by BlockFills for institutional clients constitute estate property — has not been publicly resolved in a written opinion, but the customer-led reorganization framework established in the pre-negotiated term sheet suggests the debtors and ad hoc customer group reached a negotiated accommodation rather than litigated resolution. 
The filing is supported by a pre-negotiated term sheet with an Ad Hoc Group of the company's largest customers that provides the framework for a reorganization plan centered on the creation of a new operating entity and a liquidating trust.
 That structure — NewCo plus liquidating trust — is now being implemented post-sale.


The governmental bar date is set for September 11, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET.
 Institutional creditors with unliquidated claims (including counterparties in the Babel Finance and AEXA-related exposure chains) must file by that date or risk disallowance. 
The company's liquidity crisis stemmed from cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius, compounded by stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials.


The docket logged 14 new entries in the 72 hours through July 10 (per the CourtListener feed, Dkt. entries 510–515 filed July 10) — activity volume consistent with plan confirmation preparation, though the specific filings were not described in the ground truth feed. Distressed investors considering claim purchases should note: 
the firm reported trading volume exceeding $60 billion in 2025, a 28% increase from the prior year, and served roughly 2,000 institutional clients
 — the commercial value that drove Keyrock's interest, and that anchors the NewCo's going-concern value as the basis for any recovery waterfall above the $3.25 million sale price. The customer-property characterization in the plan disclosure statement deserves close scrutiny.

### Structural Issue Watching: Customer Property in Crypto Bankruptcies — Delaware's Unresolved Question

The BlockFills sale approval without a published ruling on customer-property characterization leaves a gap in the Delaware framework that practitioners are watching. The Third Circuit has not issued a definitive opinion on whether commingled customer digital assets in a brokerage/lending hybrid constitute estate property under 11 U.S.C. § 541 — a question resolved in different factual contexts (and less favorably for customers) in the FTX and Celsius proceedings in the Southern District of New York. The negotiated NewCo/liquidating trust structure in BlockFills appears to have sidestepped a direct ruling, which means Judge Horan's docket will not produce the circuit-level precedent the crypto insolvency bar needs. Distressed investors should monitor whether the plan confirmation order contains any findings on that question — even as dicta, findings in a contested confirmation could be persuasive in subsequent cases.

The broader pattern: the three largest institutional crypto insolvencies resolved through negotiated customer settlements rather than litigated property determinations (FTX, Celsius, BlockFills). That consensus-building approach speeds resolution for current creditors but forfeits the clarity that would reduce risk premiums in future distressed crypto trading firm investments. Until a circuit court addresses the question, the customer-property risk persists as a material pricing factor in distressed crypto claim markets.

### Macro Context: 363 Sale Mechanics Under Scrutiny in Compressed-Timeline Cases

The Sleep Number timeline — petition to auction in 31 days, with a 14-week prepetition marketing process — is representative of the trend toward pre-packaged or pre-marketed 363 cases where lender support reduces the need for a lengthy postpetition marketing window. That compression creates predictable tension with unsecured creditor committee formation and effectiveness. 
The expedited asset sale is anchored by a $415 million stalking horse bid, financed by a self-priming lender DIP, drawn against $672.5 million in secured debt, with a same-day objection from the U.S. Trustee already on the docket at filing.
 A self-priming DIP structure — where the prepetition lenders roll their existing facilities into the DIP rather than bringing in new money — concentrates control in the secured lender group and limits the UCC's ability to negotiate adequate protection terms or challenge the DIP order.


A new chief executive arrived in April 2025, the company took out roughly $136 million in operating costs relative to 2024, suspended its 401(k) match in October 2025, launched a turnaround strategy in November 2025, and rolled out a redesigned mattress portfolio in March 2026.
 That operational history matters for fraudulent transfer analysis: the prepetition restructuring actions — including the IEEPA tariff-refund claim sale — warrant review by any UCC or trustee examining whether assets were monetized at full value.


When the secured lenders advanced an additional $25 million in late April 2026, they conditioned it on milestones requiring a strategic transaction that would pay their prepetition obligations in full. That expectation was not met. The bid that emerged covers roughly 62 cents on the dollar of the funded debt.
 That shortfall is structurally relevant: lenders who negotiated for a full-payoff milestone and received a 62-cent outcome will have reduced incentive to take undue risk in future forbearance negotiations, which will push the next wave of retail/consumer-facing debtors to seek chapter 11 relief earlier and with less prepetition liquidity headroom.
