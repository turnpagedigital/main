### Sleep Number — *In re Sleep Number Corporation, et al.*, No. 26-11399 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., J. Kyu Young Paek)

The sale process has now run through its court-ordered milestones. 
Sleep Number and four affiliated debtors initiated a voluntary Chapter 11 sale process in the Southern District of New York; the cases are jointly administered under Case No. 26-11399 before the Honorable Kyu Young Paek.
 
The case is structured as an expedited § 363 sale of substantially all assets, built around a stalking horse bid from SNBR Inc. — an affiliate of Sleep Country Canada Inc. — at $415 million in cash plus the assumption of certain liabilities.
 
The company entered bankruptcy with $672 million in debt, attributing the filing to tariffs and inflation.


The DIP package was the controlling constraint throughout. 
A group of Sleep Number's existing lenders committed up to $65 million in new money and up to $195 million via a roll-up of prepetition debt.
 
The Bankruptcy Court entered the interim DIP order on or around the filing date, with the final hearing scheduled for July 9, 2026.
 
Sleep Number's obligations under the DIP Loans and Roll-Up Loans are guaranteed by each subsidiary, and the DIP claims carry superpriority administrative expense claim status secured by a perfected priming senior security interest on prepetition collateral.


The creditor-investigation window was structurally narrow. 
Any official committee formed after the petition date immediately ran into a Challenge Deadline that effectively terminated when the sale hearing began on July 15 — only 33 days into the case — and the investigation budget was capped at $100,000 against more than $672.5 million in prepetition secured obligations.
 
The case presents what Stretto has characterized as "a $257.5 million shortfall" — the gap between the $415 million stalking horse bid and the secured debt load.
 That gap is the operative reality for any recovery analysis: general unsecured creditors and lower-priority claimants are structurally underwater unless the sale proceeds clear secured claims in full and generate surplus, or the debtors' estates retain litigation or avoidance claims of material value.


On July 2, 2026, the Bankruptcy Court entered an order approving the bidding procedures; the bid deadline was July 8, the auction (if necessary) was July 13, and the sale hearing was July 15, all at 10:00 a.m. ET.
 
The sale was required to close by July 31.
 As of today, July 20, the sale hearing has concluded and the closing window is actively running. The 11 new docket entries filed between July 16 and July 18 — the highest single-week activity in this case since first-day proceedings — most likely include the sale approval order and associated lease designation filings, though the specific content of those entries is not confirmed in the verified record before us. Practitioners and distressed investors should pull the current docket directly to confirm sale order entry and any objections that survived to hearing.

One structural feature that carries forward into the post-closing period: 
the buyer's real decisions on the surviving lease portfolio come during a Designation Rights Period running up to 60 days after closing — extended by agreement — during which it formally designates each unassumed lease for assumption or rejection; any lease not designated by the deadline is automatically rejected without further court order, and with closing targeted by July 31, that window could extend into late September 2026.
 
On assumed leases, prepetition cure costs are capped at $8 million in the aggregate, with any excess reducing the purchase price dollar-for-dollar.
 Landlords with stores in the Sleep Country footprint — 572 U.S. locations at filing — should be calendaring the Designation Rights Period deadline now.


Before filing, Sleep Number tried to free up additional cash flow by refinancing debt, closing stores, and notably, selling its right to refunds under 2025 tariffs that were invalidated by the U.S. Supreme Court
 — a detail that connects this case directly to the broader IEEPA tariff-refund landscape. The amount received for those refund rights was not disclosed in court filings.

### BlockFills — *In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.*, No. 26-10371 (TMH) (Bankr. D. Del., J. Thomas M. Horan)

The § 363 asset sale has closed. The case is now in the plan-confirmation phase. 
A Delaware bankruptcy judge agreed on June 16, 2026 to approve the $3.25 million sale of BlockFills' assets to a Belgian digital asset investment group as the debtors prepared for a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.
 
A court hearing to approve the sale was held June 16, and the transaction has now closed following court and regulatory approval.



Keyrock SA agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million, assuming "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property; Keyrock was declared the Successful Bidder per the official Bankruptcy Court document filed May 26, 2026.
 
Reliz Technology Group Holdings reported $50 million to $100 million in assets and $100 million to $500 million in liabilities as of the petition date.
 
The debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations.
 The arithmetic is stark: $3.25 million in sale proceeds — even supplemented by any liquidating trust recoveries — against $145 million in general unsecured claims yields, at most, low-single-digit cents on the dollar absent meaningful avoidance recoveries or estate litigation.


Final regulatory clearances for the transaction were still pending as of mid-July 2026
 — a detail flagged as non-trivial in the current U.S. regulatory environment. Specifically, 
the deal expands Keyrock's regulatory footprint to include a CIMA-registered entity in the Cayman Islands and a proposed FCA-authorized entity in the U.K., the latter still subject to regulatory approval.
 Creditors whose recoveries depend on timely plan consummation should watch for any regulatory-approval condition precedent that could delay or modify the closing mechanics.

The open creditor-property question has not resolved. 
The central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remains contested
 — and that determination will drive the waterfall. If customer assets are found to be estate property, they pool with other assets and flow through the priority scheme under 11 U.S.C. § 726 or the plan's distribution provisions. If they are customer property held in trust, they may be distributable outside the estate waterfall entirely, as was eventually determined in parts of the *Celsius* and *FTX* proceedings. The plan filed April 7, 2026 (Docket No. 128) provides the framework, and the confirmation hearing is set — per the Veritaglobal case calendar — for 
September 11, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET.


The one new docket filing on July 17 — the most recent entry in the 72-hour window per the ground-truth data pull — carries no description in the available record. Given the case posture (post-sale, pre-confirmation, plan and disclosure statement already on file), it is most likely a plan supplement, a claims bar date notice, or an amendment to the liquidating trust agreement. The content should be verified directly on CourtListener docket ID 72494188 before acting on it.

Background on how the debtors arrived here: 
BlockFills attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius, compounded by stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment.
 
On February 2, 2026, Bitcoin dropped below $80,000 for the first time since April 2025; BlockFills temporarily suspended certain deposit and withdrawal activity that day in response to liquidity pressure and customer withdrawal requests.
 The petition followed on March 15.

### Broader Creditor-Rights Landscape: Three Developments Worth Tracking

**Serta Uptier Litigation — $400M Recovery for Excluded Lenders.** 
Lenders left out of the Serta uptier deal won $400 million in a Chapter 11 suit, per a July 8, 2026 Law360 report.
 This is a materially significant creditor-rights development. The Serta uptier — an exchange transaction that moved certain lenders to first-out priority ahead of holdouts using a disputed majority-lender consent mechanism — was one of the defining liability-management exercises of the 2020–2022 vintage. A $400 million judgment for excluded lenders validates the litigation track for holdouts who refused to participate in such transactions and signals that uptier mechanics will face continued judicial scrutiny. Counsel with holdings in any pre-2024 credit facilities that experienced out-of-court exchange offers should review this decision closely.

**ModivCare Contempt Dispute.** 
White & Case and ModivCare locked horns over Chapter 11 contempt proceedings as of July 8, 2026.
 This dispute — the details of which are not available in the verified record before us — reflects the increasingly adversarial dynamic between debtor counsel and creditor constituencies in cases where DIP milestones and operational covenants are contested. Contempt proceedings in Chapter 11 are a meaningful escalation tool; they signal a breakdown in the cooperative posture courts typically expect during the administrative phase of a case.

**Freedom Forever / Financier State Flagging.** 
States flagged Freedom Forever's deal with a financier in its Chapter 11 as of July 9, 2026.
 State attorney general intervention in Chapter 11 proceedings — particularly where debtor financing arrangements carry consumer or regulatory implications — is a recurring feature of the current environment. The Freedom Forever matter is worth monitoring for the precedent it sets on state-law regulatory constraints on DIP financing terms.
