### I. SEC Retail Fraud Working Group — Enforcement Architecture Shift (July 7, 2026)


On July 7, 2026, the SEC announced the creation of a Retail Fraud Working Group designed to strengthen the Division of Enforcement's efforts to identify and combat fraud targeting everyday investors, including offering frauds, pump-and-dump schemes, market manipulation, and breaches of fiduciary duties by investment advisers and broker-dealers.
 
The Working Group will be led by Kate Zoladz, Deputy Director West, and Kim Frederick, Assistant Director of the Asset Management Unit.


The structural implication for recovery-track monitoring is direct: 
the Working Group will serve as a dedicated resource for proactive case generation and will coordinate with the Commission's regulatory partners and foreign counterparts.
 Proactive case generation, in the Division's current lexicon, means front-loading the investigation phase before charges are filed — and that means whistleblower awards are likely to accelerate as an early-signal mechanism. Under SEC Act § 21F, a whistleblower whose tip leads to a sanctions action exceeding $1 million is entitled to 10–30% of collected sanctions; historically, the gap between award and receiver appointment runs six to eighteen months. Claims investors watching for the next wave of receiver appointments should be tracking SEC whistleblower award releases with particular care over the next two quarters.


The broader enforcement posture under Chair Atkins and Acting Enforcement Director Sam Waldon — articulated at the 2026 SEC Speaks Conference — is quality over quantity, focused on bringing actions against those who "lie, cheat, and steal."
 
The Division's announced core case types include offering fraud, market manipulation, insider trading, financial accounting and disclosures, and fiduciary duty violations by investment advisers.
 Offering fraud and fiduciary-duty violations are precisely the fact patterns that produce receiver appointments and § 548 / UFTA clawback litigation. The Retail Fraud Working Group formalizes what was previously ad hoc; the institutionalization is worth noting as a forward-indicator.

For context on the recent enforcement baseline: 
SEC enforcement activity against public companies and subsidiaries continued at a slower pace in 1H FY 2026, with five actions initiated — the lowest in the first half of a fiscal year in at least 16 years.
 The new Working Group is a deliberate effort to rebuild pipeline without necessarily inflating headline case counts — consistent with Atkins' stated preference for prioritized, investor-harm-centered dockets.

### II. Power Block Coin, LLC — Bankr. D. Utah No. 24-23041 (Judge Cathleen D. Parker) — New Filings Flag

The tracked-case ground truth records three new docket entries in the window closing July 8, 2026, the most recent being entry No. 531, filed July 6. No public description is attached to any of the three entries at time of this run. The case posture as of the most recent substantively described filings remains: 
the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors filed a comprehensive liquidation plan in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah (Case No. 24-bk-23041) proposing to wind down the cryptocurrency financial services company under Chapter 11 protection; the plan was filed by Greenberg Traurig, LLP on January 30, 2025, and outlines a strategy to maximize value for creditors through the establishment of a Liquidating Trust overseen by an independent trustee.


The claim pool is large relative to the estate: 
the plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million.
 
The Committee's filing raises concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions, including a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC — an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah — the committee expressing concern about the loan's unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property.


The case has carried a judge assignment change: early docket entries (through at least Doc. 236) reflect Judge Joel T. Marker (JTM designation), while the tracked-case ground truth and the most recent publicly available filings identify the presiding judge as Judge Cathleen D. Parker. 
A prior fee application covering the previous Subchapter V trustee, Joli A. Lofstedt, was granted without objection, allowing trustee fees of $8,925.00 as Chapter 11 Administrative Claims on a final basis under 11 U.S.C. § 330.
 Fee orders of this character — routine approval of predecessor trustee fees — typically accompany an estate transitioning toward a liquidating-trust structure, consistent with the Committee's January 2025 plan.

Until the content of entries 529 and 531 is available on the public docket, the critical open items for claims investors are: (a) whether the Liquidating Trust has been formally constituted and a trustee named; (b) whether adversary proceedings targeting insider transactions — including the Solara loan and any other pre-petition cryptocurrency movements — have been filed or threatened; and (c) where the Celsius-related $133 million preference claim exposure falls in the interplay between this estate and the Celsius Network case (No. 22-10964, Bankr. S.D.N.Y.). 
The Celsius-related objecting creditors asserted over $133 million in preference claims against Power Block Coin in connection with the Celsius Network LLC bankruptcy pending in the Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York (Case No. 22-10964).
 Recovery on those claims — or a settlement — would directly affect distribution capacity in this estate.

### III. Stanford Receivership — Final Distribution in Execution, Societe Generale Settlement Remains Blocked

The Stanford Financial Group receivership (SEC v. Stanford International Bank, Ltd., et al., N.D. Tex., Dallas Division, Judge David C. Godbey) has moved decisively into its terminal phase. 
On March 9, 2026, Receiver Ralph Janvey filed with the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas the Final Distribution Schedule pursuant to the October 8, 2025 Order Approving the Final Distribution Plan; the payments listed total more than $339 million and include payments to over 14,000 claim groups, to be issued on a rolling basis.



Payments under the Final Distribution Plan began April 3, 2026.
 
During the first two months of 2026, the Receivership issued payments to eligible claimants totaling more than $100 million pursuant to Distribution Plans 1–11.
 One operational friction point is worth monitoring: check-cashing deadlines are hard stops. 
Payees must cash Final Distribution checks within 90 days of issuance; reissuance may be requested once within the same 90-day period; and funds represented by void checks not subject to a timely reissuance request revert to the Receivership Estate.
 For secondary-market claims purchasers who acquired Stanford CD claims, the operational task of ensuring checks are directed to the correct address of record — and that notifications reach claims administration in time — is non-trivial, particularly for non-U.S. claimants.

One material block persists. 
Distribution of funds from the $157 million Societe Generale settlement must await final resolution of the Antiguan Joint Liquidators' appeal.
 
In May 2026, Jaime Escalona submitted materials to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas regarding the Receiver's distribution process.
 
On June 1, 2026, the Receiver filed a response to Escalona's submission, which includes an update on the status of the Receiver's ongoing distribution efforts.
 The Escalona submission adds a minor procedural layer but is unlikely to alter the Final Distribution schedule materially. The $157 million SocGen tranche remains the primary unresolved recovery for claims investors who priced that amount into their secondary-market positions.

### IV. Madoff / Picard SIPA Liquidation — Seventeenth Distribution Running, $15.456 Billion in Aggregate Recovery

No new distribution events since the last advisory. The seventeenth distribution commenced February 27, 2026 and remains the controlling baseline. 
SIPA Trustee Irving H. Picard is distributing more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims; including the seventeenth distribution, the aggregate amount sent to eligible BLMIS customers totals nearly $15.38 billion, which includes approximately $850.9 million in advances made by SIPC.



The seventeenth interim distribution represents 1.302% of each allowed claim and will be paid on claims relating to 768 accounts; approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim will have now been paid when combined with the prior sixteen distributions, unless that claim has been fully satisfied.
 
As of June 26, 2026, the SIPA Trustee has recovered or reached agreements to recover approximately $15.456 billion.



On February 13, 2026, the SIPA Trustee and BakerHostetler filed the 50th Interim Fee Application with the Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, covering approximately 63,774.40 hours of professional and paraprofessional services billed at an average public-interest discounted rate of approximately $588.88 per hour.
 The fee applications are filed under Bankr. S.D.N.Y. No. 08-01789 before Judge Stuart M. Bernstein. For claims investors, the fee cadence (quarterly, ~$37.5 million per application at these billing rates) is a predictable drag on the Customer Fund — though under the BLMIS SIPA framework, 
no recovered funds are used to pay costs associated with the recovery; all trustee, legal, and accounting fees are paid by SIPC.
 That structural feature makes BLMIS claims among the cleanest in the secondary market from a fee-drag standpoint.

The open question for forward distribution modeling remains: what residual litigation pipeline — feeder fund defendants, offshore account clawbacks — can Picard monetize to push aggregate recovery past 73% of allowed claims toward the effective terminus. 
Picard and Sheehan have recovered 75 cents on the dollar of the stolen money
 on an all-in basis when DOJ forfeitures are included, though the BLMIS Customer Fund alone stands at the 72.848% mark. The gap between 72.848% and the 75-cent figure reflects the DOJ-recovered tranche ($4.3 billion) that flows through a separate restitution mechanism.

### V. SEC Enforcement Pipeline — Offering Fraud as the Structural Feeder Pattern

For context on what the Retail Fraud Working Group is likely to generate: 
in September 2025, the SEC sued three former Retail Ecommerce Ventures executives, alleging they raised $112 million through fraudulent offerings that operated as a Ponzi-like scheme, promising sham 25% annualized returns to revitalize REV brands including RadioShack and Pier 1 Imports.
 
In July 2025, the SEC secured — by consent — an asset freeze and other emergency relief against Georgia-based First Liberty Building & Loan, LLC and its founder in connection with an alleged Ponzi scheme that defrauded approximately 300 investors of at least $140 million; the defendants represented that investors would receive 18% returns from supposed short-term, high-interest bridge loans to businesses, but instead used incoming investor funds to pay principal and interest owed to earlier investors.


These two patterns — inflated-return real-estate debt and brand-revival equity — are the current iteration of offering fraud that routinely ends in receiver appointment. The First Liberty fact pattern (18% returns, bridge loans to businesses, Ponzi recycling) is structurally identical to cases that have generated § 548 clawback campaigns targeting net winners who received interest or principal payments in the 90-day preference window or the two-year constructive-fraud look-back. Claims investors evaluating positions in any SEC-enforcement-initiated receivership opened in the next 12–24 months should stress-test the net-winner population under both 11 U.S.C. § 548 (two-year look-back, constructive fraud) and applicable state UVTA provisions, which in many jurisdictions extend to four years under a discovery rule.
