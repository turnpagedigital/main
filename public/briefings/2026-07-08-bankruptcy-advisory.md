### Sleep Number Corporation — § 363 Bid Deadline Closes Today; Auction Monday; Second-Day Hearing Tomorrow


On June 12, 2026, Sleep Number Corporation and its subsidiaries filed voluntary chapter 11 petitions in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York.
 
The cases are jointly administered under Case No. 26-11399 by the Honorable Kyu Young Paek.
 
The company reports $642.3 million in assets against $1.3 billion in liabilities.


The near-term calendar is now at its most critical juncture. 
On July 2, 2026, Judge Paek entered an order approving bidding procedures. The resulting milestones are: Bid Deadline — July 8, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET; Sale Objection Deadline — July 10, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET; Auction (if necessary) — July 13, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET; Post-Auction Objection Deadline — July 14, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET; Sale Hearing — July 15, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET.
 
A final hearing on certain first-day motions is also scheduled for July 9, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET.


The stalking-horse position belongs to Sleep Country Canada. 
Pursuant to a Stalking Horse APA dated June 12, 2026, Sleep Country Canada committed — subject to court approval — to acquire substantially all of the company's assets for $415 million in cash plus the assumption of certain liabilities, establishing the floor for competing bids.
 
The transaction is being administered by Judge Paek and is being undertaken pursuant to § 363 of the Bankruptcy Code, with Sleep Country serving as stalking horse.


The capital structure context matters for recovery analysis. 
The SDNY court entered an interim DIP order approving DIP loans through the final hearing scheduled for July 9, 2026. Sleep Number's obligations under the DIP loans and roll-up loans are guaranteed by each subsidiary, and the DIP lenders hold superpriority administrative expense claim status secured by a perfected first-priority lien on all DIP collateral plus priming liens on prepetition collateral.
 DIP priming creates the familiar layered-priority problem for prepetition secured creditors: the adequacy of protection for prepetition lienholders subordinated by the priming grant is a threshold question at the final DIP hearing tomorrow. 
The filing indicates that there will be funds available for distribution to unsecured creditors
 — a meaningful statement given the $1.3 billion liability load, though the precise unsecured recovery waterfall will depend entirely on whether the $415 million floor holds, whether competing bids emerge today, and how the court resolves DIP-related claims.

The operational context for landlords and lease counterparties remains live. 
Sleep Number operates 572 stores and will continue operations while pursuing the expedited court-supervised sale process.
 
The company attributed the filing in part to "the unpredictable shifting of trade rules imposed by the current U.S. government, on top of an already vulnerable global supply chain."
 Lease assumption-and-cure and rejection decisions will follow the sale order; landlords with stores in the footprint should monitor the July 15 sale hearing for the assumed-and-rejected lease schedule. 
Debtors' lead restructuring counsel is Brian M. Resnick of Davis Polk & Wardwell LLP.

### BlockFills (In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc.) — Confirmation Approaching July 13; Post-Sale Posture


Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and three affiliated entities — operating under the trade name "BlockFills" — filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on March 15, 2026 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, Case No. 26-10371 (TMH).
 The presiding judge is the Honorable Thomas M. Horan. 
The debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations.


The tracked-case ground truth confirms five new docket entries as of July 6 (Dkt. Nos. 489–493), and the case is converging on its July 13 confirmation target. The sale track is now resolved: 
Keyrock SA agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some equity interests, customer lists, and proprietary technology and intellectual property, with Keyrock confirmed as the "Successful Bidder" per the official bankruptcy court document filed May 26, 2026.
 
The case has been operating under final cash-collateral authority while pursuing a dual-track restructuring built around the sale process and a plan path, with a sale hearing set for June 16, 2026, and confirmation targeted for July 13, 2026.


The central substantive tension in this case — and the one most relevant to distressed investors — has been the threshold question of estate property. 
Four debtor entities operating as BlockFills pursued a dual-track restructuring through a customer-led NewCo transaction and a competitive § 363 sale process, while the central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remained contested.
 The resolution of that question, and whether customer claims will be channeled through the § 363 sale or a liquidating trust, will govern the magnitude of unsecured recovery. 
The capital structure is concentrated in customer and trade exposure rather than traditional funded debt; the first-day record identifies roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and approximately $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.


The $3.25 million Keyrock purchase price against roughly $145 million of general unsecured claims implies a deeply distressed recovery; the operative question for claim holders is what the liquidating trust receives from the § 363 sale proceeds after satisfaction of administrative and priority claims and the Celsius secured lien. 
BlockFills reported trading volume exceeding $60 billion in 2025, a 28% increase year-over-year, and served approximately 2,000 institutional clients.
 Claim holders at the institutional level — hedge funds, asset managers, market makers — should confirm their position in the claims registry through Veritaglobal (the claims administrator at veritaglobal.net/blockfills) and monitor confirmation closely this week.

### Tehum / YesCare / Corizon Cluster — Insider Fraud Litigation Advances; Trust Payment Mechanics Under Strain

The news scan flags active Bloomberg Law coverage of insider fraud claims in the Tehum bankruptcy cluster. The underlying adversary proceeding is now well-documented on the primary docket. 
On April 27, 2026, Matthew J. Dundon (GUC Trustee), Michael Zimmerman (PI/WD Trustee), and the Post-Effective Date Debtor in the Tehum bankruptcy filed Adversary Proceeding No. 26-03138 in the Texas Bankruptcy Court. Defendants include Lefkowitz, the Lefkowitz Entities (YesCare, CHS TX, Geneva, Perigrove, Perigrove 1018, M2 HoldCo, M2 LoanCo, PharmaCorr), and six named former Corizon officers.
 
The complaint pleads sixteen counts: actual and constructive fraudulent transfer attacking both the divisional merger and approximately $31 million in prepetition cash transfers; aiding and abetting; multiple breach-of-fiduciary-duty counts; alter ego against Lefkowitz; veil piercing against Perigrove 1018; successor liability against both YesCare and CHS TX; and breach of contract on the plan and cure agreement.


The adversary proceeding is the direct product of serial settlement defaults. 
The PI/WD trustee's first annual report showed the trust had received $6.95 million of contributions from inception through December 31, 2025, had paid only $77,000 on 15 settled claims, had 323 submitted PI/WD claims in the trust process, and reported delinquent installment payments by the settling parties against plan-required funding, with a later cure agreement also defaulting by March 2026.
 The trigger mechanism for the trustee suit is structural: 
the settlement parties — YesCare and its subsidiaries including CHS TX, Geneva, Perigrove 1018, Perigrove LLC, M2 HoldCo, M2 LoanCo, and PharmaCorr — agreed to pay $50 million ($2 million on the effective date, $48 million over 30 monthly installments at 6.00% per annum). The plan releases were structured to vest only on the final payment date, and the channeling injunction terminated automatically on any payment default not cured within five business days.


The YesCare chapter 11 filing in the Middle District of Florida is the downstream consequence. 
The lead petitions — CHS FL, LLC, CHS AL, LLC, CHS TX, Inc., and YesCare Corp. — were filed May 8, 2026 in M.D. Fla. A second wave on May 13, 2026 added Corizon Health of New Mexico, LLC (Case No. 2:26-bk-01144-FMR), CHS Care NY, LLC (2:26-bk-01145-FMR), and CHS Care TX, LLC (2:26-bk-01146-FMR).
 
The First Day Declaration reports approximately $21.0 million in funded secured debt, with the sole lender identified as M2 LoanCo, LLC — an affiliate of the debtors and an insider lender. Estimated unsecured claims run to approximately $400 million, comprising trade debt and litigation exposure including the Jackson verdict and more than 100 pending matters.


For distressed investors, the Tehum cluster raises the most pointed threshold question in the advisory: insider lending by M2 LoanCo — a Lefkowitz-affiliated entity — now sits at the top of the secured waterfall in the YesCare chapter 11 while simultaneously being a named defendant in the Tehum adversary proceeding. 
The Lefkowitz insider entity web — Perigrove, Perigrove 1018, Geneva Consulting, M2 HoldCo, M2 LoanCo, and PharmaCorr — are each a Tehum settlement party, a released party, a signatory to the February 1, 2026 cure agreement consenting to Texas jurisdiction, and a named adversary defendant.
 The insider lender's secured-claim position in the M.D. Fla. proceeding is directly at risk if the Texas adversary succeeds on fraudulent transfer or equitable subordination grounds. That is the recovery variable to track.

### New Fortress Energy — English Court Sanctions $9.6 Billion Restructuring Plan; U.S. Chapter 15 Recognition Obtained

This matter is now substantially closed but warrants a final posture update as its implications for cross-border restructuring practice are immediate. 
In Re NFE Global Holdings Limited [2026] EWHC 1620 (Ch) (Cawson J, sanction judgment) and [2026] EWHC 1223 (Ch) (Hildyard J, convening judgment), the English court sanctioned two restructuring plans under Part 26A of the Companies Act 2006 that extinguished approximately US$9.6 billion of debt across the New Fortress Energy group.
 
All seven creditor classes voted in favour — six unanimously and one by 99.84% — making cross-class cram-down unnecessary.


The forum-selection holding from Cawson J is the sentence that restructuring practitioners will cite going forward: 
the court confirmed that "good forum shopping" is a legitimate basis for using an English restructuring plan where it produces a superior outcome for creditors, and described the case as an example of precisely that — the English plan was chosen because it was expected to produce a US$1.44 billion uplift versus the relevant alternative.


U.S. recognition has now followed. 
Chapter 15 recognition was granted in the United States on June 26, 2026, although Judge Glenn's detailed reasoning is awaited.
 
Under the restructuring, creditors will exchange more than $9 billion of debt for a combination of equity and new debt, with the group separated into a private "BrazilCo" and a public "New NFE."
 
The Restructuring Support Agreement was executed by 778 creditors representing 97% of in-scope liabilities and over 75% by value of each proposed creditor class. Total plan creditor consideration comprised new equity and new takeback debt instruments totaling US$971 million.


The practical significance for distressed investors monitoring large cross-border exposures: where a company with meaningful U.S. creditors elects the English Part 26A plan route, U.S. creditors face the recognition mechanics of Chapter 15 as the operative check on their rights. 
The Cawson J judgment offered notable guidance on class constitution, third-party releases, conditions precedent, and international effectiveness
 — a body of doctrine now directly applicable in any subsequent case where a non-U.S. restructuring plan with American creditor exposure seeks U.S. recognition.
