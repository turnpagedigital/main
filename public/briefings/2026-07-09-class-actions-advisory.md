### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Still Pending; Supplemental Briefing on Late Opt-Outs Closes the Record

The headline fact as of this writing: 
final approval of the $1.5 billion settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* remains pending before the Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín (N.D. Cal.).
 The May 14 fairness hearing proceeded and concluded, but the court declined to sign an approval order on the day. 
After a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in the San Francisco courtroom, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement—but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expected final approval to follow relatively quickly.


The core fee arithmetic shifted materially before the hearing. 
Class Counsel urged final approval of the $1.5 billion settlement, along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 The post-hearing record narrowed to a discrete legal question. 
In the supplemental filing ordered after the fairness hearing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any record supporting relief and because other rightsholders have not consented.


The claims posture at the hearing was exceptional. 
Lead attorney Justin Nelson reported that the opt-in rate had inched up from 91.3% the prior month to 92.77%.
 That figure derives from the April 16 claims update: 
Class Counsel reported claims for 440,490 of the 482,460 works on the Works List (Dkt. 643), up from the 54% reported in the March 19 motion for final approval.
 The per-work implied recovery at the current claims denominator is approximately $3,000–$3,100. 
There was no indication at the hearing that if the settlement is approved, the per-work payout of approximately $3,000 to $3,100 would change.


Four structural objections remain live in the record and could resurface on appeal even after approval. First, the intraclass publisher/author split: 
the distribution plan was alleged to systematically favor publishers over authors through the claims-made process and arbitration mechanism, with publishers potentially claiming roughly 50% or more of the total settlement amount—a profound departure from an author-centric lawsuit.
 Second, the group-registration undercounting issue: 
claimant Pinder, author of 40+ independently published novels registered under a single group copyright registration, receives one share under the settlement—for the same number of distinct books as an author who registered each individually would receive 40 shares.
 Third, the opt-out notice deficiency: 
objector Chakanga raised a due process argument that thousands of pages of sealed summary judgment records were not unsealed until January 21, 2026—eight days before the original opt-out deadline—and invoked the Ninth Circuit's decision in *Avery v. TEKsystems* (Jan. 28, 2026) as authority for the proposition that compressed timing combined with a sealed judicial record is a substantive defect.
 Fourth, the education-works split: 
objector James Sills (Dkt. 600) reported that Elsevier proposed to take 88% of the per-work settlement amount for one of his books; the settlement's default split for non-education works is 50/50, but education works are subject to negotiation and arbitration governed by the Special Master process.


For rights-holders still tracking this case: 
per the original administration timeline, the settlement administrator was to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later—both dates now contingent on when the final approval order actually issues.
 The CourtListener docket (ID 69058235) was last updated July 2, 2026; the most recent substantive filing date in the tracked-case ground truth is July 1, 2026, with zero new entries in the prior 72 hours, confirming the court has not yet entered the final approval order. Distribution cannot commence until that order is entered and the appeal window runs (or is waived).

### In re: Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation, MDL 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.) — $2.67B Distribution in Active Mid-Wave Rollout

The BCBS distribution is the most operationally time-sensitive matter on the tracker this week. 
The initial round of payments from the $2.67 billion settlement fund began distribution in May 2026, approximately 13 years after cases were consolidated as MDL 2406 in 2013 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama, the settlement administrator confirmed.



On April 23, 2026, subscriber plaintiffs filed a motion in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama asking the court to approve a distribution plan for nearly 7.8 million claimants.
 
The initial distribution of payments to Damages Class Members with valid claims began starting May 11, 2026.
 Payment mechanics are tiered: 
claimants with a calculated distribution of $200 or more receive 90% in the initial round, with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution; funds are issued by check, PayPal, Venmo, or electronic debit card.


Net fund math: 
after attorneys' fees of approximately $667 million and administrative costs are deducted, the remaining net fund available for direct distribution totals roughly $1.9 billion.
 Average per-claimant recovery is modest—
payouts average $333; only those who filed by the November 2021 deadline are eligible.
 Reported claimant receipts are running in the range of several hundred dollars; at least one reported claimant in Washington State reported receiving $815.47 on June 2, 2026. 
Estimated total Tier-1 wave completion is targeted by end of July 2026.


The substantive case: 
the class action, consolidated as *In re: Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation* MDL 2406, accused more than 35 independent BCBS insurance plans of violating the Sherman Antitrust Act by agreeing to divide the United States into non-competing geographic territories, with plaintiffs alleging these agreements inflated premiums and reduced coverage options for consumers and employers nationwide.
 The settlement—reached October 2020, finally approved in the Northern District of Alabama—was held up for years by objector appeals to the Eleventh Circuit before distribution could commence.

For enrolled claimants: the claims filing deadline is long past. If you filed a valid claim before November 5, 2021, your payment is either in queue or already disbursed. Check the administrator portal (bcbssettlement.com, Claim Number or Unique ID required). 
Subclass appeals, batch-processing delays, and IRS-reporting holds all continue to shift actual payment dates for affected claimants.

### Keller Postman / Google Mass Arbitration — $218B Claimed; Advertiser Claimant Enrollment Active

This is the structurally novel development in the mass-arbitration space and warrants extended treatment. 
Keller Postman, a Chicago-based litigation firm, announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings that found the company engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem.


The legal foundation rests on two separate liability findings, not pending allegations. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 The why-arbitration question has a direct answer: 
due to contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, these claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation; Keller Postman is coordinating thousands of individual claims on behalf of businesses nationwide, allowing each claimant to pursue recovery based on its own advertising spend.


The claimed pool: 
potential claims are estimated at $218 billion or more—the largest mass arbitration campaign ever attempted by dollar value.
 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist retained by the firm, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly.
 Per-claimant recovery math under Clayton Act § 4 treble-damages: 
estimated overcharge is 5–10% of ad spend, with treble damages bringing potential recovery to up to 30% of total Google Ads spend over the period; a company with $100 million in Google Ads spend since 2016 could have a claim worth up to $30 million.


Eligible period and claimant universe: 
the firm is targeting companies of all sizes—from local businesses to large enterprises—that spent money on Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016.
 
The firm cautions that deadlines vary by state and that earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.


One structural feature elevates this campaign above prior mass-arbitration analogues: 
the Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most prior group legal proceedings have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.
 Keller Postman also occupies a parallel governmental role: 
in addition to representing private claimants in the arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state AGs in a parallel case.


The FAA mechanics are central to the leverage calculus. 
Google's advertiser contracts require mandatory arbitration, blocking court access and funneling claims into exactly the kind of mass arbitration process that has cost companies billions in prior campaigns.
 Ashley Keller's track record at scale is relevant here: 
prior campaigns include the Amazon Alexa campaign that prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands, and the TurboTax campaign that ran parallel to FTC enforcement and resolved in a confidential settlement alongside a $141 million FTC fund.


Claim volume disclosure from Keller himself: 
Keller told Bloomberg in April 2026 that he had already signed up a "significant number" of advertisers and expected to file the first claims that week.
 Given the private nature of arbitration proceedings, the actual filed-claim count is not in the public record.

### Apple App Store Injunction-Violation Class Action — Hagens Berman / N.D. Cal., Motion to Dismiss Filed July 6

A discrete but important appellate-posture development landed this week in the Apple App Store litigation. 
On July 6, 2026, Apple urged a California federal court to toss, or at least pause, a proposed class action that seeks payback of profits it allegedly received in violation of an injunction blocking prohibitions on developers steering customers to alternative purchasing mechanisms, saying a prior settlement released the plaintiff developers' claims.
 The Hagens Berman-led complaint alleges Apple neutralized the *Epic Games* injunction through "scare screens" and economic penalties on linked payment systems. 
Apple has been held in contempt, the lawsuit states, and Apple penalized developers who sought to use linked payments with fees that rendered it economically non-viable.
 Apple's release-based defense, now fully briefed as a motion to dismiss or stay, is the threshold question; resolution will determine whether the injunction-profit disgorgement theory survives to class certification.
