## Analysis & Developments

### Today's Delta: The Refund Pipeline Bifurcates, Section 301 Approaches the Wire, and July 24 Looms

Three forces are converging this weekend. First, the government's June 2, 2026 appeal of Judge Eaton's universal-refund orders has split the IEEPA reimbursement pipeline into two tracks — one moving, one frozen — and CBP's own position on final-liquidated entries has hardened in a way that directly threatens the largest-dollar claims. Second, the Federal Circuit's June 11 stay in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805, means the Section 122 surcharge continues to accrue for all importers through its statutory expiration date of July 24, 2026 (19 U.S.C. § 1330 note / Trade Act of 1974, § 122), with the Federal Circuit now signaling it found the government's merits position colorable rather than frivolous — a data point refund counsel should not dismiss. Third, USTR's forced-labor Section 301 proceedings are tracking directly to that same July 24 deadline, with public hearings opening July 7 and proposed tariffs of 10–12.5% on 60 economies already on the table. That sequencing is not a coincidence; the administration designed it to eliminate any tariff gap. All three tracks materially affect whether, and how much, importers recover.

### I. IEEPA Refund Pipeline: Phase 2 Imminent, Final-Entry Appeals Escalate

The controlling decisions remain *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 607 U.S. ___ (2026), and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, 607 U.S. ___ (2026). 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held, 6-3, that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's August 2025 en banc ruling.
 
Penn-Wharton Budget Model economists estimate that IEEPA-based tariff collections total approximately $175 billion to $179 billion.


Following that ruling, the refund machinery has moved in phases under the supervision of Judge Richard K. Eaton of the CIT, who the chief judge designated as sole judge for all IEEPA-refund matters. 
In March and April 2026, the CIT in both *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States* and *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* ordered CBP to liquidate unliquidated entries without regard to IEEPA duties and to reliquidate any liquidated entries for which liquidation is not final; on April 20, 2026, CBP launched Phase 1 of the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) tool in the ACE portal, enabling importers or customs brokers to submit IEEPA duty refund claims.


The scale of Phase 1 intake is large. 
As of June 5, 2026, CBP reported that 181,155 applications for refunds had been submitted through the CAPE portal, of which 125,576 had been approved — covering about 16.74 million affected entries; the approved refund applications represented approximately $94.94 billion in claimed refunds, of which approximately $23.68 billion had already been paid as of early June 2026.


The critical complication is the government's posture on final-liquidated entries. 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion with the CIT in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, clarifying that CBP does not believe it has the authority to issue refunds of IEEPA tariffs that are both liquidated and finalized without a court order, and that it intends to appeal the universal injunction issued by the CIT with respect to those IEEPA tariffs.
 
On June 2, 2026, the government formally appealed the April 2026 CIT orders; a few days later, at a court conference before Judge Eaton, CBP announced its intention to roll out Phase 2 on June 29, 2026, and Phase 3 by the end of July — but reiterated that it would not issue refunds on finally liquidated entries to parties who have not filed a lawsuit before the CIT.


That last point is outcome-determinative for a large subset of claimants. 
CBP has noted that more than 330,000 importers paid or deposited approximately $166 billion in IEEPA duties over more than 53 million entries.
 Phase 1 addressed the easier two-thirds; the harder entries — those finally liquidated beyond the 90-day voluntary reliquidation window but within the 180-day protest window under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, and those fully final — are precisely what Phase 2 and Phase 3 were supposed to reach. The government's appeal signals it will contest whether Judge Eaton's universal orders can compel refunds for importers with no active CIT filing. 
For entries beyond the 90-day window, importers should closely monitor protest deadlines — 180 days from liquidation under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 — as, depending on the pace and scope of CAPE's expansion, filing protests may become the necessary administrative pathway to preserve refund rights.
 
The treatment of entries already beyond the protest deadline remains unresolved; for those entries, importers may ultimately need to file a case at the CIT, subject to further guidance from CBP and the Court.


Practical note on CAPE mechanics: 
a CAPE declaration cannot be amended once filed and accepted — if entries are rejected during validation, the filer must correct errors and submit a separate, new declaration. Phase 1 excludes entries that are the subject of pending administrative protests; CBP has indicated those entries will be routed to Phase 2.
 
The April 20 launch was accompanied by technical glitches, including duplicate tax ID errors and submission failures; CBP published the complete CAPE error code table on April 29, 2026, and early practitioner reporting indicated a rejection rate of approximately 15% during initial validation.
 Phase 2 launches in two days, on June 29. Filers should be staged and ready.

### II. Section 122 Surcharge: Federal Circuit Stay Issued June 11 — July 24 Expiration Now the Operative Deadline

The administration's fallback tariff — a 10% global surcharge imposed under 19 U.S.C. § 2132 (Trade Act of 1974, § 122) via Presidential Proclamation 11012, effective February 24, 2026 — has now survived its first appellate test, at least on the stay motion.

The CIT struck down Proclamation 11012 on May 7, 2026. 
The CIT held that Proclamation 11012, which imposed a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, was invalid because the proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficits" required by the statute.
 
The 2-1 decision resulted in a permanent injunction prohibiting collection of Section 122 duties — but only with respect to the prevailing plaintiffs: Burlap & Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington.


The government appealed and the Federal Circuit moved quickly. 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805, consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs under Proclamation 11012 and Section 122; while the appeal proceeds, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 tariffs as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions, including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.


On the merits posture, the Federal Circuit's stay opinion is meaningful. 
The panel found that the government made a sufficient showing of likely success on the merits, but expressly declined to adopt any final interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" at this stage; the court found the legislative history cited by the government and Judge Stanceu's dissent sufficient to call into question the CIT majority's three-measure framework.
 That is not a merits ruling, but it is the Federal Circuit signaling the issue is live — not a case where the government's arguments were deemed frivolous on first look.

The practical consequence: 
the Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026 in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely.
 Whether the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms or reverses the CIT on the merits, the surcharge collection window closes in 27 days. 
Given the stakes and the overlap with earlier IEEPA litigation, the case could reach the Supreme Court, which would significantly delay a final decision on the legality of the Section 122 tariffs; in the meantime, the Section 122 tariffs are set to expire, pursuant to the statutory limit, on July 24, 2026, unless explicitly extended by Congress.
 Congressional extension is not expected.

The refund calculus for Section 122 is materially different from IEEPA. 
If an appellate court ultimately invalidates the Section 122 tariffs, it is reasonable to expect that CBP will on its own initiative launch a refund process open to all importers under the CAPE mechanism currently used for IEEPA tariffs — however, this is not assured, and CIT intervention to compel refunds may be required.
 Importers who have not filed protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or initiated CIT actions for Section 122 entries should do so now. Waiting for a final Federal Circuit ruling on the merits risks foreclosure if the surcharge expires, entries liquidate, and the protest clock runs.

### III. Section 301 Replacement Architecture: July 7 Hearings, July 24 Target Implementation

The administration's longer-term tariff reconstruction runs on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2411. USTR has been running two parallel track investigations since March 2026. On the forced-labor track: 
on June 2, 2026, USTR issued notice of determinations and proposed responsive actions in 60 Section 301 investigations covering economies related to the failure to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on importation of goods produced with forced labor; the administration has included similar exceptions in both the IEEPA tariffs and the current Section 122 tariff, and the structure of those exceptions would likely inform what USTR is developing for the Section 301 action.


The proposed tariff structure: 
for economies that impose a forced labor import prohibition, have committed to impose and enforce such a prohibition through an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade, or have imposed a partial regime, USTR proposes 10% as the rate of additional duties; for all other economies, USTR proposes 12.5% as the rate of additional duty.
 Sixty economies are in scope. 
Written comments on the proposed actions are due by July 6, 2026; USTR will hold hearings beginning July 7, 2026.


The sequencing is intentional. 
USTR likely intends to be ready to impose the tariffs under this action by the time the 10% global Section 122 tariff expires on July 24, 2026.
 On the excess-capacity track, USTR initiated separate Section 301 investigations on March 11, 2026 covering 16 economies including China, the EU, Japan, South Korea, Vietnam, and India across sectors including steel, semiconductors, automobiles, chemicals, and solar, with an unofficial July 24 completion target as well. 
Section 301 tariffs are more durable than IEEPA tariffs because Section 301 expressly contemplates trade remedies, requires a developed administrative record, and does not carry the built-in 150-day sunset that limits Section 122.


The tariff-stacking risk is real. 
Absent contrary guidance from USTR, the default rule is that tariffs stack on each other.
 An importer who today pays a base MFN duty plus legacy Section 301 China tariffs plus the Section 122 surcharge could face those same layers plus a new 10–12.5% forced-labor Section 301 duty from July 25 forward — depending on whether the administration treats bilateral deal rate caps as true ceilings. 
On June 4, 2026, USTR Jamieson Greer stated that the United States would honor tariff caps contained in bilateral deals with Japan, the EU, and others, saying in substance that "a deal is a deal" — though the administration appears to view Section 301 as a separate legal basis that can operate alongside those agreements.

### IV. Refund Assignment, Downstream Claims, and CBP Offset Risk

Two second-order risks are crystallizing as CAPE refunds begin to flow.

First, refund assignment. 
Although CBP launched the CAPE system on April 20, 2026, the statutory prerequisites for valid assignment of customs refund claims — that the claim be "allowed," the amount "decided," and a "warrant for payment" issued — are likely not met for most IEEPA claims; an assignment made before those conditions are satisfied could be void or voidable under the Anti-Assignment Act.
 Lenders holding security interests in IEEPA refund claims, and buyers who have purchased those claims outright, should confirm the posture of each constituent entry before treating refund proceeds as bankable.

Second, CBP offset. 
IEEPA refunds returned through CAPE remain "available to offset amounts owed with respect to other duties," per CBP's confirmation; importers should not assume that voluntarily processed CAPE refunds will be insulated from later government efforts to recoup or offset, particularly given that confirmation.
 An importer with outstanding antidumping or countervailing duty liabilities may find a portion of its IEEPA refund netted against those balances without notice.

Third, pass-through exposure. 
Importing companies that mitigated IEEPA tariff impact by passing along surcharges or price increases should anticipate demands from commercial customers looking to recover their contributions.
 That second-wave litigation — downstream buyers suing importers for the passed-through cost — is already materializing in district courts.

## Recommended Actions

As of June 27, 2026, with 27 days remaining before the Section 122 surcharge expires and CAPE Phase 2 launching June 29, GCs and CFOs should take the following steps: (1) For IEEPA entries — ensure all unliquidated and recent-liquidated entries have been staged in CSV-ready format for Phase 2 submission; for entries beyond the 80-day liquidation window, file protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 immediately if not already done, and file a CIT complaint if entries are approaching final-liquidation status, because the government has explicitly stated it will not process CAPE refunds for finally-liquidated entries absent active CIT litigation; (2) For Section 122 entries — do not wait for the Federal Circuit merits ruling in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805; file protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or initiate CIT actions now to preserve refund rights before entries finalize, particularly given the statutory July 24 expiration, after which new entries will no longer accrue Section 122 liability but existing entries will begin running protest clocks immediately upon liquidation; (3) For Section 301 exposure — model the July 25 tariff scenario with proposed 10–12.5% forced-labor duties stacking on existing duties, and evaluate whether goods in transit or bonded warehouse can be cleared before new duties attach; note that comments are due July 6, 2026 and the public hearing is July 7; (4) On refund assignment and security interests — confirm the Anti-Assignment Act posture of any IEEPA claim before treating it as collateral or closing a claim purchase, and check ACE records for any pending CBP compliance flags that could trigger an offset against incoming CAPE payments; (5) Enroll in ACH and confirm CBP Form 4811 designations in ACE are current — CBP is issuing IEEPA refunds exclusively via ACH and a stale designation will delay or misdirect payment.
