## Analysis & Developments

### Today's Critical Date: CAPE Phase 2 Goes Live — and $36 Billion More in Refunds Enter the Pipeline


Today, June 29, 2026, CBP's CAPE Phase 2 launches. Phase 2 covers reconciliation entries where the entry remains unliquidated or was liquidated within 80 days of the Phase 2 filing date, and where the reconciliation (Type 09) entry has not yet been filed — both conditions must be met. CBP's Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas estimates Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries, with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.


Phase 2's launch is the most operationally consequential development of this morning's briefing. Importers holding reconciliation entries need to act today: the 80-day liquidation window is the gating condition, and entries outside that window will not qualify for Phase 2. 
Phase 2 will not cover all reconciliation entries. Entries filed before roughly May 31, 2025, that liquidated on a standard 314-day cycle will have liquidated more than 80 days before June 29, 2026, and fall outside Phase 2's scope. Importers with reconciliation entries from the earliest months of the IEEPA tariff period should not assume Phase 2 will capture those entries and should plan accordingly.


The backstory matters for context: 
According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 
As reported at the June 9, 2026 hearing before CIT Judge Richard Eaton, CBP's EAC Thomas disclosed that Phase 1 has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, and approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund. To underscore the scale: in all of the last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.



The government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.
 That disbursement figure — $40 billion out the door by end of this month — is the most specific public commitment Treasury has made, and it is worth holding against whatever CBP reports in its next weekly status update to Judge Eaton.

### The Finally-Liquidated Problem: Phase 3 and the Litigation-Only Gate

The single largest unresolved refund dispute involves finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period. 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion asserting, for the first time since the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, that it lacks the legal authority to issue IEEPA refunds on finally liquidated entries without an importer-specific court judgment. For importers who had been counting on the refund process to cover their finally liquidated entries, CBP's new position is a significant setback: if CBP's reasoning prevails, those importers may need to file individual cases in federal court to obtain refunds.



On May 27, 2026, in *V.O.S. Selections*, CIT Judge Richard Eaton — whom the CIT has designated as the lead judge for IEEPA refunds — directed CBP to show cause why the Court should not remove the suspension of its order requiring CBP to liquidate or reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, all entries entered subject to IEEPA duties. This would require reliquidation of finally liquidated entries. The Court ordered each party to submit a brief by June 4, 2026, and scheduled a hearing for June 9, 2026, directing CBP Commissioner Rodney S. Scott to appear in person. This Order signals that Judge Eaton is growing impatient with the pace of CBP's compliance and is actively considering lifting the stay that has held the universal refund order in suspension.



Phase 3 is targeted for late July 2026 and will cover finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period. Critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT. Approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected, if CBP proceeds as planned, to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined, regardless of liquidation status.


The practical implication is sharp: any importer that has not filed a protective CIT action and whose entries are finally liquidated is not on Phase 3's list. The filing deadline to preserve 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest rights on a liquidated entry is 180 days from liquidation. Importers should map their liquidation dates immediately. 
If, after reviewing applicable scenarios, you still believe your refund amount contains an error, you may file a Protest to dispute CBP's refund decision upon liquidation or reliquidation.


The lead dockets for refund enforcement are *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* and *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, consolidated before Judge Eaton at the CIT. 
CAPE was developed and implemented pursuant to an April 7, 2026 order of Judge Richard K. Eaton of the U.S. Court of International Trade (CIT) in *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* and *Euro-Notions Florida Inc.*
 These cases remain the operational heartbeat of the $166 billion refund program.

### Section 122: 25 Days to Statutory Expiration — Federal Circuit Stay Holds


The Section 122 tariffs are set to expire, pursuant to the statutory limit, on July 24, 2026, unless explicitly extended by Congress.
 That date is now 25 days out, and the Section 122 litigation posture has stabilized — temporarily — in the government's favor at the appellate level.

Recap of the structural posture: 
On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the U.S. Court of International Trade invalidated the 10% across-the-board Section 122 tariffs, but limited relief to three importer plaintiffs before it. The government has appealed. In late July, while the appeal is pending, the tariffs will expire.


The three cases and their dockets: 
In a divided 2-1 decision, the CIT held that President Trump exceeded the statutory authority under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 when he imposed the administration's temporary 10% balance-of-payments tariffs. *The State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026).
 The majority comprised 
Chief Judge Mark A. Barnett and Judge Claire R. Kelly, with Judge Timothy C. Stanceu dissenting, the court concluding that the presidential proclamation improperly invoked Section 122's "balance-of-payments deficits" authority to address what are, in substance, trade deficits.



Critically, the CIT's remedy is party-specific, not universal. The permanent injunction extends only to the State of Washington (through its university's direct import activity), Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.


On the appellate side: 
On May 8, 2026, the day after the CIT's opinion, the Government appealed the decision to the Federal Circuit (CAFC). On May 12, 2026, the CAFC granted the Government's motion for an immediate administrative stay, such that the CIT's permanent injunction is temporarily stayed while the appellate court considers a more prolonged stay during the duration of the appeal.
 
On May 20, the CIT separately denied the government's motion to stay its own enforcement, finding that the government had not shown irreparable harm or likelihood of success on the merits. The Federal Circuit's stay, however, remains in place — and that is the one that matters for collection. Section 122 duties are still being assessed on every importer who is not a named plaintiff.


The statutory mechanics matter for refund planning: 
The CIT's judgment declared Proclamation 11012 "invalid as contrary to law" and ordered Section 122 surcharges paid by the importer plaintiffs to be "refunded with interest as provided by law."
 
If the CIT's ruling is ultimately upheld by the CAFC, and after potential Supreme Court review, companies would likely be eligible for refunds.
 But the Section 122 refund path is categorically distinct from CAPE: 
Section 122 is a separate 10% surcharge introduced February 24, 2026 under the Trade Act of 1974. It is not refundable through CAPE — it is a completely separate authority from IEEPA.


There is a parallel wrinkle on the CIT's standing analysis that matters for non-importer plaintiffs: 
The CIT found that only direct importer plaintiffs — Burlap and Barrel, Basic Fun, and the State of Washington (through the University of Washington's direct import activity) — had standing. The remaining 23 state plaintiffs were dismissed because their alleged injuries, based on indirect passthrough costs, were deemed too speculative to support standing. The CIT issued a permanent injunction limited to those plaintiffs and declined to enter a universal injunction.


For importers paying Section 122 duties who are not named plaintiffs, the clock is running in two directions simultaneously: the July 24 statutory sunset may moot the legal challenge before the Federal Circuit rules on the merits, but if the CIT ruling is ultimately upheld and Section 122 refunds become available, any importer who failed to preserve claims — through timely protest filings under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, or through a CIT action — will likely be outside the recovery pool.

### Section 301 Investigation Pipeline: The Replacement Authority Race Against July 24


The administration recently initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices. The administration reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.



On March 11, 2026, the U.S. Trade Representative launched sweeping Section 301 investigations targeting structural manufacturing sector surpluses caused by trade with countries that have signed trade framework agreements under the Trump administration, including China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan, and India. On March 12, USTR also announced investigations to consider tariffs on a list of 60 countries in connection with forced labor practices.



Historically, timelines for Section 301 probes have generally ranged between 6 and 18 months before the recommendation phase, with tariffs typically implemented a few weeks later. It is possible these investigations will proceed faster, given that the Section 122 tariff is set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 
Many expect new Section 301 tariffs to be announced by July 24, 2026, the date the Section 122 tariffs expire.


The Section 301 tariff path matters structurally because, unlike Section 122, Section 301 authority is not time-capped at 150 days and does not require a balance-of-payments trigger. 
The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump administration that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Courts have rejected prior attempts to limit the scope of Section 232 and Section 301.
 This means that Section 301 tariffs, if announced by or around July 24, would not carry the statutory vulnerability that brought down both the IEEPA and, at the CIT level, the Section 122 regimes.

For importers doing forward-planning on duty exposure, the operative question is whether Section 301 rates, when announced, will match the effective burden that existed under IEEPA (country-specific rates ranging from 10% to 46%-plus). 
China's current effective rate is approximately 10% Section 122 + 25–100% Section 301, producing a 35–110% effective rate depending on product.
 If Section 301 replaces Section 122 at or near current levels, total-duty exposure for China-origin goods is unlikely to decrease materially after July 24.

### The Supreme Court Ruling — Standing Framework and What Remains Open

The predicate for all of the above is *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, Nos. 24-1287 and 25-250 (S. Ct. Feb. 20, 2026). 
The Supreme Court released its decision on February 20, 2026. The majority opinion, written by Chief Justice John Roberts, and joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson, stated that IEEPA does not give the President the power to set tariffs.



The Supreme Court's 6-3 ruling came down squarely on the side of the importers and the twelve state attorneys general who challenged the tariffs, holding that IEEPA's authorization to "regulate … importation" does not encompass the power to levy tariffs of unlimited scope, rate, and duration.
 
The merits holding is unqualified: IEEPA is not a tariff statute.



The decision definitively resolves only the statutory authority question and does not address the consequences of invalidating the tariffs. The Court issued no directives concerning enforcement, refunds, or other remedial actions, and did not prescribe how its holding should be implemented. The Court left the practical and remedial consequences of its ruling to be addressed in future administrative action or separate judicial proceedings at the lower court — the U.S. Court of International Trade.


Three issues remain open in the lower courts that will shape the refund universe: (1) whether CBP has authority to reliquidate finally liquidated entries absent an importer-specific judgment — the question posed in Judge Eaton's May 27 show-cause order; (2) whether the Section 122 appeal at the Federal Circuit produces a broader stay or merits ruling before July 24; and (3) whether Section 301 investigations conclude with rates that functionally replicate the IEEPA burden. Each of these tracks is moving simultaneously.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and claim-recovery counsel should act on three fronts before Friday, July 3: **First**, confirm that all Phase 1 CAPE declarations have been validated and accepted in ACE, and prepare Phase 2 submissions for any reconciliation entries that meet the dual-condition test (unliquidated or liquidated within 80 days of June 29, and Type 09 not yet filed) — 
CBP has indicated that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, absent compliance concerns requiring further CBP review
, so Phase 2 timing will be critical to cash-flow planning. **Second**, audit the full entry population for finally liquidated entries and assess whether a CIT protective action has been filed; if not, evaluate urgency in light of the government's Phase 3 litigation-only gate and consult the 180-day protest deadline under 19 U.S.C. § 1514. **Third**, immediately identify and track all Section 122 duty payments through CBP Form 7501 or the ES-003 Entry Summary Detail Report; these duties are assessed under HTSUS subheading 9903.03.01 and are not recoverable through CAPE — they require a separate recovery posture, and with the statutory sunset 25 days out, the window for preserving Section 122 refund claims through timely protest or litigation is closing. 
Importers should review Section 122 duties that they have paid and are currently paying, to preserve their rights to assert claims and seek refunds in the future.
 For importers without the bandwidth to file protectively on both tracks, prioritize the finally-liquidated CIT action over Section 122 stand-alone suits, given that Section 122's July 24 sunset may moot the legal challenge before the Federal Circuit rules.
