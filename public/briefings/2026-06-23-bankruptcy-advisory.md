### BlockFills (In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al., Case No. 26-10371 (TMH), Bankr. D. Del.)


A Delaware bankruptcy judge on June 16, 2026 agreed to approve the $3.25 million sale of cryptocurrency financial technology firm BlockFills to a Belgian digital asset investment group, as BlockFills prepares for a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.


The sale outcome is significant for creditors in two respects. First, the price itself: 
according to the bankruptcy filing, Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property.
 Against a general unsecured pool that 
the debtors reported at approximately $145 million
, the $3.25 million sale price is a thin recovery floor — the operative question going into confirmation is how much cash the liquidating trust accumulates from the sale proceeds plus wind-down collections before unsecured distributions are made.

Second, the customer-property question that has driven the case from the outset remains unresolved. 
The central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remains contested
 — an issue that will be dispositive for institutional creditors asserting property rights over deposited digital assets rather than general unsecured claims. The resolution of that question in the plan and disclosure statement, expected before the September 11 confirmation hearing, will determine whether certain customers sit in a senior property-recovery position or pool into the general unsecured class alongside trade creditors.


The four debtors filed voluntary petitions for relief under Chapter 11 on March 15, 2026, and the cases are jointly administered under Case No. 26-10371 before Judge Thomas M. Horan in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware.
 The adversary proceeding against Artha Investment Partners LLC, 
Adv. Pro. No. 26-50230 (TMH)
, remains a live docket item; resolution of that dispute may affect the estate's total recovery pool and, indirectly, the plan's distributable value.


The case began after a liquidity crisis tied to counterparty exposures, the Babel Finance bankruptcy, a failed equipment-loan settlement with Nexo, an unpaid Celsius arbitration award, customer litigation and TROs, and a February 2026 crypto-market decline that forced the debtors to suspend deposits and withdrawals.
 
The capital structure is concentrated in customer and trade exposure: the first-day record identifies roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and about $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.



On February 6, 2026, the company publicly announced a broader suspension of deposits and withdrawals. In the weeks preceding the filing, two customer lawsuits were filed against certain debtor entities and current or former directors and officers, alleging misappropriation of customer funds and conversion.
 
The courts in both matters granted temporary restraining orders that restricted the debtors' ability to conduct operations and exercise control over certain assets; the automatic stay afforded by Chapter 11 was intended to halt further litigation and prevent individual customers from pursuing assets outside the bankruptcy process.


For distressed-debt buyers: there is no public 3001 transfer notice activity confirmed on the docket as of June 22, 2026 (the last confirmed sync date), but with confirmation now approximately eleven weeks out and a liquidating trust structure on track, claims in this estate are approaching the window where secondary-market pricing becomes more predictable. Watch the plan and disclosure statement filing — likely in the next two to four weeks — for the classification of customer property claims, the treatment of the Celsius secured position, and any proposed releases.

### Sleep Number Corporation (In re Sleep Number Corporation, et al., Case No. 26-11399, Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Kyu Young Paek)


On Friday, June 12, 2026, Sleep Number filed for bankruptcy in the Southern District of New York, docket #26-11399. The company plans to sell its assets under a $415 million buyout offer from Canadian bedding company Sleep Country Canada Inc.



Substantially all of the company's debt, with balances of approximately $672.5 million in the aggregate principal amount as of the petition date, is in default and accelerated, but subject to the automatic stay under the Bankruptcy Code.
 The petition disclosed assets in the range of $500 million to $1 billion against liabilities exceeding $1 billion — a balance sheet with visible upside for secured creditors but a more uncertain picture for unsecured trade claims and landlords across the company's 572-store footprint.


Pursuant to the stalking horse APA dated June 12, 2026, the stalking horse bidder has committed — subject to court approval — to acquire substantially all of the company's assets for $415 million in cash plus the assumption of certain liabilities, establishing the floor for competing bids.
 
The transaction is being undertaken pursuant to Section 363 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code, and under the terms of the agreement, Sleep Country will serve as the "stalking horse" bidder.



The cases are being administered by the Honorable Kyu Young Paek.
 Davis Polk & Wardwell LLP is serving as debtor's counsel, with 
Brian M. Resnick of Davis Polk & Wardwell LLP as lead counsel.
 The claims administrator is Kroll, administering the case at restructuring.ra.kroll.com/SleepNumber.

The timeline is compressed. 
An auction is scheduled for July 13, but the company is open to higher and better offers.
 
Because the firm tried to sell itself in the months leading up to the Chapter 11 filing, the debtors are seeking a 26-day sale process — any competing bids would be due July 8, and the sale would close by July 31 under the company's proposed timeline.
 The bidding procedures hearing is scheduled for 
July 2, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET, and the meeting of creditors is set for July 8, 2026 at 1:00 p.m. ET.
 A final hearing on first day motions is set for 
July 9, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET.



Sleep Number blamed its bankruptcy, in part, on "the unpredictable shifting of trade rules imposed by the current U.S. government, on top of an already vulnerable global supply chain."
 That framing matters for the broader distressed-markets picture: it positions the company among the wave of consumer-goods retailers citing IEEPA tariff exposure as a proximate cause of insolvency, which has implications for trade creditor recovery theories and potential preference-exposure analysis in the 90-day window preceding June 12.


The filing indicates that there will be funds available for distribution to unsecured creditors
 — a notable representation at this stage, though its weight depends entirely on whether competing bids emerge before July 8 to push proceeds above the stalking-horse floor. Landlords with unexpired leases across 572 stores face the standard 11 U.S.C. § 365 clock: absent assumption or rejection, leases are deemed rejected at plan confirmation. The July 13 auction result will be the first concrete signal of how much of the retail footprint Sleep Country absorbs versus what gets rejected and litigated.

### Chapter 15 Provisional Relief: An Expanding Toolkit

The live-news scan flagged a Reuters item on provisional relief as "another tool in the Chapter 15 toolbox," and the development is worth anchoring with the doctrinal backdrop that has been building through 2025 and into 2026.


Because the Bankruptcy Code requires notice of a hearing to grant recognition of a foreign proceeding under Chapter 15, Chapter 15 debtors may seek provisional relief, which includes the imposition of the automatic stay and temporary recognition of the foreign court's insolvency order on an interim basis until the hearing on recognition of the foreign proceeding.
 Under 
11 U.S.C. § 1519, the U.S. court is authorized to issue preliminary relief as soon as the petition for recognition is filed.


The recent Cannabist case is an instructive data point. 
On May 9, 2026, Judge Brendan L. Shannon of the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware granted recognition of Cannabist's Canadian restructuring proceedings; Cannabist had commenced restructuring proceedings in Canada under the Canadian Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act seeking a path to sell the assets of its U.S. subsidiaries, and secured, among other relief, an order in Canada granting a stay of litigation and enforcement actions against Cannabist's Canadian parent company and U.S.-based subsidiaries.
 
Judge Shannon also granted Cannabist immediate provisional relief enforcing the Canadian Court's order following a first day hearing.


Delaware courts are simultaneously raising questions about the scope of U.S.-side relief relative to what was obtained abroad. 
A Canadian integrated real estate technology company has secured provisional Chapter 15 relief pending recognition of its CCAA proceeding, despite a Delaware judge raising concerns that the debtor was seeking more relief in the U.S. than it secured in Canada.
 That tension — between comity and the principle that Chapter 15 should not be used to expand the foreign representative's power beyond what the home court granted — is the live doctrinal fault line.

The mandatory-versus-discretionary framework governs the outer limits. 
Chapter 15 distinguishes between mandatory and discretionary relief: Section 1517 of the Bankruptcy Code mandates that a bankruptcy court grant recognition of any foreign main proceeding or foreign non-main proceeding so long as a foreign representative has properly instituted the Chapter 15 proceeding and has satisfied certain procedural requirements set forth in section 1515.
 The only exception is the public policy override. 
While Chapter 15 explicitly requires no good faith showing for recognition of foreign insolvency proceedings, courts have carved out narrow exceptions that effectively punish bad actors: through strategic application of COMI manipulation standards and the public policy exemption, judges have denied recognition to foreign representatives who abuse the process.


For creditors in cross-border cases: 
provisional relief is only granted as to those parties that the foreign representative specifies; typically, a debtor's key contract counterparties, lenders, and parties in litigation with the debtor are among those against whom provisional relief is sought.
 If you are not named in the provisional relief motion, the stay may not bind you before the recognition hearing — a critical window for enforcement that is frequently overlooked.

## Recommended Actions

Creditors and claim-holders in the BlockFills estate should treat September 11, 2026 as the controlling date: that is the confirmed plan confirmation hearing before Judge Horan, and the disclosure statement filing — expected within the next several weeks — will be the first document that quantifies the proposed treatment of customer property claims versus general unsecured claims and the projected recovery waterfall under the Celsius secured position. Review the adversary proceeding in Adv. Pro. No. 26-50230 (TMH) against Artha Investment Partners LLC for any developments that add to or subtract from distributable value. In Sleep Number (26-11399, Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Paek), the July 2 bidding procedures hearing and the July 8 competing-bid deadline are the actionable near-term dates: any creditor holding a lease, trade claim, or contract with the debtors should assess assumption/rejection risk now, not after the July 13 auction. Landlords should file protective motions for administrative claims immediately if post-petition rent is in arrears. Chapter 15 counterparties monitoring cross-border proceedings should confirm whether they are named in any provisional relief motion under § 1519 before the recognition hearing — the pre-recognition window remains the last unobstructed enforcement opportunity.
