### I. Bartz v. Anthropic (No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Still Pending; Distribution Timeline Slipping


The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement.
 
After a 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement — though given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect she will grant final approval relatively quickly.


The proceeding is substantively important on three fronts: **participation rate**, **fee scrutiny**, and **distribution mechanics**.

On participation: 
class counsel filed papers showing that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported when counsel filed the Motion for Final Approval on March 19, 2026, eleven days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed at the hearing that the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100.
 
The high claims rate means that earlier forecasts of a larger per-work payout — premised on the 54% figure — will not materialize; the actual payout will likely settle near the original $3,000 per work figure.


On fees: 
plaintiffs' counsel are seeking 25% of the $1.5 billion fund — $375 million — in attorneys' fees, with administrative costs handled by JND Legal Administration and service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff ($150,000 total for the three class representatives).
 
During the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín asked counsel to identify three leading cases supporting the size of the fee request; class counsel's responses leaned on the settlement amount — the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history — and the difficulty of litigating an untested area of law.
 Whether she accepts that justification in full, partially, or with a reduction is the open question. At the 25% figure, the net-of-fees settlement fund would produce roughly $843 million for the class — approximately $1,748 per claimed work before administration costs.

On the post-hearing procedural record: 
by order signed May 14, 2026, Judge Martínez-Olguín directed Anthropic to file no more than two supplemental pages by May 21 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and directed plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30 (Dkt. 646-1), by the same date; the Court indicated it would not consider further submissions from objectors.


On distribution timing: 
per the Plan of Allocation and Distribution, the settlement administrator's calculation of per-work and per-claimant distributions was projected for June 11, 2026 — with summer 2026 as the earliest payment window if objectors are present but no appeals are filed.
 
Payouts will only be issued after final approval is granted and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 The June 11 calculation date assumed a final approval order before that date; none has issued. The distribution timeline should be treated as rolling forward by at least the length of the current gap between the May 14 hearing and whenever the order issues. 
Anthropic is funding the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.


One structural point for claims-recovery analysts: 
the scope of the *Bartz* settlement has been widely misunderstood — it is not, in fact, a settlement about AI training per se.
 The class is limited to works Anthropic copied from LibGen and the Pirate Library Mirror. 
In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled on summary judgment that using books without permission to train AI was fair use *if they were acquired legally*, but denied Anthropic's request for summary judgment related to piracy — finding that the piracy was not fair use.
 The settlement resolves only the piracy-based claims, not the broader question of AI training legality. 
Anthropic has denied wrongdoing, the infringement question has not been adjudicated, and the settlement creates no precedent binding on future courts.


**Bottom line for today:** The settlement is structurally sound and likely to clear final approval. The outstanding risks are (1) a fee reduction that re-shuffles the per-work math, (2) any late-opt-out order that expands the pool, and (3) any objector appeal to the Ninth Circuit that delays disbursement past summer 2026.

### II. Concord I — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.) — Cross-MSJ Fully Briefed; Decision Imminent

This matter has now produced the most consequential live fair-use briefing in the AI-copyright system. 
As of June 22, 2026, the case remains assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen handling discovery, in the Northern District of California.



Plaintiffs — a group of music publishers led by Concord Music Group, Inc. and Universal Music Corp. — brought claims for direct and secondary copyright infringement and removal of copyright management information (CMI) against Anthropic, stemming from Anthropic's use of copyrighted musical lyrics to train Claude and from Claude's reproduction of those lyrics in output responses.



The publishers moved for partial summary judgment that fair use does not excuse Anthropic's conduct because Anthropic used those lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 Anthropic filed a cross-motion. Per Anthropic's own motion to stay briefing filed in Concord II, 
the parties' dispositive motions in Concord I were scheduled to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026.


This is the operative posture as of today, June 30: briefing closed June 8; Judge Lee has not yet ruled. When she does, the decision will be the first merits ruling from a federal court on the § 107 fair-use question as applied to *commercial AI training on licensed music* — distinct from the *Bartz* piracy-acquisition track and from Judge Alsup's narrower 2025 ruling on legally-acquired books. The four-factor analysis will turn most heavily on factor four (§ 107(4) market harm): 
the publishers have argued that a functioning licensing market for lyrics already exists — occupied by established lyrics aggregators who have licensed those works — and that Anthropic simply bypassed it.


The Claude AI citation error — which surfaced in an Anthropic expert declaration during discovery — adds credibility drag. 
A California federal magistrate judge partially struck an Anthropic expert report that cited a nonexistent study, calling the error "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged using Anthropic's own Claude.ai tool to help draft the declaration containing the erroneous citation.
 That episode — Anthropic's own product generating a hallucinated citation in litigation against Anthropic — is precisely the kind of credibility incident that judges remember at summary judgment and at trial.

### III. Concord II — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.) — $3 Billion Sheet-Music Action; Stay Motion Post-Hearing


Major music publishers already suing Anthropic in Concord I filed a second, $3 billion suit against Anthropic — Concord II — alleging newly discovered mass infringement of sheet music and songbooks.
 
The complaint was filed January 28, 2026.



Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending final resolution of Concord I, or in the alternative, pending resolution of the Concord I summary judgment motions (including any subsequent appeal); the stay motion was heard on May 20, 2026, at 10:00 a.m. in Courtroom 7 of the N.D. Cal. San Jose Division.
 No public order on that motion has issued as of today based on the last CourtListener sync (May 31, 2026 for Concord II).


Anthropic's stay brief argued that Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — which could have preclusive effect in Concord II.
 The publishers' counter-position: Concord II involves distinct works (sheet music, songbooks) not captured by Concord I, and a stay indefinitely defers claims on those works.

The structural risk for rights-holders in the $3 billion action is extended dormancy. If Judge Lee grants the stay and then rules against the publishers on fair use in Concord I's cross-MSJ, Concord II's viability narrows significantly. If the publishers prevail on the market-harm prong in Concord I, the sheet-music claims become substantially stronger — and Anthropic loses its best argument for a stay.

### IV. Parallel Docket System — Key Positions as of June 30, 2026

**In re OpenAI Copyright Litigation, MDL No. 3:24-md-03143 (N.D. Cal.):** This MDL consolidates the *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* action and several related book-author complaints. Discovery is proceeding. The MDL's fair-use briefing schedule should be watched for convergence with Judge Lee's Concord I ruling — whichever judge rules first will set the tone for the other.

**NYT v. OpenAI/Microsoft (S.D.N.Y.):** Not yet at summary judgment. Discovery disputes on training-data disclosure have continued. The S.D.N.Y. trajectory is slower than N.D. Cal. but matters because a divergent fair-use ruling would create circuit-level pressure.

**Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (D. Del.):** The only fully-tried AI-copyright matter to date. Judge Stephanos Bibas issued his bench-trial ruling in February 2025 finding direct infringement on the legal-headnote copying theory — but Ross's training process was legally distinct from LLM-scale web-crawl training. The ruling's precedential weight for Concord I's § 107(4) market-harm analysis is real but limited; music publishers have invoked it in their partial-MSJ papers as evidence that courts will enforce market-harm arguments against AI defendants.

**Getty v. Stability AI (D. Del. and UK):** Remain in discovery. The UK proceedings before the Court of Appeal are the principal international watch item — the UK lacks the § 107 four-factor structure and instead applies the DSM Directive Article 4 text-and-data mining exception, which contains an express opt-out mechanism that Getty has invoked. A UK appellate ruling would have no U.S. preclusive effect but would be cited by rights-holders in fee and licensing negotiations.

### V. Shadow-Library Litigation Strategy — Broader Replication Risk

The *Bartz* outcome is already reshaping how plaintiffs' counsel structures new AI-copyright cases. 
Law firms like Susman Godfrey have developed what commentators call a "shadow library strategy" — attempting to bypass the AI training fair-use debate (where courts have so far been skeptical of plaintiffs) and proceed directly on the piracy-acquisition theory. Most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies now allege that defendants downloaded from shadow libraries.


The practical implication for claims-recovery investors: piracy-track cases carry meaningfully lower litigation risk than training-fairness cases, because the acquiring-from-LibGen theory — which Judge Alsup blessed as non-fair-use in the *Bartz* MSJ — sidesteps the most contested doctrinal ground. The question is whether other AI developers have comparable LibGen or PiLiMi exposure. Discovery in the OpenAI MDL has focused on exactly that question.
