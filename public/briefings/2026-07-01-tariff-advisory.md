# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Wednesday, July 01, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's lede:** Three intersecting developments demand immediate attention as of July 1. First, a Seattle federal judge consolidated competing Amazon consumer class actions on June 29, sharpening a litigation theory that could loop retailers who did not file for IEEPA refunds into liability for the same $166 billion pool that importers are fighting to recover. Second, CAPE Phase 2 launched on schedule June 29 — 
CBP's Phase 2 of the CAPE portal launched June 29 and covers IEEPA refund requests for unliquidated entries flagged for reconciliation and antidumping/countervailing duties.
 Third, and most urgently for any importer without a CIT filing, the Section 122 surcharge expires in 23 days — July 24, 2026 — with Section 301 replacement tariffs still pending public comment and no effective-date certainty.

---

### I. The Seattle Consolidation: Amazon Consumer Class Actions and the Retailer-Liability Overhang


A federal judge in Seattle consolidated a pair of proposed class actions brought by Amazon customers seeking refunds for the now-invalidated IEEPA tariffs, as the two suits made essentially identical allegations.
 The cases are pending in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington against Amazon.com, Inc. The lead plaintiff vehicle previously identified by Hagens Berman asserts 
that "the funds Amazon is using to stay in the President's good graces do not belong to Amazon" — characterizing them as wrongfully taken from consumers to cover IEEPA tariffs that have since been invalidated. The case is styled as a class action targeting all customers who purchased imported products on Amazon between February 2025 and February 2026.


The consolidation is legally significant for a reason distinct from the Amazon-specific facts. 
Many retailers celebrated when the Supreme Court in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* struck down IEEPA tariffs; in the weeks since the February 20 decision, a wave of class action lawsuits swept across multiple industries targeting major retailers and consumer goods companies. These suits pose serious legal and financial exposure.
 The unjust-enrichment theory being tested in Seattle is not Amazon-specific. 
Watch for class action filings against Walmart, Target, Costco, Wayfair, Best Buy, and large public DTC brands on the same theory — filings against any two of those in the next 60 days would signal a litigation category, not a single case.


The structural tension is this: 
under federal trade law, only the importer of record has standing to seek a refund from the government for an improperly assessed tariff — even when the importer has passed 100% of the tariff cost on to consumers through higher prices. Plaintiffs are seizing on this perceived gap.
 Companies that publicly attributed price increases to tariffs — through line-item surcharges, customer emails, or public statements — carry materially higher exposure on this theory. 
The suits collectively illustrate significant litigation risk for any company that (1) passed IEEPA tariff costs on to consumers through higher prices and (2) is now seeking or has obtained refunds from the government for those same tariffs.

### II. CAPE Phase 2 Live; Phase 3 on Track for Late July; DOJ Appeal Is the Choke Point

The refund pipeline is moving at scale, but the DOJ appeal now casts a shadow over the finally-liquidated tranche that may account for $30 billion or more in duties.

On the processing side: 
CAPE Phase 1 has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, and approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.
 As of the June 9 CIT hearing, 
in all of the prior fiscal year CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.
 By end of June, 
the government represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and that more than $40 billion in refunds should have been disbursed.


Phase 2, which launched June 29 per CBP's CSMS #69035485, 
allows submission of entries flagged for reconciliation under entry types 01, 02, and 06, subject to existing Phase 1 limitations — entries must be unliquidated or within 80 days of liquidation — with the CAPE process removing IEEPA duties from flagged entries prior to reconciliation, separating the IEEPA refund calculation from the reconciliation entry calculation.
 Critically, 
once a reconciliation entry is filed, the underlying entries will not be eligible for a subsequent CAPE declaration under this phase.


Phase 3 — the category covering finally-liquidated entries — 
will address finally-liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs, with programming targeted to be ready in late July 2026.
 The legal prerequisite, however, is disputed: 
the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 This is the core of the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal.

On June 2–3, 2026, 
the DOJ formally appealed at the Federal Circuit the order of CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection*, Case No. 25-595, compelling CBP to refund importers of record $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs.
 The DOJ's two-front challenge: 
(1) CBP cannot reprocess older entries without a court order specific to each importer, requiring each importer seeking refunds to file its own CIT lawsuit; and (2) the Refund Order is an overly broad "universal injunction" that exceeds what the CIT is allowed to order.
 The government anchors the second argument on the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in *Trump v. CASA*, and on the Federal Circuit's prior en banc IEEPA decision in *V.O.S. Selections* vacating an earlier universal injunction. 
The petition for writ of mandamus argues that universal injunctions are unlawful under *CASA*; the CIT had previously distinguished *CASA* from its own statutory authority.



The dispute centers on finally-liquidated entries — those liquidated for more than 90 days (80 days under CAPE, which includes a ten-day buffer period) — which may account for an estimated $30 billion or more in unlawfully collected IEEPA tariff payments.
 The government's estoppel problem is real: 
in December 2025, in *AGS Company Automotive Solutions v. CBP*, Slip Op. 25-154, the DOJ affirmatively represented to the CIT that it "will not object to the [c]ourt ordering reliquidation of plaintiff's entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful." On January 8, 2026, the government further stipulated in the CIT that it would refund IEEPA tariffs for "all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs" following a "final and unappealable decision."


For non-litigant importers, the stakes are unambiguous: 
timing is essential — the two-year deadline to file suit is already running. The CIT's authority to hear tariff refund cases has a two-year time limit. The deadlines for importers whose entries were among the earliest to be hit with IEEPA duties (starting early 2025) will begin to expire in early 2027.


Meanwhile, a separate class-certification motion filed June 4 by Terry Precision Cycling in *V.O.S. Selections* seeks to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for CAPE processing — 
taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response was due June 25, 2026.
 The Federal Circuit has not yet acted on a stay motion.

### III. Section 122: 23 Days to Hard Expiration; Federal Circuit Stay Locks In Collections Through July 24


On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the CIT, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606, invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 The majority — Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly — 
held that Proclamation 11012 exceeded Section 122 authority; they rejected the administration's argument that a goods trade deficit and current-account deficit satisfied the statutory predicate, reasoning that "balance-of-payments deficits" and "fundamental international payments problems" are distinct from a long-running trade imbalance.


Relief is party-specific. 
The 2-1 decision results in a permanent injunction prohibiting collection of Section 122 duties — but only as to the prevailing plaintiffs: Burlap & Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington.
 Most state plaintiffs were dismissed for lack of standing: 
only the direct importer plaintiffs — Burlap and Barrel, Basic Fun, and the State of Washington — had standing; the remaining 23 state plaintiffs were dismissed because their alleged injuries, based on indirect pass-through costs, were too speculative, and the CIT declined to enter a universal injunction.


The Federal Circuit issued a temporary administrative stay on May 12, and then on June 11, 2026, 
the CAFC granted the government's motion to stay the lower court's injunction, allowing collection of the 10% Section 122 surcharges even from the three prevailing plaintiffs. The court found three of four traditional stay factors favored the government, with the fourth neutral — effectively pausing all CIT-granted relief for the duration of the appeal.


The statutory clock runs regardless of appellate outcome. 
Because of the time limits imposed by Section 122, the 10% tariffs imposed under Proclamation 11012 are set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 The administration's answer to expiration is not an extension of Section 122 — 
USTR is conducting two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations, expected to conclude before the Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026.


For importers deciding whether to file their own CIT challenge before expiration: 
Section 122 tariffs became effective February 24, 2026, so most entries will not reach final liquidation until early summer 2027. Importers will have until then to file administrative protests for Section 122 duty refunds if necessary.
 That said, 
refunds, if any, may be administered through the CAPE system used for IEEPA duty refunds, and for lower-volume importers unburdened by cash flow concerns, the best course for now may be to pay Section 122 duties where no exemption is available, monitor appellate proceedings, and maintain records to be used in a future claims system.

### IV. Section 301 Replacement Architecture: July 6 Comment Deadline and the Germany Pharmaceutical Probe

The administration's tariff reconstruction effort is now running on two parallel tracks — a broad replacement-tariff program targeting 60+ economies, and a targeted pharmaceutical-pricing probe focused specifically on Germany.

On the replacement track, USTR's forced-labor and excess-capacity Section 301 investigations are entering the final comment phase. 
There is a public comment period running until July 6, 2026, and USTR will hold a public hearing on July 7, 2026.
 USTR has proposed 10% tariffs for 15 trading partners that reached bilateral arrangements under the prior IEEPA framework, and 
12.5% tariffs for the remaining 45 trading partners.
 
The hearing date in July closely tracks the expiration of the Section 122 tariff action, and the proposed rates are designed to provide continuity after July 24.
 If Section 301 tariffs are not in place by that date, 
imports would revert to pre-IEEPA duty rates, plus any existing Section 232 and prior Section 301 tariffs on China.


On the pharmaceutical track, 
on June 18, 2026, USTR self-initiated a Section 301 investigation pursuant to the Trade Act of 1974, targeting Germany's "persistent underpayment" for innovative pharmaceutical products.
 The investigation, 
filed under Docket Nos. USTR-2026-0463 and USTR-2026-0464, adds a second trade-law instrument to the administration's pharmaceutical posture — one that, when read alongside Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs proclaimed on April 2, 2026, signals a multi-statute strategy to pressure trading partners to pay more for pharmaceutical R&D.
 USTR's initial focus targets 
two specific German policies: confidentiality-conditioned discounts requiring a mandatory 9% price reduction, and 2026 draft legislation proposing variable rebates on patented medicines that industry estimates could reach 20% by 2030.
 
Written comments are due August 10, 2026, and a public hearing is scheduled for September 22, 2026.
 This timeline is meaningfully slower than the Section 301 replacement-tariff investigations — Germany-specific relief, if any, would not land before year-end at the earliest.

A parallel concern for importers: 
on June 24, 2026, CBP published two interim final rules indefinitely suspending the de minimis administrative exemption under 19 U.S.C. § 1321, with the suspension for postal shipments taking effect July 24, 2026, and a delayed compliance date of October 22, 2026 for certain categories of merchandise including goods subject to PGA requirements and HTSUS Chapter 98 or 99 duties.
 The July 24 de minimis suspension is concurrent with Section 122 expiration — the same date carries two distinct compliance triggers.

### V. Small Business Access and the CAPE Participation Gap

The Bloomberg Law report from June 30 flags what the CIT itself surfaced earlier: 
most refunds processed so far are understood to have gone to large importers rather than small importers. Judge Eaton wrote that CBP has not yet proposed a method for complying with the court's order to refund all unlawfully collected duties, including amounts owed to small importers.
 The practical barriers are documented: 
some importers said they were unable to access their Customs importer accounts and waited on hold for hours trying to resolve administrative errors; Beth Benike, co-founder of Busy Baby, has not been able to apply for tens of thousands of dollars in refunds due to portal access issues; and Dahlia Rizk, owner of Buckle Me Baby, reported struggles applying for $66,000 in refunds she is owed.


The structural problem is ACE enrollment: 
some importers have reported that new ACE account setup takes 3–4 weeks.
 For small businesses whose entries are in the finally-liquidated category and who have not filed CIT suit, 
the deadlines for importers whose entries were among the earliest to be hit with IEEPA duties (starting early 2025) will begin to expire in early 2027. Importers who adopt a "wait and see" approach risk losing their right to seek a refund entirely if the two-year window closes before they file suit.

## Recommended Actions

**For importers and their counsel:** The July 6 Section 301 comment deadline is 5 days out — if you source from any of the 60+ economies under investigation, the comment period is the only procedural window to influence proposed rates before USTR finalizes the architecture that replaces Section 122 on July 24; retain trade counsel immediately if you have not already submitted. On the refund side, confirm your CAPE Phase 2 enrollment posture — CBP's June 23 CSMS #69035485 opened reconciliation and AD/CVD entries effective June 29; if any of your entries have an imminent reconciliation deadline (within 30 days), CBP's guidance prioritizes filing the reconciliation entry over waiting for future CAPE functionality. For entries in the finally-liquidated category — those liquidated more than 80 days before the CAPE processing window — the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal makes individual CIT filings the only legally secure path to recovery; the two-year limitations clock under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) runs from date of entry, meaning earliest 2025 entries begin expiring in early 2027, which is closer than it appears given legal fees, retainer timelines, and court processing. For any retailer that passed IEEPA tariff costs to consumers and is simultaneously seeking government refunds, the Seattle consolidation should trigger a litigation-readiness review now — document your pricing decisions, tariff cost-attribution methodology, and your refund-filing status before plaintiffs' counsel serves interrogatories; the unjust-enrichment theory tested against Amazon is transportable, and companies with explicit tariff surcharges, customer emails citing tariff costs, or public pricing statements carry the highest risk profile. For pharmaceutical importers, file comments on the Germany Section 301 investigation (docket USTR-2026-0463/0464) by August 10; the hearing is September 22 — the window to shape the evidentiary record on German pricing practices and potential Section 301 remedy scope is open now.
