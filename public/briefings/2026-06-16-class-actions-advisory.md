### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Post-Fairness-Hearing Settlement Watch

Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín held the fairness hearing on May 14, 2026; following that 75-minute session she did not immediately enter a final approval order but directed Anthropic, by May 21, 2026, to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored. Plaintiffs were simultaneously ordered to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.

The settlement terms themselves are now well-documented and undisputed in substance. Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion into a settlement fund; plaintiff counsel seek 25% of that fund — $375 million in fees — plus service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for all three class representatives) and JND Legal Administration costs. Class counsel apprised the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% participation rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 Motion for Final Approval. At the hearing, class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100.

The settlement administrator was scheduled to calculate distributions on June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement anticipated to begin in June 2026 or later, subject to change. However, the payment waterfall is structured in tranches: the plan of allocation provides for Anthropic to pay $450 million (Schedule Payment 1) by September 25, 2026 — 12 months after the preliminary approval order — and a second $450 million payment by September 25, 2027. If objectors pursue appeals, earliest payments to class members shift to summer 2026 at earliest.

The critical open question for rights-holders monitoring this case is whether any of the seven objectors who appeared at the May 14 hearing — each given two minutes to speak, with attorney James Bartolomei receiving five minutes to address arguments for multiple objectors — will file a timely notice of appeal. Any appeal would trigger the appellate delay track and postpone first-wave distribution well into 2027. Counsel should flag the 30-day appeal window running from the date of entry of the final approval order and advise claimant clients to monitor the docket at CourtListener.com/docket/69058235/ for the order's entry. The tracked-case ground truth confirms the last CourtListener docket sync was June 12, 2026; as of that date, no final approval order had been entered and zero new filings had posted in the prior 72 hours — consistent with the court's post-hearing supplemental briefing schedule.

**Structural note:** Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy, not for the act of AI training itself; the fair use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class members. This means the settlement does not create precedential liability for AI training on lawfully acquired books — a point that will shape how defendants in parallel litigations (OpenAI, Meta, Google) frame their own summary judgment postures.

### II. Visa/Mastercard Interchange Settlement — *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee & Merchant Discount Antitrust Litig.*, Master File No. 1:05-md-01720-MKB-JAM (E.D.N.Y.) — Preliminary Approval Granted

Judge Brian Cogan granted preliminary approval to the revised Visa and Mastercard interchange fee settlement, signing off on a deal struck by the networks with merchants who had sued over 20 years ago; the settlement resolves claims that the networks unlawfully worked together to set the interchange fees merchants pay when customers use Visa or Mastercard credit and debit cards. Judge Cogan stated the accord offers broader relief than a proposal the court rejected in June 2024 and granted preliminary approval on that basis.

Approximately 12 million merchants in the class action would be covered — generally businesses that accepted Visa or Mastercard during the period covered by the case. The ruling followed an April 27 hearing at which Cogan heard objections from several large merchants, including counsel for Walmart, that did not support the settlement. Merchants filing objections by the Friday deadline included the National Restaurant Association, the National Association of Convenience Stores, Walmart, and Hugo Boss Group; the litigation dates to 2005 and is venued in the Eastern District of New York.

The damages-only settlement — the $5.54 billion *Rule 23(b)(3)* component covering the January 1, 2004 through January 25, 2019 class period, previously administered through paymentcardsettlement.com — remains separately procedurally complete. That settlement covers interchange fees attributable to merchants that accepted Visa or Mastercard credit or debit cards between January 1, 2004 and January 25, 2019; the Class Administrator holds defendant data permitting it to estimate total interchange fees attributable to each authorized claimant. The newly preliminarily approved injunctive-relief component addresses ongoing fee-cap and surcharging rules going forward — the piece large merchant groups have fought hardest to reshape.

For qualifying merchants, the payment amount is not fixed but is tied to a formula based on factors including card transaction volume and the value of eligible interchange fees during the covered period, along with total approved claims; individual payment estimates are not final until the administrator processes claims and the settlement becomes effective. Merchants and their counsel should track the preliminary approval order for the claims-filing deadline and objection cure deadline, and evaluate whether the injunctive relief provisions materially improve on the June 2024 rejected term sheet before advising clients to participate or opt out.

### III. Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Estimated $218 Billion in Claims, Active Filing Phase

On May 11, 2026, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman announced it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges. The campaign is structured around Google's own mandatory arbitration clause — a contractual provision that forecloses class treatment but, at scale, creates the mass-arbitration pressure dynamic Keller Postman has deployed successfully against DoorDash, Postmates, and Intuit.

The liability predicate is unusually strong by mass-arbitration standards. Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business. Federal courts have now determined that Google unlawfully maintained dominance across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem; according to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.

The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist Keller Postman has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability. Federal antitrust law permits treble damages, meaning a 10% overcharge claim could translate into a recovery of up to 30% of the advertiser's ad spend. A company with $100 million in Google Ads spend since 2016 could thus have a claim worth up to $30 million.

According to Keller Postman, any company that purchased ads on Google on or after August 2016 may qualify for the arbitration process. The firm is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines; Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad tech case is also expected in 2026, which could strengthen the damages foundation. For large advertisers — including major brands with hundreds of millions of dollars in annual Google media spend — mass anonymous arbitration allows pursuit of settlements without public brand exposure or disruption to active media relationships.

The AAA/JAMS fee-front dynamic that previously constrained claimants in earlier mass-arb campaigns is the principal procedural variable. If Google pursues a filing-fee challenge or seeks a stay pending the remedies ruling, the active-demand timeline could shift materially. Counsel advising advertiser clients should evaluate the statute of limitations landscape carefully: Keller Postman is expressly filing ahead of state filing deadlines, signaling that tolling strategy is already baked into the campaign's intake model.

### IV. *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litig.*, Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.) — Distribution Underway

After years of court approval and appeals, the $2.67 billion BCBS subscriber antitrust settlement reached the payment stage in May 2026; a court order directed the Settlement Administrator to begin issuing payments during the week of May 11, 2026. Approximately $1.9 billion will be distributed to roughly six million claimants, with individual payouts varying based on coverage history and premiums paid. The distribution plan covers nearly 7.8 million claimants; subscriber plaintiffs had filed a motion on April 23, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama to approve the distribution plan.

Claimants with a calculated distribution of $200 or more will receive 90% in the initial round with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution; claimants receive funds by check, PayPal, Venmo, or electronic debit card. Wave 2 of prepaid debit cards was mailed during the week of May 19; paper-check distribution began rolling out May 25; direct deposit confirmations are running 5–10 business days behind notification email; and Tier 2 (employer/employee) notification emails started June 1, 2026 for approximately 15% of validated Tier-2 claims.

A parallel BCBS provider-side settlement is also proceeding. In October 2024, the BCBS Association and affiliates agreed to pay $2.8 billion to settle antitrust claims from hospitals, physician practices, and other providers — the largest such settlement in the healthcare industry; a federal judge approved that settlement in August 2025. Nearly 6,500 providers opted out, however, including facilities owned by Mayo Clinic, Providence, University of Michigan Health, and AdventHealth; dozens of health systems subsequently filed new antitrust lawsuits alleging that anticompetitive practices are ongoing. The Settlement Claims Administrator began issuing claim notices to providers on February 16, 2026; claimants may receive email or mail notification and can log in to their portal to view or download their notice.

The subscriber distribution is now in its rolling middle phase. Claimants who received notification but have not yet redeemed their payment instrument should act promptly; unclaimed cards and checks typically enter an escheat or secondary distribution pool under the plan documents.
