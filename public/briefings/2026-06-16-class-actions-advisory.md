### I. Bartz v. Anthropic PBC (N.D. Cal. No. 3:24-cv-05417) — Final Approval Under Submission

The $1.5 billion Anthropic copyright settlement — the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history when Anthropic agreed to the figure in August 2025 — is now pending final approval by Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, sitting in San Francisco. After a 75-minute hearing on May 14, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined to approve the settlement from the bench; given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect her to grant final approval relatively quickly.

The participation metrics are extraordinary by class-action standards. Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% participation rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval filed 11 days before the March 30 claims deadline. Class counsel Justin Nelson reported at the hearing that the per-work payout will be approximately $3,100. That figure contracts from earlier projections that had assumed lower uptake; the high claims rate means the payout will be closer to the original $3,000-per-work estimate rather than the higher per-work figures that circulated in March.

The settlement's economic architecture: plaintiffs' counsel are seeking 25% of the fund, or $375 million in attorneys' fees; administration by JND Legal Administration will cost several million dollars; and service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff ($150,000 total) are requested for the three class representatives. Following the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered Anthropic to file by May 21, 2026 a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and directed plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections with a redline to the April 30, 2026 proposed order. The court indicated it would not consider further submissions from objectors. Seven objectors were heard at the hearing, each given two minutes, except attorney James Bartolomei who was given five minutes to speak for several objectors.

The critical doctrinal caveat counsel must internalize: the scope of the Bartz settlement has been widely misunderstood — it is not a settlement about AI training. The class was certified only on the piracy theory (unauthorized acquisition of LibGen/PiLiMi copies), not on the act of training itself. If the court grants final approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and pay the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund — but for many authors, the litigation around AI training is not ending; it is shifting to other cases. Claimants seeking recovery for training-use infringement will need to track the consolidated OpenAI/Microsoft actions and the parallel putative class suits against Meta and other LLM developers proceeding on separate dockets.

The most recent docket activity per the tracked-case ground truth: Entry 674 (Jun. 4, 2026) is an order granting a motion to withdraw as attorney, with no new substantive filings in the 12 days since. The case is in the post-hearing submission window; counsel should monitor PACER at CourtListener docket ID 69058235 daily for the final approval order.

### II. Visa/Mastercard Interchange Fee Settlement (E.D.N.Y.) — Preliminary Approval Granted June 10

The most consequential development of the past seven days in the class-action landscape is the June 10 preliminary approval of the revised Visa/Mastercard interchange fee settlement. Judge Brian Cogan (E.D.N.Y.) signed off on the deal, which resolves claims that the networks unlawfully worked together to set interchange fees merchants pay when customers use Visa or Mastercard credit and debit cards. Cogan found the accord offers broader relief than the proposal the court rejected in June 2024.

Approximately 12 million merchants in the class would be covered — generally, businesses that accepted Visa or Mastercard during the period covered by the case. The substantive rate relief: the proposed settlement would trim credit interchange fee rates by 10 basis points for five years and impose a 1.25% rate for standard consumer cards for eight years.

The path to preliminary approval was contested. The ruling follows an April 27 hearing at which Cogan heard objections from large merchants, including an attorney for Walmart, that do not support the settlement. Walmart, in its December 2025 objection, asked Cogan to divide the enormous class, arguing that the interests of large national retailers diverge too widely from smaller class members. Five consumer and business advocacy groups — including the American Economic Liberties Project, Consumer Reports, Demand Progress Education Fund, Small Business Majority, and U.S. PIRG — challenged the settlement, arguing that changes from the prior rejected proposal were "too insubstantial and too susceptible to undermining by Visa, Mastercard, and card issuers."

The litigation, pending before Judge Cogan in the Eastern District of New York, has been active since 2005. The interchange fee settlement is aimed at ending 21 years of litigation on the matter. With preliminary approval now in hand, the case advances to the notice-and-claims phase, after which a final fairness hearing will be scheduled. Merchants who accepted Visa or Mastercard during the covered period should consult the official settlement portal at paymentcardsettlement.com to assess eligibility and monitor the claims submission window that will open under the court-supervised process.

### III. BCBS Antitrust Settlement (N.D. Ala. MDL No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP) — Payment Distribution Active

The subscriber-side Blue Cross Blue Shield settlement is now in active pay-out. The $2.67 billion settlement began paying out on May 11, 2026; on April 23, subscriber plaintiffs had filed a motion in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama asking the court to approve a distribution plan for nearly 7.8 million claimants. The initial distribution of payments to Damages Class Members with valid claims began on May 11, 2026.

The parties reached settlement on October 16, 2020; Judge R. David Proctor granted preliminary approval that November and final approval on August 9, 2022; objectors appealed; and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed the settlement in 2023 before the Supreme Court declined certiorari. The five-year appellate odyssey is the reason payment has only now reached claimants. Roughly $2 billion of the fund will go to approved claimants after legal fees, with individual payments varying based on premiums paid and coverage type.

The provider-side parallel track has closed out separately: the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama granted final approval to the provider settlement on August 19, 2025, and it became effective on September 19, 2025. Nearly 6,500 providers opted out, however, including facilities owned by Mayo Clinic, Providence, University of Michigan Health, and AdventHealth; dozens of health systems subsequently filed new antitrust lawsuits alleging anticompetitive practices are ongoing. In February 2026, physician staffing groups filed a new federal antitrust lawsuit in California against the BCBS Association and its affiliates, alleging market allocation, reimbursement suppression, and price fixing. Opt-out litigation and successor suits will likely define the next BCBS litigation cycle for health system counsel tracking creditor-rights and antitrust exposure through 2028.

### IV. Purdue Pharma / Sackler Family — $7.4B Plan Confirmed; Company Dissolved

The Purdue Pharma opioid reorganization, the most legally complex pharmaceutical bankruptcy in U.S. history, has now crossed from litigation into implementation. The United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York confirmed the Purdue Pharma bankruptcy plan, approving a $7.4 billion settlement between 55 attorneys general, Purdue Pharma, and the Sackler family. Purdue's bankruptcy case came to a close after more than six years in court following a lengthy series of appeals; the criminal sentencing (a $5.5 billion figure) was one of the final hurdles before the settlement could proceed, and Purdue emerged from bankruptcy on May 1, 2026, ceasing prior operations and reorganizing as a nonprofit manufacturer of opioid addiction treatment and overdose-reversal medicines.

The plan's architecture reflects the Supreme Court's June 2024 ruling in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma* (No. 23-124), which on a 5-4 vote held that the Bankruptcy Code does not permit a reorganization plan to discharge third-party claims against non-debtors who have not themselves filed for bankruptcy. The original plan was rejected because it gave Sackler family members protection from lawsuits even though they had not personally filed for bankruptcy; the revised plan allows lawsuits against Sackler family members by those who do not opt into the deal — a change that was key to gaining approval. Under the revised plan, the Sackler family agreed to pay up to $6.5 billion over time; in exchange they received narrower legal protections, not the total immunity originally sought.

Victims and beneficiaries tracking distributions should note: in 2026, settlement funds are finally reaching victims, families, and communities; after years of bankruptcy proceedings, Supreme Court battles, and revised settlement plans, billions of dollars are now being distributed. Claims-processing through the opioid abatement trusts is active. Counsel representing individual opioid claimants should monitor the trust administrator's portal and the S.D.N.Y. reorganization docket (Case No. 19-23649, Judge Sean H. Lane) for distribution schedules across the personal injury, NAS/NEC, and third-party payor channels.

### V. Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — ~$218B Campaign, AAA/JAMS Proceedings Active

The largest mass-arbitration campaign ever attempted by estimated dollar value is now live. On May 11, 2026, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman announced it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges. Ashley Keller is filing mass-arbitration claims against Google/Alphabet on behalf of advertisers, seeking approximately $218 billion tied to the federal monopoly rulings.

The liability foundation rests on two trial-court findings: Judge Amit P. Mehta (D.D.C.) on the search-monopolization side, and Judge Leonie M. Brinkema (E.D. Va.) on the ad-tech side. Federal courts have determined that Google unlawfully maintained dominance across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem; according to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade. Estimated overcharges are in the range of 5–10% of ad spend, and with treble damages under federal antitrust law, a company with $100 million in Google Ads spend since 2016 could have a claim worth up to $30 million.

The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most prior mass arbitrations have involved consumer or labor claims; arbitration clauses in Google's advertiser contracts prevent class-action damages suits, so advertisers can instead join together as a group in arbitration, which gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations. Google's advertiser contracts specify a particular arbitration forum — likely AAA or JAMS — both of which have mass-arbitration rules triggered at 25 or more simultaneous coordinated claims. The $218 billion figure is an estimate, not a filed demand amount; in mass arbitration, leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger.

The remedies dimension is critical to the arbitration timeline. Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad-tech case is expected in 2026; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines. That ruling — which will resolve whether the DOJ's preferred remedy of divesting Google's AdX exchange or a behavioral-remedies-only approach is adopted — will sharpen the factual predicate for per-claim damages calculations in the arbitration proceedings. Businesses that purchased Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016 should assess eligibility through Keller Postman's intake portal at adsrefund.com before state limitation periods foreclose claims.
