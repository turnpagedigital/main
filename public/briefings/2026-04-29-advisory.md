# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 29, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Tomorrow marks a significant milestone in the administration of the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history: Anthropic's second $300 million installment under the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement is due on April 30, 2026, bringing total funded amounts to $600 million of the $1.5 billion agreed payout. The fairness hearing before Judge Martínez-Olguín has been confirmed for May 14, 2026 at 2:00 p.m. PT at the San Francisco Federal Courthouse, with remote participation available via Zoom — a procedural accommodation that will allow the unprecedented number of claimants to observe and, in some cases, be heard on the record. Class counsel's latest filing reports a 91.3 percent claim rate, with 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works claimed by roughly 120,000 authors, a figure that dwarfs the typical class-action claim rate of approximately ten percent and will weigh heavily in the Rule 23(e) fairness analysis. ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/anthropic-settlement-update-91-percent-of-books-claimed/)) ([Anthropic Settlement Key Dates](https://www.anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com/dates))

The unsealed objections now available on the docket present a spectrum of challenges that Judge Martínez-Olguín will need to address at the fairness hearing. Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's objection raises a structural concern about publisher favoritism in the distribution plan, arguing that unclaimed amounts may disproportionately flow to publishers rather than being held in escrow or directed to cy pres. A related objection targets the group-registration collapse problem — where multiple books registered under a single Copyright Office group registration are treated as one "claimable work," effectively reducing per-title compensation for prolific authors. Other objectors contend that the settlement's approximately $3,000-per-work payout is inadequate when measured against the statutory damages ceiling of $150,000 per work under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c), arguing that the class notice obscured this comparison by framing the choice as accepting the settlement or "getting nothing." ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) ([Copyright Alliance](https://copyrightalliance.org/participating-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement/))

On the parallel docket, the In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation (MDL No. 3143) continues to generate significant discovery rulings in the Southern District of New York. Judge Stein's March 9, 2026 order enforcing the production of the full 20-million ChatGPT log sample — rejecting OpenAI's privacy-based objections — remains the most expansive compelled disclosure in any AI copyright proceeding. Plaintiffs are also pressing for discovery into OpenAI's deletion of the Books1 and Books2 training datasets, an issue with potential spoliation implications under Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e). Summary judgment briefing in the NYT v. OpenAI track is expected by mid-2026, and the Advance Local v. Cohere "substitutive summaries" doctrine from Judge McMahon's recent MTD denial is already being cited by plaintiffs across the MDL as output-side infringement precedent. ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-must-turn-over-20-million-chatgpt-logs-judge-affirms)) ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/openai-loses-privacy-gambit-20-million-chatgpt-logs-likely-headed-copyright))

In the music-publisher litigation, the Computer & Communications Industry Association, AI Progress, and NetChoice have filed an amicus brief in Concord Music Group v. Anthropic urging the court to affirm that AI training constitutes fair use, characterizing the process as "intermediate, non-expressive copying" that produces a "fundamentally new kind of tool" rather than a market substitute. This brief arrives as the publishers' partial summary judgment motion and Anthropic's cross-motion approach the May hearing calendar, with the $3 billion second complaint (filed January 2026 covering 20,000+ works) adding statutory-damages pressure. Meanwhile, the RIAA, NMPA, and six other industry groups have filed their own amicus brief characterizing Anthropic as a "holdout" that scrapes rather than licenses — a framing that could influence the court's weighing of the fourth fair-use factor, market harm, under 17 U.S.C. § 107. ([CCIA](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/04/ccia-urges-court-to-affirm-fair-use-protections-for-generative-ai-training/)) ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/))

Internationally, the CJEU held its first-ever hearing on generative AI and copyright on March 10, 2026 in Like Company v. Google, a case that will shape the interpretation of the DSM Directive Article 4 text-and-data-mining exception across the EU. The UK Publishers Association's March 2026 report reveals an AI licensing market "far beyond previous estimates," with nearly 40 Independent Publishers Guild members having sent letters of claim in February to Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, Google, and xAI via Fox Williams LLP. These coordinated pre-action letters represent the first collective enforcement action by UK independent publishers and may accelerate the timeline for UK litigation that would parallel the U.S. docket. The Supreme Court's denial of certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter (March 2, 2026) has cemented the rule that AI-generated works require human authorship for copyright protection, a principle that may influence output-side theories across all pending cases. ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/like-company-v-google-cjeu-holds-first-ever-hearing-on-generative-ai-and-copyright-on-10-march-2026)) ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/04/15/copyright-owners-v-ai-developers-the-emerging-trends)) ([Fox Williams](https://www.foxwilliams.com/2026/02/11/fox-williams-advises-independent-publishers-on-ai-copyright-claims-against-major-technology-companies/))

## Recommended Actions

With Anthropic's second $300 million installment due tomorrow and the fairness hearing fifteen days away, class members who filed claims should verify their claim status through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and confirm that all contact and payment information is current, particularly authors whose works were registered under group Copyright Office registrations — the group-registration collapse objection may prompt the court to seek supplemental briefing or amend the distribution methodology. Rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement and are pursuing independent claims should ensure their preservation obligations are current, as the Bartz fair-use summary-judgment framework (training as transformative use; retention as non-fair-use) is being cited across the parallel dockets and may inform dispositive motions in their own proceedings. AI developers facing similar copyright theories should evaluate their training-data provenance documentation in light of the OpenAI MDL discovery orders, particularly the Books1/Books2 spoliation inquiry, and benchmark their licensing posture against the growing market documented in the UK Publishers Association report — the RIAA's "holdout" framing in the Concord v. Anthropic amicus demonstrates that courts may weigh the availability of licensing alternatives under the fourth fair-use factor. Practitioners tracking the CJEU's Like Company v. Google reference should monitor for the Advocate General's opinion, expected later in 2026, as it will be the first EU-level guidance on whether generative AI training falls within the Article 4 TDM exception.
