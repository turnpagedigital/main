## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: The Final Thirteen Days — Section 122 Expires July 24; Section 301 Is Not Yet Final

The dominant operational fact as of this morning is a hard statutory deadline thirteen days away. 
The Section 122 surcharge was in effect for the 150-day period beginning February 24, 2026, and terminates on July 24, 2026, absent congressional extension.
 
The President cannot extend it unilaterally, Congress has not acted to extend it, and bipartisan appetite is uncertain with the "Reclaim Trade Powers Act" moving in the opposite direction.
 That creates a forced inflection: either USTR finalizes Section 301 replacement duties before the sunset, or a broad swath of imports reverts to pre-IEEPA MFN-plus-232/301 rates at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24. 
If Section 301 tariffs are not in place by July 24, imports revert to pre-IEEPA duty rates: MFN plus existing Section 232 and existing China Section 301.


The replacement architecture is largely visible but not yet finalized. 
On June 2, 2026, USTR issued a press release announcing affirmative determinations in all 60 of its Section 301 forced-labor investigations, concluding that every major U.S. trading partner has failed to adequately prohibit or enforce bans on imports of goods produced with forced labor — 60 economies that collectively account for 99.4% of all goods imported into the United States — with proposed additional tariffs ranging from 10% to 12.5%.
 
Public comment closed April 15 on the capacity track, and USTR is running an accelerated ~5-month timeline with a completion deadline of July 20, 2026 — four days before Section 122 lapses.
 Critically, 
the proposed tariff actions are not final — the President retains final decision-making authority on tariff rates and country coverage, and President Trump may depart from USTR's proposed approach.


Two other live news items from the 72-hour scan bear watching: **South China Morning Post** reported this week that the U.S. is scrutinizing China's worker-protection practices as it weighs additional forced-labor tariffs. The timing aligns directly with the USTR's June 2 Section 301 determination, which named all 60 major trading partners — including China — for inadequate forced-labor enforcement, and the July 7 public hearing before the USITC. **Foley & Lardner** simultaneously published a client alert titled *After Learning Resources: Defending Against the Wave of Consumer Class Actions Seeking Tariff Refunds*, flagging a new litigation risk: retailers and importers who passed IEEPA tariff costs to consumers via surcharges are now being named as defendants in class actions by those consumers seeking pass-through refunds. This is a distinct and underappreciated second-order exposure that the refund pipeline does not address.

### Section 301 Transition: Rate Architecture, Exemptions, and the July 20 Completion Deadline

USTR's June 2 proposed framework covers two parallel tariff tracks. 
USTR proposed two tariff rates: 10% for economies that have made forced-labor import-prohibition commitments under the Agreement on Reciprocal Trade ("ART") or have a forced-labor import prohibition in place but do not enforce it effectively; and 12.5% for economies without such commitments.
 
On March 11–12, 2026, USTR launched two parallel Section 301 investigations — one covering structural excess manufacturing capacity in 16 major economies, a second covering forced-labor enforcement practices in 60+ trading partners — both explicitly designed to produce replacement tariffs before Section 122 expires. Section 301 has no rate cap and no time limit.



Despite the breadth of economies implicated, USTR's proposed remedy includes meaningful categorical exclusions, including goods of Canada or Mexico that are compliant with the USMCA and textiles and apparel that enter duty-free under CAFTA-DR.
 
EU countries are largely insulated: as of July 1, 2026, they pay the trade deal's 15% all-inclusive ceiling, which insulates them from both the Section 122 expiry and the Section 301 reset.


For China, the stacking math is punishing regardless. 
China faces 10% Section 122 plus 25–100% Section 301, for a 35–110% effective rate on most goods
 — and the proposed 12.5% forced-labor rate would represent a new layer stacked atop that already elevated base. 
Separately, importers had until Friday, July 10 to submit comments to USTR about Chinese-origin products that should receive tariff relief under the newly chartered US-China Board of Trade, which will consider up to $30 billion in "non-sensitive" imports from China for duty reductions.
 That comment window has now closed as of yesterday.

On the Section 122 litigation front, the legal vulnerability of the surcharge itself remains live. 
The CIT issued a 2-1 decision on May 7, 2026, finding the Section 122 tariffs were not justified and placing an injunction limited to the plaintiffs; the Federal Circuit issued a stay of that injunction in June 2026, though did not rule on the merits.
 
The status of duties collected during the Section 122 period remains subject to the pending appeal in *State of Oregon v.*
 [Trump] before the Federal Circuit. The statutory expiration on July 24 may moot the merits of that appeal as to prospective collection, but it does not resolve whether approximately $150 billion in Section 122 duties collected since February 24, 2026, are subject to refund. That exposure is analytically distinct from the IEEPA refund pipeline and has received far less attention.

### CAPE Refund Pipeline: $23.68 Billion Paid, $71.26 Billion Approved-But-Unpaid, Final-Entry Dispute Escalates to Federal Circuit

The IEEPA refund machinery has moved at unprecedented speed for a government payment system, but the harder legal questions — concerning entries with final liquidation — remain unresolved and are now on appeal.

**CAPE Phase 1 volumes (as of June 2026):** 
As of June 5, 2026, CBP reported that 181,155 applications for refunds had been submitted through the CAPE portal, of which 125,576 had been approved, covering approximately 16.74 million affected entries; the approved applications represented approximately $94.94 billion in claimed refunds, of which approximately $23.68 billion had already been paid as of early June 2026.
 Against CBP's reported total collection of $166 billion across approximately 53 million entries, 
more than 330,000 importers paid or deposited approximately $166 billion in IEEPA duties over more than 53 million entries
 — meaning roughly $142 billion in IEEPA refunds remains outstanding even before accounting for interest.

**The final-liquidation dispute — Federal Circuit Case No. 26-1898:** 
On June 2, 2026, the government appealed the April 2026 CIT orders. The government argued that Judge Eaton had acted beyond the scope of the CIT's powers in issuing a universal injunction to apply to "anyone," including parties without active claims before the CIT. CBP announced its intention to roll out the remaining Phases 2 and 3 of IEEPA refunds through the CAPE portal with Phase 2 scheduled to launch on June 29, 2026, and Phase 3 expected by the end of July.
 
CBP reiterated its position that the agency would not issue refunds on finally liquidated entries to parties who have not filed a lawsuit before the CIT.


The government's position is unambiguous: 
DOJ reiterated that CBP lacks authority to reliquidate final entries to remove IEEPA tariffs or to issue refunds absent a court order.
 Judge Eaton disagreed. 
Judge Eaton held that "[a]ll importers of record whose entries were subject to IEEPA duties are entitled to the benefit of the Learning Resources decision," also issuing an unequivocal response that the Supreme Court's holding in *Trump v. Casa, Inc.* that universal injunctions are not permissible does not apply to the CIT because the court has been granted national geographic jurisdiction by statute.


**CAPE Phase limitations and protest clock:** 
Although CAPE Phase 1 addresses a significant volume of entries, it still leaves large numbers of entries tentatively unrefunded, namely the entries whose liquidation is "final."
 
For entries beyond CBP's 90-day voluntary reliquidation window, importers should closely monitor protest deadlines — 180 days from liquidation under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 — as, depending on the pace and scope of CAPE's expansion, filing protests may become the necessary administrative pathway to preserve refund rights.
 
The treatment of entries already beyond the protest deadline remains unresolved; for those entries, importers may ultimately need to file a case at the CIT, subject to further guidance from CBP and the Court.


**Compliance offset risk:** 
CBP indicated that when reviewing CAPE declarations it may consider whether an importer of record has compliance issues or outstanding bills unrelated to IEEPA, and more recent guidance suggests that later phases of CAPE may introduce tools to offset an IOR's outstanding bills against IEEPA refunds.
 This is not a small risk for large-volume importers with unrelated customs enforcement exposure.

### Consumer Pass-Through Class Actions: The Second-Order Refund Exposure

The Foley & Lardner alert (*After Learning Resources: Defending Against the Wave of Consumer Class Actions Seeking Tariff Refunds*) is the most practically underappreciated development in the 72-hour scan. The theory is straightforward: importers who collected IEEPA tariff-related surcharges from downstream buyers — whether retail consumers, commercial purchasers, or e-commerce customers — may face demand that they return those amounts now that the underlying tariff has been declared unlawful. 
The refund process and commercial disputes over which party is entitled to refunds — e.g., the importer or the importer's customer who may have paid a tariff surcharge added by the importer — are likely to drag on for months or even years, creating additional challenges for some companies.


The litigation posture is early, but the exposure is real. Importers who itemized IEEPA tariff costs as separate line items in customer invoices are in the most exposed position. Those who simply raised prices without attribution have a more defensible position — the refund flows to the importer of record, full stop, absent a contractual or unjust-enrichment theory. Counsel should review customer-facing invoice language, tariff surcharge clauses in commercial agreements, and any representations made to buyers about cost composition for the February 2025 through February 2026 IEEPA period.

### Section 301 USTR Public Hearing — July 7 (Now Complete); Final Determination Pending


USTR proposed duties ranging from 10% to 12.5% on 60 economies for alleged shortfalls in enforcing anti-forced-labor laws. Following the close of the public comment docket, USTR launched public hearings starting July 7.
 Those hearings are now complete. 
USTR is actively soliciting final input on which products should be added to or removed from the tariff scope, whether the Annex A exclusions are appropriately drawn, the rate structure, features of the textile mechanism, and whether ART-commitment countries should be treated differently from countries that have already enacted prohibitions.
 The final determination is expected by July 20, giving USTR a four-day buffer before July 24.

What the final determination will look like is not publicly confirmed. The administration has signaled that the 12.5% rate is the ceiling, not the floor; 
President Trump may depart from USTR's proposed approach
, and politically sensitive countries — particularly Mexico in the wake of the July 1 USMCA joint-review commencement — could see adjusted treatment. 
Mexico — the USMCA partner where the U.S. just declined renewal at the July 1 joint review — is where Section 301 is most politically fraught.


For pharma importers, a separate clock runs in parallel: 
Section 232 actions are proceeding in parallel, including 100% duties on patented pharmaceuticals and active pharmaceutical ingredients effective July 31 for larger companies and September 29 for others.
 That stacks on top of whatever Section 301 rate applies, and unlike Section 122, 
these authorities carry no statutory rate ceiling and no fixed expiration.

## Recommended Actions

Clients carrying IEEPA refund exposure — whether as importers, claimants, or parties who have assigned or are considering assigning refund claims — face a simultaneous five-front deadline crunch. First, verify CAPE Phase 1 declaration status through the ACE portal immediately; 
once CBP undergoes mass processing of an importer of record's CAPE declaration, unliquidated entries will be set to liquidate within 45 days of the acceptance date, with valid refunds generally issued within 60 to 90 days following acceptance — absent compliance concerns
, which triggers the offset-risk review noted above. Second, for any entry liquidated more than 80 days ago, confirm whether the 180-day protest window under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 is still open; 
CBP has stated that any importer whose entries have finally liquidated and who has not filed its own claim at the CIT will not be eligible for a refund unless and until there is a court order in its favor
 — meaning the Federal Circuit's resolution of Case No. 26-1898 will be outcome-determinative for a material portion of the $166 billion refund pool. Third, companies importing from any of USTR's 60 named economies should model their July 25 landed-cost scenarios under both a 10% and a 12.5% Section 301 rate and identify which HTS codes may be eligible for Annex A exclusions before the final determination issues on or around July 20; 
the shift from Section 122 to Section 301 is not a simple swap — the architecture is meaningfully different and for many importers more complex.
 Fourth, review all customer contracts and commercial invoices for IEEPA tariff pass-through language covering February 2025 through February 2026; the consumer class-action risk flagged by Foley & Lardner is not speculative. Fifth, USMCA qualification analyses run pre-2024 should be revalidated now; USMCA-qualifying goods from Canada and Mexico remain exempt under the proposed Section 301 framework, and the value of qualification is material at a 12.5% potential rate. 
The Tariff Refund Act of 2026 (S. 3905) would require CBP to refund all IEEPA duties within 180 days of enactment with interest, prioritizing small businesses
 — worth tracking as a legislative backstop, though passage remains uncertain.
