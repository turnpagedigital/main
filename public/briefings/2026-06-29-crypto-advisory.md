### BlockFills: Background, Capital Structure, and the § 541 Exposure


Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and three affiliated entities filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on March 15, 2026 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware (Case No. 26-10371 (TMH)). The Debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations. The filing is supported by a pre-negotiated term sheet with an Ad Hoc Group of the company's largest customers that provides the framework for a reorganization plan centered on the creation of a new operating entity and a liquidating trust.



The company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius. Compounding these balance sheet pressures were stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials.
 Those facts are important for fraudulent-transfer analysis: the Babel and AEXA impairments are documentable arms-length losses, but the arbitration obligation to Celsius and the mining-hardware write-off will require tracing to determine whether any value was transferred to insiders during the relevant look-back periods under 11 U.S.C. §§ 547–548.


Bertram v. Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al., Case No. 26-cv-02451 (N.D. Ill. March 5, 2026): the plaintiffs sought emergency injunctive relief, and the courts granted temporary restraining orders that significantly restricted the Debtors' ability to conduct business operations and exercise control over certain assets. These restrictions created substantial operational and liquidity challenges.
 The adversary proceeding docketed as Adv. Pro. No. 26-50224 (TMH) appears to be the Debtors' injunction action against those very customer litigants. 
In the weeks immediately preceding the filing, two customer lawsuits were filed alleging misappropriation of customer funds, conversion, breach of contract, and fraud-based claims. Both courts granted temporary restraining orders that significantly restricted the Debtors' operations and asset control. The automatic stay imposed by the Chapter 11 filing on March 15, 2026 halted these actions.


The sale price also invites scrutiny. 
The acquisition price tag of $3.25 million represents a stark valuation collapse for an entity that claimed over $60 billion in trading volume just one year prior.
 
According to BlockFills, trading volume exceeded $60 billion in 2025, a 28% increase from the previous year. The firm said it served approximately 2,000 institutional clients and ranked among the more active desks in the institutional crypto lending and borrowing market.
 At $3.25 million against $145 million in unsecured obligations — and reported liabilities of $100 million to $500 million — the implied recovery for unsecured creditors through the sale alone is minimal. The liquidating trust contemplated under the Plan would need to generate meaningful avoidance-action or litigation-fund proceeds to close that gap.


Keyrock SA has been declared the "Successful Bidder" for certain assets of Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and its affiliated debtors.
 
The acquisition comes months after Keyrock raised a Series C round led by SC Ventures, Standard Chartered's venture capital arm, at a $1.1 billion valuation.
 Whether Keyrock's assumed liabilities — which the filing describes as "certain liabilities" — include any portion of blocked customer deposits is the dispositive post-sale question for customer claimants. The § 363(f) "free and clear" mechanism would ordinarily strip successor liability for pre-petition claims, but customer-trust or bailment claims that attach to specific identified assets (rather than general unsecured claims against the estate) may survive a § 363 sale depending on how Judge Horan structured the order. That distinction — §§ 363(e) and (f) interplay with constructive-trust theory — is the same fault line litigated in Voyager Digital and Celsius, and it is live here.

### Genesis / DCG Wind-Down: Post-Effective-Date Litigation Posture

Genesis crossed its August 2, 2024 plan effective date and is now a wind-down debtor under Case No. 23-10063 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Sean Lane). The estate-versus-DCG litigation is the live risk for creditor recovery beyond the base plan. 
The docket now includes Genesis Global Holdco, LLC et al. v. Digital Currency Group, Inc. et al., Adv. Pro. No. 26-03230
 — a 2026-originated adversary proceeding against DCG, confirming that post-confirmation litigation against the corporate parent is active. Additional adversary proceedings cataloged on the Kroll site include: 
Genesis Global Capital, LLC v. Insider Defendants, Adv. Pro. No. 25-01097
; 
Genesis Global Capital, LLC v. Digital Currency Group, Inc., Adv. Pro. No. 24-01312
; and DCG v. Genesis Global Cap., Adv. Pro. No. 25-01129, suggesting DCG itself filed a cross-claim or independent adversary post-confirmation.

The structural result from plan confirmation remains significant precedent for this beat. 
The decision allows creditors to receive in-kind distributions of cryptocurrency assets based on the present value of the assets they loaned to Genesis, rather than being limited to the value as of the petition date. As a result of the decision, Genesis is authorized to distribute all of its assets to creditors, even if those assets appreciated in value after the petition date.
 
Genesis reached a $2.2 billion settlement with Gemini, resulting in the full in-kind repayment of more than 225,000 Gemini Earn creditors; a $175 million settlement with FTX resolving billions of dollars in complex claims; and a $33 million settlement with Three Arrows Capital for over $1 billion in disputed claims.
 
DCG did not appeal.
 The non-appeal is significant: it locked in the in-kind distribution precedent without appellate reversal risk, leaving it as the strongest authority for any crypto-insolvency plaintiff arguing against petition-date dollarization.

The contrast with FTX is stark and worth maintaining in the analytical frame. FTX creditors are recovering on a petition-date-dollar basis: 
repayments have been calculated using the U.S. dollar value of crypto assets at the time of the November 2022 bankruptcy filing. Some creditors have objected to this method, arguing that cryptocurrency prices, especially Bitcoin, have risen sharply since then.
 Genesis creditors, by contrast, received in-kind crypto distributions that captured post-petition appreciation. The doctrinal divergence turns on Genesis's "distribution principles" — a negotiated creditor allocation framework that Judge Lane accepted — versus the standard § 502(b) dollarization applied in FTX. BlockFills and any future institutional-crypto debtor will face the same fork.
