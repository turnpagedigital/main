### I. *Monsanto v. Durnell*, No. 24-1068 (U.S. 2026) — FIFRA Preemption Ruling Reshapes Roundup Docket


On June 25, the Supreme Court sided with Monsanto in a high-stakes dispute over cancer warnings on pesticide labels, ruling 7-2 that state lawsuits aimed at holding Monsanto liable for failing to warn consumers about the potential risks of Roundup exposure are barred by the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act.
 
Justice Kavanaugh delivered the opinion of the Court.
 
The Court held that FIFRA expressly preempts the state-law failure-to-warn claim, reasoning that EPA's approval of Roundup's label — without a cancer warning — constitutes a federal labeling requirement, and that FIFRA's preemption clause prohibits states from imposing labeling requirements "in addition to or different from" federal requirements, making Missouri's cancer-warning requirement preempted.



The ruling centers on a lawsuit brought by Missouri resident John L. Durnell, who alleged that his non-Hodgkin's lymphoma resulted from exposure to Roundup. A Missouri jury had previously awarded Durnell $1.25 million after finding in his favor on failure-to-warn claims.
 
The Court reversed the judgment of the Missouri Court of Appeals and remanded for further proceedings.



Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joined by Justice Neil Gorsuch, dissented, arguing that the majority misread the statute and that adding a cancer warning would not conflict with federal law. She warned the ruling broke from the near-unanimous view of state and federal courts that had previously rejected the same preemption argument, and that it left an injured plaintiff with no remedy.


The settlement implications are immediate. 
The decision intersects directly with the separate effort to wind down the Roundup litigation: Monsanto has proposed a $7.25 billion nationwide class settlement to resolve current and future claims from people diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and a hearing on final approval is scheduled for July 9.
 
Bayer has already paid more than $10 billion in prior verdicts and settlements and still faces roughly 67,000 active claims.
 The structural tension is significant: the preemption ruling materially weakens plaintiffs' BATNA heading into July 9, but class counsel can argue it makes the $7.25 billion settlement fund — with 21-year funding — more valuable on a risk-adjusted basis precisely because future trial recoveries just got harder. 
The settlement also faces practical problems beyond payout numbers: health care plans object that the release may improperly wipe out lien or subrogation rights, which could create a collateral fight over who gets paid from the settlement fund.


On the venue front: 
Bayer won a fight that brings the company closer to getting Missouri state court approval for the proposed $7.25 billion settlement, after a federal judge in St. Louis rejected a bid by opponents to derail the settlement by transferring the case to a federal judge in San Francisco who had expressed skepticism of the deal, sending it back to state court for approval.
 That San Francisco judge is Judge Vince Chhabria, who 
previously rejected Bayer's offer to settle future Roundup class-action lawsuits for $2 billion, saying it wasn't enough.
 Routing final approval through Missouri state court — away from Chhabria — is therefore a meaningful strategic win for Bayer, independent of the *Durnell* ruling.

The claims-made denominator math cuts both ways for recovery counsel: the preemption ruling removes the asymmetric leverage that failure-to-warn verdicts provided, but the opt-out deadline of June 4, 2026, has already passed. 
The proposed class deal would allow future claimants to submit claims for compensation but gives Monsanto the right to walk away if too many people opt out.
 Whether the number of timely opt-outs triggers that walk-away right is a live factual question; class counsel has a strong incentive to demonstrate opt-out rates stayed below any contractual threshold before July 9.

### II. Social Media Addiction MDL, No. 4:22-md-03047-YGR (N.D. Cal.) — AG Trial Pretrial Ruling and Bellwether Track


On June 26, a California federal judge overseeing an upcoming trial over states' claims against Meta in the social media addiction multidistrict litigation said she will likely deny most requests from both sides to limit trial evidence, calling the requests overbroad and criticizing Meta's "shocking" and "ridiculous" number of sealing requests.
 The presiding judge is 
Hon. Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in the Northern District of California, MDL 3047 (Case No. 4:22-md-03047-YGR).
 
Discovery matters are overseen by Judge Peter H. Kang.



At a May 27 hearing, Judge Gonzalez Rogers laid out plans to empanel an eight-member advisory jury panel in August to help her decide claims from state attorneys general against Meta Platforms, while expressing concerns that the states had not disclosed their specific damages demands yet.
 The AG claims are being tried to an advisory jury — a structural choice that reserves the ultimate merits determination for the court, not the jury, and limits appellate exposure on the damages award.

The broader docket context is significant. 
The MDL includes more than 2,407 active cases as of March 2026, brought by individuals, school districts, and attorneys general from 42 states.
 
The Delaware Superior Court ruled in March 2026 that Meta's own insurers, including Hartford Casualty Insurance Company, are not obligated to defend child safety claims, reasoning that the claims arise from intentional design choices and are therefore not covered by standard Commercial General Liability policies.
 The insurance coverage denial means any damages awarded flow directly to Meta's balance sheet — a fact that concentrates settlement pressure on the company.

The state bellwether track has already produced a liability verdict. 
On March 25, 2026, a Los Angeles Superior Court jury returned a $6 million verdict against the remaining defendants in K.G.M. v. Meta & YouTube — $3M compensatory (Meta 70% / Google 30%) plus $3M punitive.
 
Meta and YouTube have asked the Los Angeles Superior Court judge to set aside the $6 million verdict.
 On the federal school-district bellwether track, 
the first federal MDL bellwether — Breathitt County School District (Kentucky) — was set for trial June 15, 2026 before Judge Gonzalez Rogers.
 
Judge Gonzalez Rogers selected six school districts — from Maryland, Georgia, Kentucky, New Jersey, South Carolina, and Arizona — as the first wave.
 The outcome of the school-district bellwethers will directly inform settlement posture for 
the nearly 800 other school-district cases pending in MDL-3047 and the broader personal-injury docket.


The sealing dispute flagged in Friday's ruling deserves focused attention: if Judge Gonzalez Rogers forces disclosure of internal Meta communications about platform design and minor-user engagement as trial exhibits, those documents become public record and cross-pollinate into the state AG track, the school-district track, and the parallel JCCP 5255 proceeding in Los Angeles before Judge Carolyn B. Kuhl.

### III. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Awaiting Final Approval Order

The $1.5 billion settlement in *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC* — before 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín
 in the Northern District of California — remains under submission. 
At a May 14 hearing to approve the $1.5 billion agreement, there was little indication that Judge Martínez-Olguín would not give her blessing to the deal. After a 75-minute hearing, the judge declined, for now, to approve the settlement but given the tone, most observers expect final approval to come relatively quickly.


The post-hearing briefing schedule is tight. 
By no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic was permitted to file a supplemental brief of no more than 2 pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 
Plaintiffs were directed to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.
 The tracked-case ground truth confirms two new docket entries were filed June 25, 2026 — their substance is unconfirmed from public sources at this writing, but the cadence is consistent with the court circulating a draft final approval order for comment or issuing ancillary administrative directions.

The claims-rate picture is the single most important number for per-work recovery. 
Class counsel filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3 percent rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10 percent).
 
The high rate of claim means that the forecasts about a bigger payout based on the March 19 report of 54 percent will not happen, and the actual payout will likely end up being closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.
 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work in the class will be $3,100.


Fee exposure matters for net fund value. 
Authors alleging Anthropic pirated their work urged Judge Martínez-Olguín to grant final approval to the $1.5 billion settlement, along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 The fee reduction from $300M to $187.5M is a meaningful concession — it represents a lodestar compression that class counsel would not have made without judicial signaling. At $187.5M on a $1.5B fund, the effective fee percentage is 12.5%, well below the 25% Ninth Circuit benchmark, which suggests counsel may have accepted a lodestar-based cross-check rather than a percentage-of-fund award.


Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 Claimants should note that payment will not be immediate even upon final approval; 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved. There is currently no exact timeline, but payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest.

## Recommended Actions

Claimants and recovery counsel should treat the *Durnell* ruling as a structural inflection point for the Roundup docket: the preemption holding under FIFRA, 7 U.S.C. § 136v(b), eliminates the failure-to-warn theory that generated the largest individual jury awards (including the $2.1 billion Georgia verdict Bayer separately settled), and it materially reduces the walk-away value for any plaintiff who opted out of the $7.25 billion class settlement before the June 4 deadline; those opt-out claimants should confer with counsel immediately about their remaining viable theories — design defect and manufacturing defect claims are not directly addressed by *Durnell* and may survive, but the circuit landscape is now adverse. For *Bartz v. Anthropic* rights-holders, the 91.3% claim rate is locked; the operative variable now is whether the final approval order issues before any objector files a notice of appeal — a successful appeal would delay the September 25, 2026 and September 25, 2027 Anthropic installments and could push first distribution well into 2027 or beyond. Counsel tracking the social media MDL should monitor what Judge Gonzalez Rogers's June 26 pretrial ruling forces into the public record: documents that survive her denial of sealing requests become available for use in state AG proceedings, insurance-coverage litigation (particularly given the Delaware Superior Court's March ruling denying Hartford Casualty's defense obligation), and the school-district bellwether trials; the evidentiary spillover is one of the most consequential secondary effects of her bench rulings.
