# 💰 $1B+ Class Actions & Mass Arb | Monday, July 27, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The dominant development since last week's advisory:** 
On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the landmark $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 That closes the pleadings chapter — but opens the distribution phase, where the real denominator math plays out. Everything else tracked below carries forward from prior posture: the Visa/Mastercard interchange second distribution window approaching in September, BCBS payments in ongoing rollout, the Purdue/Knoa trust structure now operational, and the Keller Postman Google arbitration campaign accumulating claimants ahead of Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling.

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Final Approval Entered; Distribution Clock Starts (N.D. Cal. 3:24-cv-05417)

The July 20 final approval order is the primary event of this cycle. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín issued a 23-page order that grants final approval, resolves outstanding administrative details, and rules on attorneys' fees.
 On the merits of the settlement, 
the court called the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," ruled that it "was negotiated at arm's length," and overruled all 53 objections filed opposing the settlement on various grounds.


The fee award is the most consequential discrete ruling in the order. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, later trimmed to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million.
 
$122 million of the settlement money will go toward attorneys and litigation costs in total
 — a meaningful departure from the 25% benchmark that class counsel originally anchored. 
By rough calculation, the approved fee works out to approximately $2,953 per hour across the 34,381.6 hours of attorney time the court credited.
 The lodestar cross-check, in other words, produced a significant downward cut from the percentage-of-fund request — directly increasing the net distributable pool.

On claims economics: 
the court reported a claims rate of 92.77 percent.
 That figure is exceptionally high for a content-creator class action and compresses the per-claim upside that lower-filing-rate settlements typically generate for active claimants. 
The settlement covers claims for approximately 440,490 works downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror, paying about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works
 — figures that track the Bloomberg reporting cited in the order. 
Class counsel separately reported 350 valid opt-outs, representing 1,802 works, or approximately 0.03% of the total number of works.
 The opt-out population is negligibly small, which forecloses meaningful collateral attack from that flank.

The non-class-member objection posture warrants attention for downstream litigation strategy. 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but took care to remind those individuals that, precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.
 
That is already happening — the *Chicken Soup for the Soul* and *Carreyrou* suits are separate, individual actions — and the court's order may prompt others to follow.
 The final approval order thus functions as a roadmap for individual litigants: Judge Martínez-Olguín's articulation of the liability perimeter effectively signals what Anthropic's exposure looks like outside the settled class.

On distribution timing: 
the Authors Guild looks forward to the commencement of the distribution process but notes that a specific date for payouts has not been announced.
 
Any disputes that cannot be resolved by mutual agreement with the settlement administrator will be submitted to the Special Master, and all submissions should remain confidential and under seal.
 The absence of a distribution date is a practical gap rights-holders should monitor — the Special Master dispute-resolution track is the next procedural milestone.

### II. *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation* — Second Distribution Approved for September 2026 (E.D.N.Y. MDL No. 1720)

The interchange fee track moved forward in June. 
On May 26, 2026, Class Counsel filed a motion requesting court approval of a second initial distribution of settlement funds; on June 15, 2026, the court approved the motion, and the second initial distribution is anticipated to occur in September 2026.
 This is a partial distribution — 
claims included for payment are limited to the population detailed in Class Counsel's motion for the second initial distribution.


The broader settlement posture: 
on June 9/10, 2026, a federal judge signed off on a new deal struck by Visa and Mastercard with merchants who brought suit more than 20 years ago, aimed at resolving claims that the networks unlawfully worked together to set the interchange fees merchants pay when customers use Visa or Mastercard credit and debit cards.
 
Judge Brian Cogan in Brooklyn said the accord offers broader relief than a proposal the court rejected in June 2024 and granted preliminary approval.
 
Approximately 12 million merchants in the class action would be covered.


The merchant objector campaign remains an open appellate risk. 
Within hours of preliminary approval, merchant lobbyists were citing "next steps," while analysts predicted an appeal.
 
Analysts project the settlement may not be fully finalized until 2029 while legal cases continue.
 The September 2026 second initial distribution proceeds under MDL No. 1720 for the already-approved earlier settlement tranche; the newly preliminary-approved 2026 structure runs on a parallel track toward final approval hearings yet to be scheduled. Merchants who accepted Visa or Mastercard between January 1, 2004 and January 25, 2019 and filed claims by the February 4, 2025 deadline are in the distribution pool; 
an appeal to the final-approval order was filed
 — that appellate record is worth monitoring before treating any payout as final.

### III. *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation* — Distribution Underway (N.D. Ala. MDL 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP)


The initial round of payments from the $2.67 billion settlement fund began distribution in May 2026, approximately 13 years after the cases were consolidated as MDL 2406 in 2013 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama.
 
A motion for distribution of settlement funds was filed on April 23, 2026 and approved by the court on April 24, 2026; according to the approved order, payments were to be issued on or around May 11, 2026.


Distribution mechanics: 
the total subscriber settlement fund is $2.67 billion; after court-approved attorney fees of 25% (approximately $667.5 million) and administration costs of roughly $100 million, approximately $1.9 billion remains for distribution to roughly 6 million claimants.
 
Average payout is approximately $333.
 
Payments are calculated using a pro rata formula: each claimant's total premiums paid, divided by total premiums of all approved claimants in their fund pool, multiplied by the available fund amount.
 The small per-claimant average is a direct function of the high claims volume against a fixed fund — the structural inverse of *Bartz*, where a 92.77% claims rate compresses per-claimant recovery from the other direction.

The combined BCBS antitrust resolution is materially larger when the provider track is aggregated: 
combined with the separate $2.8 billion provider settlement that received final approval on August 19, 2025, the total BCBS antitrust resolution exceeds $5.47 billion.
 Claimants who have not yet received payment should note the rolling distribution structure — 
payments are running in waves
 and secondary tranches are processing claims requiring additional verification.

### IV. *In re Purdue Pharma L.P.* — Knoa Pharma Operational; Trust Distributions Pending (Bankr. S.D.N.Y.; *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*, 144 S. Ct. 2071 (2024))

The Purdue reorganization is now past its effective date. 
U.S. Bankruptcy Judge Sean Lane formally approved Purdue Pharma's Chapter 11 plan of reorganization in November 2025, mandating the Sackler family to pay up to $7 billion over 15 years.
 
The settlement means that Purdue's manufacturing operations transferred to Knoa Pharma LLC, which will be overseen by a board of directors who had no prior connection to Purdue.


The opt-in release mechanics are the structural innovation of this plan — the direct legislative response to *Harrington*: 
creditors who affirmatively elected to release the Sackler Released Parties by the March 1, 2026 deadline receive additional distributions from "Direct Claims Settlements"; creditors who did not opt in retain their direct claims against the Sackler Released Parties; the mechanism replaced the nonconsensual third-party releases that the Supreme Court invalidated in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*
 
Practitioner commentary has described the design as a template for post-*Harrington* mass-tort plans.



Most of the money is to go to government entities to fight the opioid crisis, while a pool of approximately $850 million is designated for individual victims.
 
Money will be placed into a series of trusts to be distributed once necessary state court proceedings conclude; Connecticut, for example, anticipates receiving its first payment in fall 2026.
 Individual victim claimants who did not opt in to the Sackler releases retain civil claims but face a longer, more uncertain recovery path.

### V. Keller Postman v. Google — Mass Arbitration Scaling; Ad TOS Revised (AAA; JAMS)

The Google advertiser mass arbitration campaign is the largest active mass arbitration in the U.S. market by claimed exposure. 
Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman, as of May 11, 2026, is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist Keller Postman has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability.


The two liability anchors: 
federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the DOJ, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
Any company that purchased ads on Google on or after August 2016 may qualify for the arbitration process.


The contractual architecture is distinctive and creates a compelled arbitration dynamic with its own pressure mechanics: 
arbitration clauses Google has in its advertisers' contracts effectively prevent damages via class action litigation; instead, advertisers can join together to make claims as part of a group arbitration, which is usually handled by a single law firm and gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations.
 
The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most group legal proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.


Google's response signal: 
in May 2026, following Keller Postman's campaign launch, Google updated its Ads Terms of Service, with the firm's update rationale implicitly acknowledging the arbitration pressure.
 TOS revisions in the wake of a mass arbitration filing are a recognized indicator of a defendant moving toward settlement posture — the same pattern preceded resolution of the TurboTax and Amazon Alexa campaigns that Keller Postman ran previously.

Third, for BCBS (N.D. Ala. No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP): distribution is in rolling waves; late-stage claimants with address or banking verification pending should contact JND Legal Administration at 888-681-1142 or info@BCBSsettlement.com to avoid exclusion from secondary tranches. Va. ad tech case.
