### DELTA: Bartz v. Anthropic — Final Judgment Entered; Distribution Clock Now Running

The principal development in this system for the week is no longer prospective. 
On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 The case caption is *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.). The docket is available at CourtListener ID 69058235. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín issued a 23-page order that grants final approval, resolves several outstanding administrative details, and rules on attorneys' fees.


The fee award is the most consequential near-term variable for net recovery. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then trimmed the request to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million.
 
The court also trimmed the requested service awards for the three named plaintiffs — Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson — from $50,000 to $15,000 each.
 
Every dollar not paid in fees stays in the non-reversionary fund for authors.
 The court's restraint on fees — shaving roughly $86 million off the final ask — incrementally improves per-work recovery.

On claims participation: 
440,490 of 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a 91.3 percent rate.
 
Bloomberg reported the settlement pays "about $3,100 per author" for more than 480,000 works, with claims filed for 440,490 works downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror.
 
In aggregate, this decision marks the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77 percent.
 The spread between the 91.3 and 92.77 percent figures reflects different denominators (works vs. participating rightsholders); both figures confirm a participation rate roughly nine times the typical class action benchmark.

The court's reasoning on adequacy tracks the § 107 fair-use uncertainty squarely. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín stated that the settlement "provides meaningful relief to the Settlement Class given the reasonable range of Class Members' possible recoveries, especially since further litigation would likely be complex, expensive, lengthy, and risky."
 That framing is material for collateral proceedings: it is an explicit judicial acknowledgment that the fair-use defense retained colorable merit. 
Prior to class certification, Judge William Alsup had held that AI companies can use legally purchased books for training, not illegal ones, stating "Anthropic had no entitlement to use pirated copies for a central library."
 The final judgment does not adjudicate infringement; 
Anthropic, in agreeing to the settlement, denied any wrongdoing, and the infringement question was not adjudicated.


**Distribution mechanics and the payment waterfall** are now the operative monitoring points. Per the settlement agreement's payment schedule (IPG timeline document), Anthropic's installment structure runs: (1) an initial $300 million tranche due within 30 days of the final approval order; (2) a $450 million payment (Schedule Payment 1) due September 25, 2026 — twelve months after preliminary approval; and (3) a $450 million payment (Schedule Payment 2) due September 25, 2027. 
There is currently no exact timeline for commencement of payouts, but payments will likely begin in the late fall at the earliest. Anthropic is paying into the $1.5 billion fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.
 The non-reversionary structure means any unclaimed residual flows back to the class rather than to Anthropic — a meaningful protection given the 7.23 percent of works that went unclaimed.

Two categories of post-judgment risk warrant monitoring: **objector appeals** and **non-class-member litigation**. 
The court overruled all 53 filed objections.
 Any objector seeking to challenge the final approval order before the Ninth Circuit must file a notice of appeal within 30 days of entry of judgment (Fed. R. App. P. 4(a)(1)(B)), placing the appeal window at approximately August 19, 2026. 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members but reminded those individuals that, because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims. That is already happening — the Chicken Soup for the Soul and Carreyrou suits are separate, individual actions.
 Non-class-member plaintiffs who filed, or will file, their own infringement actions against Anthropic face a more difficult posture on the § 504 statutory damages calculus, since Alsup's earlier training-data ruling will be available as persuasive authority.

### DELTA: Concord I — Second Amended Complaint Filed July 22; MSJ Hearing Set October 21

The music-publisher track against Anthropic accelerated materially this week. 
Concord Music Group, Universal Music Publishing Group, and ABKCO Music filed a Second Amended Complaint on July 22, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California — an expanded version of the amended complaint first put forward in May — updating the case they originally brought in October 2023, which covers approximately 500 songs.
 The operative case is *Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.), assigned to Judge Eumi Lee.

The substantive posture is cross-MSJ pending. 
Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord, and ABKCO had asked Judge Lee to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrights and to reject the AI company's fair-use defense; the motion for partial summary judgment was filed on March 23 in the Northern District of California, the latest step in the case brought in October 2023 over the alleged infringement of 499 copyrighted musical works.
 
The motion argues that Anthropic's own admissions and evidence gathered during the case leave no material facts in dispute on key issues, and is accompanied by a 47-page statement setting out 218 "undisputed facts" supported by deposition testimony, internal Anthropic documents, and the company's own admissions.


The publishers' theory on market harm is analytically significant for the fourth factor of the § 107 four-part fair-use analysis. 
The filing states that when Claude outputs lyrics in response to user requests, "Anthropic provides the same service to its users as Publishers' licensees, without Publishers' authorization or any restrictions on that use," and cites data showing AI-generated songs are increasingly flooding streaming platforms, with a Deezer study estimating over 60,000 AI-generated tracks submitted to the platform daily by January 2026, accounting for 3 percent of total streams.
 On the training-data admission: 
the filing states that Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works" and "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."


The MSJ hearing is confirmed for October 21, 2026 before Judge Lee. That date governs the fair-use merits determination on Concord I and, by extension, the threshold issues in Concord II.

**Concord II** is the more expansive parallel action. 
The second, separate suit, filed in January 2026, covers more than 20,000 songs and seeks over $3 billion over Anthropic's alleged torrenting of lyrics from pirate "shadow" libraries.
 
Anthropic moved to stay Concord II — formally *Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK — pending final resolution of Concord I, 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK.
 
Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues governing these claims; those threshold issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — will determine the viability of most if not all of publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect.


The financial context materially sharpens the stakes. 
Anthropic confidentially filed for an initial public offering in June 2026, days after a $65 billion funding round valued the company at $965 billion — lifting it above rival OpenAI for the first time — and the company's run-rate revenue crossed $47 billion in May.
 A defendant with that balance sheet and IPO ambitions faces intensified pressure to resolve the music-publisher track before it feeds into a pre-IPO S-1 risk-factor disclosure.

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Judgment Entered; Distribution Clock Running

The most important administrative event on this beat occurred on July 20. 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 
The 23-page order grants final approval of the class action settlement, resolves several outstanding administrative details, and rules on attorneys' fees.


The order is unambiguous on the merits standard. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín called the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," ruled that it "was negotiated at arm's length," that "the relief provided for the Class is adequate," and that "the proposed Settlement treats Class Members equitably relative to each other." She overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds.


On the fee question — the order's most contested section — the court cut class counsel significantly. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then trimmed it to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million in fees.
 The full cost structure from the Ars Technica-hosted order PDF (CourtListener Dkt. 681, filed July 21, 2026) is: 
$1.5 billion plus interest paid by Anthropic or accrued in the Settlement Fund; expense reimbursement of $2,635,197.46; a cost reserve of $18,220,000 in anticipated expenses; and service awards of $15,000 for each of the three class representatives, totaling $45,000.
 
The court will withhold 10 percent of class counsel's fee award until the parties file a post-distribution accounting in accordance with the Northern District's Procedural Guidance for Class Action Settlements.


The economics at the class level are now fixed. 
*Bartz v. Anthropic* is "the largest copyright class action settlement in US history," paying "about $3,100 per author" across more than 480,000 works, with claims filed for 440,490 works downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror by Anthropic.
 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate and 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works — or .03% of the total number of covered works.
 That participation rate is extraordinary: 
440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works were claimed, dwarfing the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed on March 19, 2026, eleven days before the March 30 claims deadline.


**Distribution timeline.** The controlling payment trigger is the Ninth Circuit appeal window. 
Under the Settlement Agreement, payments begin one business day after the "Effective Date," defined as the date on which the time to file or notice an appeal of the Final Judgment expires; the Ninth Circuit generally allows 30 days to appeal.
 The 30-day window runs from July 20, placing the appeal deadline at approximately August 19. 
Assuming neither side appeals, funds should begin going out in a little over a month.
 
There is currently no exact timeline for commencement of payments, but Anthropic is paying into the $1.5 billion fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.


**Objector and opt-out landscape.** 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but reminded those individuals that, because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.
 
That is already happening — the Chicken Soup for the Soul and Carreyrou suits are separate individual actions — and the court's approval order may prompt others to follow.
 Rights-holders whose works were not registered with the Copyright Office under § 411's prerequisite, or whose titles did not appear in the LibGen/PiLiMi corpora, sit entirely outside this settlement and retain their 17 U.S.C. § 504 statutory-damages exposure against Anthropic.


Prior to class certification, Judge William Alsup held that AI companies can use legally purchased books for training, not illegal ones, stating: "Anthropic had no entitlement to use pirated copies for a central library."
 That ruling shaped the settlement's framing — Anthropic's $1.5 billion exposure was bounded by the pirated-corpus perimeter, not a universal training-data liability. Rights-holders outside that perimeter pursuing fair-use challenges against legally licensed but unlicensed-for-AI-training corpora face a harder doctrinal path.

Concord Music Group, Universal Music Publishing Group, and ABKCO Music filed a Second Amended Complaint on July 22, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California — an expanded version of the amended complaint first filed in May, updating the case originally brought in October 2023, which covers approximately 500 songs.
 
The publishers have since narrowed the case to direct infringement and alleged removal of copyright management information, dropping earlier contributory and vicarious infringement claims.


The docket is dense. Per CourtListener, Dkt. No. 5:24-cv-03811 (last accessed July 28, 2026): Anthropic filed a notice supplementing its response to Fact No. 149 in Plaintiffs' MSJ separate statement on July 21; 
a Case Management Conference is set for September 2, 2026 at 3:00 PM before Judge Eumi K. Lee.
 
The cross-MSJ hearing is scheduled for October 21, 2026 before Judge Lee.


The briefing posture heading into that October 21 argument is fully joined. 
The publishers moved for partial summary judgment in March 2026, asking the court to reject Anthropic's fair use defense ahead of trial; Anthropic is contesting the case and has argued that training Claude on copyrighted material amounts to fair use.
 
Anthropic has asked the court to find its use "transformative" fair use, comparing Claude's learning to how humans learn from reading and internalizing themes of various works.


The publishers' § 107(4) market-harm submission is direct. 
On market harm, the publishers' filing states that when Claude outputs lyrics in response to user requests, "Anthropic provides the same service to its users as Publishers' licensees, without Publishers' authorization or any restrictions on that use," and cites a Deezer study estimating over 60,000 AI-generated tracks were being submitted to that platform daily by January 2026, accounting for 3% of total streams.
 On the training-data admission: 
Anthropic "admits at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works" and "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."


The amicus coalition is notably broad. 
The RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, SONA, BMAC, the Music Artists Coalition, and the Artist Rights Alliance filed an amicus brief on March 30 urging the court to reject Anthropic's fair use defense.
 
A second amicus brief, filed by the Association of American Publishers, News/Media Alliance, the International Association of Scientific, Technical & Medical Publishers, and the Authors Guild, makes similar arguments from the perspective of book, news, and academic publishing industries.


**Concord II exposure.** This case is separate from, and substantially larger than, the first. 
The publishers' second, larger lawsuit against Anthropic, filed in January 2026, covers more than 20,000 songs and seeks over $3 billion in statutory damages.
 Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending resolution of Concord I's summary judgment; 
Concord I had completed fact and expert discovery and was approaching summary judgment on threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — that will determine the viability of most if not all of the publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect.


**Why this matters for the *Bartz* settlement as a pricing comparator.** The $1.5 billion *Bartz* figure resolves pirated-book training at approximately $3,100 per work. Concord I involves lyrics reproduction in Claude's outputs — a distinct, and arguably more commercially proximate, infringement theory. The publishers' § 107(4) submission and CMI claims sit outside the book-training fair-use framework that shaped *Bartz*. If Judge Lee rules for the publishers on partial MSJ — finding infringement and rejecting fair use — the Concord II $3 billion statutory-damages ask becomes a live litigation floor, not a negotiating posture. 
Anthropic confidentially filed for an IPO in June 2026, days after a $65 billion funding round valued the company at $965 billion — lifting it above rival OpenAI for the first time — and its run-rate revenue crossed $47 billion in May.

### III. *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein / Mag. Judge Ona T. Wang) — Discovery Active; NYT Sanctions Motion Filed


The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein and referred to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, pending in the Southern District of New York as No. 1:25-md-03143.
 
The consolidated action combines 16 copyright lawsuits.


The January 2026 discovery order remains the operative benchmark for the docket. 
In January 2026, Judge Stein affirmed Magistrate Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce 20 million de-identified ChatGPT logs — a landmark discovery ruling.
 The relevance theory is § 107(4) market harm: 
Judge Wang found that even output logs without reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's fair use defense, and logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal patterns relevant to whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works.


The NYT front has escalated. 
In July 2026, *The New York Times* filed a new motion requesting sanctions against OpenAI.
 
The filing requests that the judge impose legal sanctions, which could result in financial penalties or procedural rulings favoring plaintiffs; the publishers argue that OpenAI has failed to fully produce documents and information requested.
 Discovery sanctions in copyright cases — adverse inference instructions under the § 107 fair-use framework — could be materially dispositive: an adverse-inference instruction on training-data comprehensiveness would undercut OpenAI's § 107(1) transformativeness argument at summary judgment.

The circuit split that will ultimately resolve the doctrinal question is now in motion. 
Three U.S. judges have ruled on AI training and fair use — Alsup (Bartz), Chhabria (Kadrey), and Bibas (Thomson Reuters v. Ross) — with Alsup and Chhabria finding for AI companies on training, and Bibas finding against; the Third Circuit's first appellate ruling, argued June 11, 2026, is pending.
 The Third Circuit outcome in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* will be the first appellate word on AI training fair use in any U.S. circuit, and its reasoning under § 107 factors one and four will directly inform Judge Lee's MSJ analysis in Concord I and Judge Stein's summary judgment posture in the OpenAI MDL.

### IV. Systemic Observations: Settlement-Pricing, Licensing Comparators, and the Post-*Bartz* Litigation Landscape

Three structural points are now cleanly in view.

**First, the *Bartz* final judgment sets a floor, not a ceiling.** The settlement resolved liability for pirated-corpus training at roughly $3,100 per registered work. That figure reflects the Alsup/Chhabria fair-use rulings limiting liability to the piracy nexus. Cases premised on output reproduction — Concord I and II, NYT v. OpenAI, Authors Guild v. OpenAI — assert a different and commercially more proximate theory: not merely that copyrighted works were ingested during training, but that the model *reproduces and distributes* substantial portions on demand. The per-work value in a successful output-reproduction case would not be bounded by the $3,100 Bartz reference.

**Second, the 91.3% claims rate is a data point for damages calibration.** 
440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a participation rate far above the typical class-action average.
 Rights-holders who opted out (350 valid opt-outs, 1,802 works, 0.03% of the class) retain independent § 504 statutory-damages exposure against Anthropic ranging from $750 to $150,000 per work, subject to willfulness findings. The court's reminder that non-class-member objectors are free to bring their own claims creates a separate layer of tail exposure.

**Third, Anthropic's IPO posture creates near-term settlement pressure in Concord.** 
Anthropic confidentially filed for an IPO in June 2026.
 Unresolved material litigation is a S-1 disclosure obligation. A company filing to go public with a $3 billion-plus statutory-damages case unresolved and a cross-MSJ hearing scheduled for October 21 has structural incentives to negotiate before the offering window opens. Rights-holder counsel should treat the October 21 MSJ hearing date as an implicit negotiating deadline.
