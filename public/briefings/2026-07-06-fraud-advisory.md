### I. BLMIS / Madoff — Picard Trustee (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., No. 08-01789-LGB)

The seventeenth pro rata interim distribution commenced February 27, 2026. 
The SIPA Trustee distributed more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims, bringing the aggregate amount sent to eligible customers to nearly $15.38 billion, which includes approximately $850.9 million in advances made by SIPC.
 
The seventeenth distribution represented 1.302% of each allowed claim and was paid on claims relating to 768 accounts; when combined with the prior 16 distributions, approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim will have been paid, unless that claim has been fully satisfied.


Fee posture is a pricing signal. 
On February 13, 2026, the SIPA Trustee and BakerHostetler filed the 50th Interim Fee Application covering work from August 1, 2025 through November 30, 2025, seeking approval for approximately 63,774.40 hours of professional and paraprofessional services billed at an average public-interest-discounted rate of approximately $588.88 per hour.
 
No funds recovered in the Madoff Recovery Initiative are used to pay those costs — all trustee, legal, and accounting fees, as well as administrative expenses, are paid by SIPC.
 That structural insulation of the Customer Fund from direct fee drag is meaningful for secondary-market claim pricing: net recovery per allowed dollar is not eroded by ongoing BakerHostetler billings, which SIPC absorbs.


As of June 26, 2026, SIPC has committed approximately $850.9 million to the BLMIS liquidation; those advances continue to increase as litigated claims are resolved; SIPC is reimbursed once each respective customer claim is fully satisfied; and $272.4 million in reimbursement has been received, leaving $578.5 million in SIPC advances outstanding.
 The residual advance balance indicates a significant tail of claims not yet fully satisfied — relevant for anyone modeling when SIPC reimbursement activity concludes and what that means for any remaining distribution cadence.

The court is now Judge Lorna G. Schofield; the case docket caption has moved from the earlier "CGM" designation to "LGB" on recent filings. Claims investors should update their monitoring designations accordingly.

### II. Stanford Financial Receivership — Janvey (N.D. Tex., Dallas Div., SEC v. Stanford Int'l Bank)

The Stanford receivership has entered its terminal distribution phase after more than 16 years. 
On March 9, 2026, Receiver Ralph S. Janvey filed with the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, Dallas Division, the Schedule of Payments to Be Made to Eligible Investor CD Claimants Pursuant to the Final Distribution Plan, in accordance with the October 8, 2025 Order Approving the Final Distribution Plan and the November 25, 2025 Order Approving Joint Motion to Amend.
 
Payments under the Final Distribution Plan began on April 3, 2026, per the Receiver's update posted April 6, 2026.


A secondary asset tranche remains unresolved. 
As of March 11, 2026, claimant group COViSAL sent a second formal written request to the Receiver asking about the status of $8.1 million in Swiss assets released to the Receiver on December 17, 2025; as of the April filing, the Receiver had not publicly responded to that request, and it was unclear whether those funds would be included in a future distribution separate from the current Final Distribution Plan.
 For claims investors, this Swiss tranche represents unquantified upside: if distributed, it flows into the existing plan waterfall; if it requires a supplemental distribution order, the timing extends further.

The overall recovery context: 
the $14.9 billion Stanford judgment — the largest single component of the SEC's $17.9 billion in FY2025 monetary relief — itself reflects the court's entry of a final judgment in the Stanford case, which originated in 2009.
 The gap between that judgment figure and actual distributable cash recovered underscores the structural problem in fraud-adjacent receiverships: judgment size and realized distribution are very different animals.


The receivership site also reflects that the court has granted the Receiver's motion for authorization to dispose of records and equipment
 — a late-stage administrative signal that the Receiver is moving toward wind-down.

### III. OneCoin / Ruja Ignatova — DOJ Remission + Multi-Jurisdictional Asset Recovery

**Claims window closed June 30, 2026.** 
The DOJ announced the beginning of the remission compensation process for victims of the international OneCoin investment scheme; between 2014 and 2019, co-founders Ruja Ignatova and Karl Sebastian Greenwood orchestrated a large international cryptocurrency fraud that operated through a global MLM network from Sofia, Bulgaria; victims invested over $4 billion worldwide in the fraudulent cryptocurrency.
 
The DOJ launched this remission process with over $40 million in forfeited assets available for distribution.
 The $40 million in available forfeited assets against $4 billion in stated investor losses represents a recovery ratio below 1% — meaningful primarily as a partial restitution signal and as a floor on what multi-jurisdictional tracing has yielded so far in the U.S. channel.


Kroll Settlement Administration LLC is serving as the remission administrator; the FBI and IRS-CI conducted the criminal fraud investigation.
 
U.S. Attorney Jay Clayton directed victims to obtain petition forms at www.onecoinremission.com.
 The claims deadline has now passed. Victims who missed the June 30 cutoff face an uncertain path to any subsequent tranche; there is no announced second window.

The deeper recovery story runs through European channels. 
Following international legal cooperation between Guernsey and Germany, on January 16, 2026, the Royal Court of Guernsey ordered the registration and enforcement of an overseas forfeiture order against funds totaling £8,590,200.92, held for the benefit of two Guernsey-registered shell companies — Abbots Holdings Property Limited and Abbots Property Limited — through which Ignatova acquired two freehold flats in Kensington, London.
 
German proceedings found that OneCoin was released with fraudulent intent; criminal proceedings against Ignatova could not conclude because her whereabouts remain unknown; and the German authorities accordingly initiated "Independent Confiscation Proceedings" that produced the overseas forfeiture order.
 This is a non-conviction-based asset forfeiture — operationally significant because it does not require Ignatova's arrest or a completed criminal proceeding in Germany.


Frank Schneider, a former deputy chief of Luxembourg's foreign-intelligence service who advised Ignatova through his firm Sandstone, fled French house arrest in May 2023, was returned, and in April 2026 the Nancy Court of Appeal sided with Washington's extradition request; he has indicated he will appeal.
 Schneider's extradition trajectory is the most active pressure point in the consultant-liability chain; if extradited and prosecuted in the Southern District of New York, he could be a significant source of cooperation testimony and potentially a clawback defendant.

Ignatova herself remains on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted list. The SDNY indictment (unsealed October 2017) remains live; no confirmed sighting or arrest has occurred.

### IV. SEC Enforcement Posture — Structural Context for the Next Wave of Receiverships

For claims investors watching pipeline risk, the Atkins-era SEC matters. 
In April 2026, David Woodcock took the reins as Director of Enforcement; his first public remarks outlined a traditional enforcement agenda focused on high-impact cases involving investor harm, market integrity, and core fraud priorities, with specific emphasis on offering fraud, accounting and disclosure fraud, market manipulation, insider trading, private funds, and cross-border fraud.



The current leadership is reorienting the program away from what it characterizes as headline-driven, high-volume enforcement activity toward a "back to basics" approach to fraud, investor protection, and individual accountability; Woodcock's appointment followed the abrupt departure of his predecessor, Judge Margaret Ryan, on March 16, 2026.


The practical implication: the SEC's prioritization of offering fraud and cross-border fraud under Woodcock is consistent with continued receivership appointments in cases targeting retail investors — the Ponzi scheme pipeline does not slow under either enforcement philosophy. What does shift is that 
the SEC filed 456 enforcement actions in FY2025 (a 22% decrease from FY2024) and returned $262 million to investors, down 24% from the prior year.
 Lower volume with a retained fraud focus means fewer but larger receivership appointments. Claims pricing in new SEC receiverships should account for the probability that fewer competing claimant groups will be driving early distribution motions.

The ongoing question is disgorgement authority. 
Investor harm is expected to remain in sharp focus through FY2026, as the U.S. Supreme Court has been slated to decide *SEC v. Sripetch* on whether the SEC has the ability to seek disgorgement as a penalty without showing investors suffered financial harm.
 If the Court narrows disgorgement to require a demonstrated investor-harm nexus, the effective monetary relief available in Ponzi receiverships where investor harm is structural (i.e., all net winners received stolen principal) is unlikely to be affected — but the ruling could constrain peripheral theories used to sweep in remote facilitators.

## Recommended Actions

**For claim holders and secondary-market buyers:** The OneCoin DOJ remission window closed June 30, 2026 — if you represent any victim who failed to file at www.onecoinremission.com before that date, assess immediately whether the SDNY MLAT or parallel German/Guernsey recovery channels offer any alternative restitution pathway; the Bielefeld-to-Guernsey forfeiture mechanism (non-conviction-based, under German independent confiscation proceedings) is a live template for tracing further Ignatova assets without requiring her arrest. In the Stanford receivership, press the Receiver's team for a definitive written position on the $8.1 million Swiss tranche before any secondary-market transaction closes — that asset is material at the margin and its disposition is genuinely unclear. In the BLMIS estate, note that the 17-distribution aggregate (72.848% of allowed claims) leaves approximately 27% of principal outstanding; the next distribution will require a fresh motion, allocation order, and court approval cycle of roughly 90–120 days from the date the Trustee amasses sufficient recoveries to justify the motion; the 50th BakerHostetler fee application signals the case remains active. For the Power Block Coin docket (Bankr. D. Utah, 24-23041), request copies of Docket Entries 518 and 519 from CourtListener or direct docket access to determine whether a retention application, adversary complaint, or sale motion has been filed — the absence of a public description does not mean the entries are immaterial. Under the SEC's current enforcement posture, monitor the *SEC v. Sripetch* disgorgement ruling (Supreme Court, argued April 20, 2026) as a potential constraint on recovery in cases where the SEC-as-plaintiff theory relies on disgorgement without particularized investor-harm evidence.
