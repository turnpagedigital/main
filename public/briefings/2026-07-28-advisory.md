# 🤖 LLM / Copyright | Tuesday, July 28, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The dominant development since last advisory: final approval entered.** 
On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the landmark $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 The shift in posture is now decisive — this matter moves from litigation into administration. The NPR wire that circulated yesterday surfaces the first sustained wave of class-member sentiment, and it is notably mixed. The implications for the broader AI-copyright system are more consequential than the headline number suggests, for reasons addressed below. Parallel matters — the music-publisher cross-MSJ at *Concord/UMG/ABKCO v. Anthropic*, the *In re OpenAI* MDL in S.D.N.Y., and the Third Circuit's pending decision in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence*, No. 25-2153 — each carry distinct fair-use exposures that the *Bartz* settlement resolves nothing about.

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval, Fee Award, and Distribution Timeline

**The order.** 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín issued a 23-page order granting final approval of the class action settlement, resolving several outstanding administrative details, and ruling on attorneys' fees.
 
The court called the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," reaffirmed that it "was negotiated at arm's length," and overruled all 53 objections filed opposing the settlement on various grounds.


**Fee award.** The fee question was the order's most litigated element. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then later trimmed it to $187.5 million; the court approved an approximately $101.5 million payment to the attorneys.
 
The court will withhold 10 percent of class counsel's fee award until the parties file a post-distribution accounting in accordance with the district's Procedural Guidance for Class Action Settlements.
 That 10% holdback — roughly $10.15 million — is structurally important: it creates a performance obligation that keeps class counsel engaged through final distribution.

**Claims rate and opt-outs.** 
The claims rate, which stood at 91.3% as of the April 30 update, rose to 92.77% by the May 14 fairness hearing. Counsel stated that opt-outs remain modest: 350 valid opt-outs covering 1,802 works, with a handful of late opt-outs received after the deadline.
 
The agreement requires Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to the authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from notorious pirate sites, as well as destroy all original files of works torrented and downloaded from those sites and any copies that originate from the torrented copies.


**Per-work economics.** 
*Bartz v. Anthropic* is "the largest copyright class action settlement in US history," paying "about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works," with claims being filed for 440,490 works that were downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror by Anthropic.


**Distribution timeline.** 
The Authors Guild looks forward to the commencement of the distribution process; while a specific date for the commencement of payouts has not been announced, the expectation is that this will happen quickly.
 Per the pre-approval distribution timeline, 
summer 2026 is the earliest projected payment window if objectors appealed but no appeals were filed.
 The critical open question — not yet publicly resolved as of this advisory — is whether any of the 53 overruled objectors has filed or intends to file a 9th Circuit notice of appeal within the standard 30-day window following entry of final judgment on July 20. An appeal would toll distribution. Rights-holder counsel should monitor the docket at CourtListener ID 69058235 for any notice of appeal through approximately August 19, 2026.

**Non-class members.** 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but reminded those individuals that, precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims. That is already happening — the Chicken Soup for the Soul and Carreyrou suits are separate, individual actions — and the court's order may prompt others to follow.

### II. The NPR Reaction Wire — What the Class-Member Sentiment Tells the Market

Yesterday's NPR story is factually significant beyond its headline. 
Anthropic agreed to settle after a federal judge ruled last year that it had used the contents of millions of digitized copyrighted books to train the large language models behind its chatbot, Claude, without seeking the consent of or compensating the authors of those works.



The case could be seen as more of a win for the AI companies than for the publishing industry — because within the narrow terms of this particular lawsuit, the court did not find it illegal for Anthropic to train its AI algorithms on authors' copyrighted works, as long as the company pays for the books it uses.


That framing deserves unpacking for claims-recovery counsel. Judge William Alsup's underlying fair-use ruling — which preceded settlement and served as the liability anchor — 
held that training Claude on legally acquired books was fair use, specifically "quintessentially transformative," while maintaining pirated copies from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror in a permanent digital library for unspecified future uses was not fair use regardless of what those copies were eventually used for. The settlement covers the second category.
 Anthropic therefore exits this litigation without any adjudicated finding that LLM training on lawfully acquired copyrighted text is infringing under 17 U.S.C. § 107. That is the precedential gap that the NPR reaction piece is circling, and it is precisely the gap that the music publishers and news organizations are pressing in their separate actions.

The named plaintiff's economics confirm the friction at the individual level. One plaintiff stated he is entitled to approximately $3,100 per qualifying work — 
but has mixed feelings about the last two-plus years spent fighting Anthropic, citing "a lot of travel, a lot of discussion about what to do and how to proceed and a lot of jobs passed up," adding "I'm much poorer for this settlement, ironically."
 For claims-recovery investors evaluating future opt-out positions in analogous classes, this cost-of-participation data is directly relevant to rational opt-out calculus in successor cases.

### III. *Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC* — Cross-MSJ Pending; Music-Publisher Track Remains Open

The tracked-case ground truth flags this matter as cross-MSJ pending, with docket sync awaiting. The last confirmed public filing remains the Anthropic cross-motion for summary judgment on fair use, filed April 22, 2026. No resolution has been announced. 
Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO Music filed a $3.1 billion lawsuit against Anthropic on January 28, 2026, alleging Anthropic built Claude AI on a foundation of torrented piracy.



The music publishers allege that Claude generates copyrighted song lyrics when prompted, proving that copyrighted lyrics were in training data. The case tests a narrower question: even if training is arguably fair use, does the AI's ability to reproduce copyrighted content on demand constitute infringement?
 
Settlement discussions were reported but no resolution was reached as of April 2026.


The *Bartz* settlement is structurally unhelpful to Anthropic's position here. The Alsup training-is-fair-use holding covers lawfully acquired books; the music case turns on output reproduction — lyric verbatim recall on demand — which is a distinct § 107(4) market-substitution theory. Output-as-infringement is analytically separable from training-data provenance, and the music publishers have invested significant resources in developing that distinction. The cross-MSJ outcome in the M.D. Tenn. / N.D. Cal. track will be the next major inflection point for this beat.

### IV. *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-3143-SHS (S.D.N.Y.) — MDL Discovery Phase


In April 2025, the U.S. Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated twelve cases against OpenAI and another defendant originating in the Southern District of New York and the Northern District of California. The MDL encompasses class actions originally filed in California by authors, New York suits brought by news organizations, DMCA-focused suits, and a case brought by an online video creator. The panel found that these cases involve common questions of fact regarding the defendants' use of copyrighted works, without consent or compensation, to train their LLMs.
 
The consolidated matter was assigned to the Southern District of New York and aims to streamline infringement claims against OpenAI and provide some consistency in rulings.



The MDL — captioned *In re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-3143-SHS-OTW — encompasses sub-matters including *Baldacci, et al. v. OpenAI, Inc. et al.* and *Authors Guild, et al. v. OpenAI, Inc. et al.*, No. 1:23-cv-08292-SHS-OTW.
 The MDL's discovery posture is the key monitoring point: training-data disclosure orders in this forum will generate comparator evidence on § 107(4) market-harm that transfers directly to *Concord* and the news-publisher track.

### V. *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence, Inc.*, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.) — First Federal Appellate Argument on AI Training Fair Use

This is the most consequential pending appellate development in the AI-copyright system. 
Ross has gone from a Delaware courtroom to the Third Circuit and is on track to be the first federal appeals argument in the country on whether training an AI system on copyrighted material qualifies as fair use. The Third Circuit heard argument on June 11, 2026, in No. 25-2153.


Background: 
In February 2025, Judge Stephanos Bibas ruled against Ross at the district level, finding that using copyrighted material in AI training was not transformative under fair use doctrine and therefore constituted infringement — the first US ruling of its kind.
 
The court granted partial summary judgment for Thomson Reuters on its claims of direct infringement and fair use, while denying Ross's defenses — finding that Ross infringed on 2,243 Westlaw headnotes — and upheld the validity of Thomson Reuters's copyrights.



The appeal turns on two core questions: whether Westlaw's headnotes are copyrightable at all (they summarize judicial holdings, which are public documents), and whether internal use of those headnotes as training data might qualify as transformative use despite the district court's contrary finding.


The Third Circuit's ruling — expected in the coming months, given that argument closed June 11 — will be the first appellate precedent on AI training fair use in the United States. A decision affirming Bibas would harden the liability exposure across the entire AI-training docket. A reversal on transformativeness would provide the defendants in *In re OpenAI MDL* and the music-publisher suits their strongest § 107(1) hook to date. Either outcome will land with force on the *Concord* cross-MSJ briefing and any future settlement discussions in the news-publisher track. 
The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling is pending after oral argument on June 11, 2026.

### VI. System-Level Framework: What *Bartz* Does and Does Not Settle

The *Bartz* final approval order is as instructive for what it does not resolve as for what it does. 
Anthropic, in agreeing to the settlement, has denied any wrongdoing, and the infringement question has not been adjudicated. The settlement does not create any precedent that is binding on future courts, who may one day find that such use is non-infringing.



The major AI copyright training data lawsuits in 2026 are moving toward a fact-specific market-harm test, not a blanket rule that all training is legal or illegal. *NYT v. OpenAI*, *Getty v. Stability AI*, *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence*, and *Concord Music v. Anthropic* each test a different part of the same question: when does AI training or output substitute for the market the copyright owner controls?


The § 107 factor-four market-harm inquiry will, in practice, be decided case-by-case on the nature of the copyrighted work, the degree of output substitution, and the extent to which the defendant has disrupted an existing or nascent licensing market. 
The licensing side matters: major AI companies have pivoted to licensing agreements, with OpenAI signing deals with the Associated Press, Axel Springer, and multiple publishers
 — each deal creating a comparator data point that rights-holders will use to demonstrate the existence and value of the market Anthropic and OpenAI allegedly displaced.

Internationally, the same pressure is building. 
In Germany, the Munich Regional Court rejected a fair-use equivalent for AI training in *GEMA v. OpenAI* under German copyright law.
 That ruling, while not binding in U.S. courts, reinforces the § 107(4) argument that a viable licensing market exists — which is exactly the argument that defeats an AI company's transformativeness defense under the Campbell framework.
