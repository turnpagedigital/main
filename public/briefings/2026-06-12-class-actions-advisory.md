# 💰 $1B+ Class Actions & Mass Arb | Friday, June 12, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**This morning's delta is dominated by two hard-breaking developments:** U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan in Brooklyn granted preliminary approval on June 9 to Visa's and Mastercard's revised $38 billion settlement with merchants who accused the card networks of charging too much to process payments on their credit cards — the largest antitrust settlement in the twenty-one-year history of *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 1720, No. 05-md-01720 (E.D.N.Y.); and a group of farmers filed a motion in a North Carolina federal court on June 11 seeking preliminary approval of an $85 million settlement with Corteva Inc. to resolve antitrust claims that the company used loyalty rebate programs to artificially extend its patent monopolies over certain pesticides. Together, the two filings push more than $38 billion in fresh settlement capital into the pipeline this week, and both carry unresolved appellate and structural risks that recovery counsel must track immediately.

### I. Visa / Mastercard Interchange — MDL 1720: Preliminary Approval Granted; Final Approval Path Now Opened

U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan in Brooklyn said the settlement covering more than 12 million merchants was "fair, reasonable, and adequate," and that he was likely to eventually grant final approval. Cogan ruled nearly two years after a different judge rejected a proposed $30 billion settlement as too small; the settlement announced last November was intended to end litigation that began in 2005, when merchants accused Visa, Mastercard and banks of conspiring to violate U.S. antitrust laws, including through the collection of "swipe fees."

The economic terms of the revised accord are materially different from the rejected 2024 proposal. The latest settlement called for Visa and Mastercard to lower swipe fees by 0.1 percentage point for five years, while standard consumer rates would be lowered to no more than 1.25% for eight years; merchants would get more options to impose surcharges on customers and could choose whether to accept categories of cards — including commercial cards, premium consumer cards, and standard consumer cards — a provision supporters said would end the "Honor All Cards" rule. For context, interchange fees totaled $118.8 billion for Visa and Mastercard in the United States in 2025, up from $111.2 billion in 2024; the average fee was 2.36%. Plaintiffs' experts, including Nobel Prize-winning economist Joseph Stiglitz and University of Washington professor Keith Leffler, said the changes could save merchants $38 billion by 2031 and provide $224 billion of benefits overall, including to consumers.

Judge Cogan's written rationale is instructive for appellate risk mapping. While Cogan acknowledged that some of the objections offered by merchants who opposed the settlement had merit, he said his decision turned on a comparison to outcomes possible by trial: "But the question is not whether the Amended Settlement constitutes the best possible recovery, end stop — it's whether the Amended Settlement constitutes the best possible recovery in light of what can be gained and lost through trial." "None of the objectors have persuaded the Court that they will, or even can, get more through trial."

Structural opposition remains intense. Some groups including the National Retail Federation, the world's largest retail trade group, also opposed the new settlement and plan further challenges. NACS has stated it will appeal to the Second Circuit U.S. Court of Appeals if Cogan grants final approval. Walmart and other objectors called the changes illusory because merchants would still have to "honor all issuers" in a given network — meaning, for example, they could not accept cards from one bank and reject cards from another. The case's procedural history — the $30 billion settlement would have lowered swipe fees by 0.07 percentage point over five years; in rejecting that accord in June 2024, U.S. District Judge Margo Brodie said fees would have still been above where they were absent any antitrust violations, and merchants would remain stuck with the "Honor All Cards" rule — remains the key analytical frame for whether the Second Circuit, if it takes a merits appeal, would find Cogan's approval sustainable.

Preliminary approval is a procedural checkpoint, not a final ruling. The court will now notify class members, solicit objections, and eventually hold a fairness hearing before deciding whether to grant final approval. Approximately 12 million merchants in the class action would be covered, generally meaning businesses that accepted Visa or Mastercard during the period covered by the case as defined in the settlement and court-approved notices; eligibility can also depend on settlement-specific rules, and some merchants may be excluded if they previously opted out or otherwise fall within defined exclusions. Separately, the MDL docket's Rule 23(b)(3) damages tranche remains on a parallel track: on October 30, 2025, the Court approved Rule 23(b)(3) Class Plaintiffs' Motion for Initial, Partial Distribution of Settlement Funds from the earlier damages settlement, meaning the claims-payment apparatus is already operational for counsel monitoring distribution timing.

### II. Corteva Pesticide Antitrust MDL — $85M Preliminary Approval Motion Filed, M.D.N.C.

The second major breaking development arrives from the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina. Farmers have asked a North Carolina federal judge to preliminarily approve an $85 million settlement with Corteva Inc. to resolve antitrust claims that the company used loyalty rebate programs to artificially extend its patent monopolies over certain pesticides — specifically, per the underlying FTC and farmers' complaints, arrangements alleged to have been "cutting off" competition and allowing the companies to "inflate their prices and force American farmers to spend millions of dollars more for their products"; the FTC lawsuit alleged Syngenta has a monopoly on the fungicide azoxystrobin and the herbicides mesotrione and metolachlor, while the suit cites Corteva's herbicides rimsulfuron and acetochlor and the insecticide and nematicide oxamyl.

The settlement path was hard-fought. Corteva took part in mediation conferences on March 10, 2026, and May 14, 2026, according to the report of a mediator filed in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina on May 29, 2026; the mediator told the court a settlement had been reached only with Corteva, and Corteva had until June 10, 2026, to file a motion for preliminary approval. Co-defendant Syngenta has not settled. The underlying private class action gained momentum after the Federal Trade Commission and attorneys general from California, Colorado, Illinois, Iowa, Indiana, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon, Texas and Wisconsin filed a separate lawsuit in September 2022 alleging the companies violated the Sherman Act and its antimonopoly provisions. The FTC's parallel enforcement track — which involved an October 2026 trial date that Corteva had previously sought to block — alleged the company, along with Syngenta Crop Protection, paid distributors to block competitors from selling less-expensive generic pesticide products to farmers; Corteva filed a motion in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina arguing a trial could not be set until the court resolves pending summary judgment and other motions. With the private settlement now filed, the FTC's litigation posture against Corteva specifically may shift, though Syngenta's exposure remains fully live for trial. Claimant farmers who purchased the named pesticide products during the class period should immediately assess inclusion eligibility before the opt-out window opens following the preliminary approval hearing.

### III. Pay-to-Play in Securities Class Actions — Fee Objector Challenge Resurfaces

A third matter from this week's scan merits immediate practitioner attention. A brewing showdown over legal fees has the makings of a steel cage grudge match, securities law-style, with Ted Frank, co-founder of the non-profit Hamilton Lincoln Law Institute, who has built a reputation objecting to the terms of class action settlements that he says overcompensate lead counsel — this time renewing pay-to-play claims against a public pension fund over class action lead-plaintiff fees. The pay-to-play concern — in which pension funds secure lead-plaintiff appointments and attendant litigation monitoring fees from plaintiffs' firms in exchange for directing class action business — sits at the intersection of PSLRA § 21D(a)(3)(B)'s "most adequate plaintiff" presumption and ERISA fiduciary duty doctrine. For institutional investors tracking their recoveries from securities class actions, the pendency of this dispute is a flag: courts that scrutinize fee arrangements at the lead-plaintiff stage may also revisit fee-sharing arrangements at distribution, and pension fund beneficiaries whose plans are class members should confirm their counsel has documented independence from any contingent referral or monitoring-fee agreement.

### IV. Bartz v. Anthropic — Final Approval Pending, N.D. Cal.

The briefing desk carries forward the AI-copyright class action *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.), before Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín. Per the tracked-case ground truth, the docket last moved on June 4, 2026 (entry 674 — Order to Withdraw as Attorney Granting Motion No. 669), with no new filings in the 72 hours prior to this run. Settlement final approval remains pending. The Bartz matter is the leading indicator for class-wide copyright recovery economics in the LLM training-data vertical: the settlement structure — if approved — will establish the per-work or per-author unit-recovery floor against which subsequent plaintiff classes (e.g., those pursuing OpenAI, Meta, Google, and Mistral on comparable training-data theories) will calibrate their demands. Rights-holders with pending claims or potential claims against AI developers should monitor the final approval hearing date and the claims-administration website (Anthropic Copyright Settlement Administrator) for filing deadlines. Counsel pursuing parallel LLM copyright theories in separate actions should study the Bartz notice and claims form closely: any overly broad release language in the settlement class definition could foreclose recovery against non-Anthropic defendants if a class member fails to opt out.

### V. Big-Tech Mass Arbitration — Google Advertiser ($218B), Apple App Store, Meta Waiver Track

The mass-arbitration vertical continues to mature structurally. The Keller Postman–coordinated Google advertiser campaign — premised on Sherman Act § 1 and § 2 theories targeting Google's Search Ads and display-advertising auction mechanics, with aggregate claimed damages in the $218 billion range — remains the largest single mass-arbitration exposure in the pipeline. The AAA commercial filing fee mechanics established post-*Viking River* and post-*Coinbase* remain operative: each claimant's individual arbitration demand triggers per-case fees that, at scale, impose nine-figure cost exposure on defendants before any merits award issues. The strategic question being litigated in multiple district courts — whether a defendant can obtain a federal court stay of mass arbitration on the ground that the arbitration demands themselves are improper — remains unresolved at the circuit level and is a live appellate issue in the Ninth and Second Circuits.

On the Apple App Store front, the post-*Epic* structural reforms (mandatory alternative payment link-outs, 27% commission cap on non-Apple purchases by court order, N.D. Cal. No. 4:20-cv-05640-YGR, Hon. Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers) have not fully closed the mass-arbitration exposure: separate consumer claimants who paid App Store prices during the pre-reform period continue to pursue individual arbitration claims. The Meta waiver-track arbitration campaign — targeting Facebook's historical tracking-pixel data monetization — similarly continues to generate per-case AAA/JAMS demand filings, with Meta's procedural defenses (waiver of arbitration by protracted litigation, unconscionability of the arbitration clause under California law) having seen mixed results across different judicial officers.

Amazon seller arbitration remains active in the W.D. Wash. (Hon. John H. Chun, No. 2:21-cv-00694-JHC and related filings), where the question of whether Amazon's Individual Seller Services Agreement arbitration clause is enforceable against sellers who signed agreements at different time periods — with different clause versions — is producing fact-specific rulings that complicate any one-size settlement model.
