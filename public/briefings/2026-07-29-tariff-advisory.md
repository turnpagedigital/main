## Analysis & Developments

**The Section 122 chapter closes; the Section 301 chapter opens on the same day.** 
The 150-day Section 122 surcharge under Proclamation 11012 expired at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, and USTR concluded its forced-labor investigations the same day, imposing new duties on products from all 60 investigated economies under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 That sequencing — one authority expiring, another activating within hours — is itself now the central theory of plaintiffs' opening briefs. 
Critics note that USTR opened 60 forced-labor investigations just three weeks after losing the IEEPA case, closed all 60 on the same day, and landed on a near-uniform 10%/12.5% rate structure covering roughly 99.4% of imports — with notices stating rates were set "in accordance with the specific direction of the President."
 The litigation timeline has reset, but the refund arithmetic at CBP has not: the IEEPA CAPE pipeline remains live, Phase 3 reliquidation orders are now in place, and the Federal Circuit Section 122 appeal (*State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) continues on a parallel merits track. The administration, meanwhile, is signaling a third Section 301 wave. Taken together, the week's developments require separate attention across four discrete risk registers: (1) the new Section 301 litigation exposure; (2) CAPE Phase 3 mechanics and the August 4 reporting deadline; (3) the Federal Circuit Section 122 merits briefing; and (4) USTR's industrial-overcapacity investigation that could produce an additional tariff tranche before year-end.

### I. *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. v. Greer* — Section 301 Challenge Filed at the CIT, July 24, 2026


The Liberty Justice Center filed *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. et al. v. Greer et al.* in the U.S. Court of International Trade on July 24, 2026. The complaint asks the court to declare the tariffs unlawful, prevent their enforcement, and preserve complete relief for plaintiffs and the proposed class, including refunds of all duties paid with interest.
 
Plaintiffs are Burlap & Barrel, a New York-based online retailer of single-origin spices sourced directly from smallholder farmers, and Collective Horology, a California retailer and distributor that supports independent watchmakers.
 The docket number has not yet been confirmed in published sources; parties and counsel should monitor PACER for assignment to a CIT judge.


The administration announced on July 23 that it will collect duties of between 10% and 12.5% on imports from most major trading partners, following USTR investigations into the alleged failure of roughly 60 economies to prevent forced labor in their supply chains.
 
The complaint in *Burlap & Barrel* alleges that USTR failed to explain "how each economy's particular practices burden or restrict United States commerce," relying instead on generalized assertions about forced labor in global supply chains. The case was filed as a proposed class action covering all importers of record who will pay the new tariffs.


A parallel suit — the second filed that day — was brought on behalf of Learning Resources and hand2mind. 
That suit was brought by toymaker Learning Resources, along with a flooring manufacturer and affiliated firms; Learning Resources was a party to the consolidated Supreme Court litigation in which the justices invalidated Trump's emergency-powers tariffs, though its own case was dismissed on jurisdictional grounds.
 
The challenge will move through the U.S. Court of International Trade.


The core statutory argument is a meaningful departure from prior rounds. 
The legal foundation for these tariffs — Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — has historically been used to target specific unfair trade practices by individual countries, not to impose sweeping duties across dozens of nations.
 
The complaint argues the tariffs violate the requirements of Section 301 and also run afoul of the major questions and nondelegation doctrines.
 
USTR Greer has defended the use of Section 301, arguing that the Supreme Court's February 2026 IEEPA decision itself pointed to Section 301 as proper authority.
 That argument is not frivolous — 
experts note that it may be tougher to successfully challenge the current round of tariffs than prior rounds, given that Section 301 was invoked to impose steep duties on Chinese goods in Trump's first term and survived court challenges.
 The government retains the structural advantage it exploited in the Section 122 appeal: filing an emergency stay motion the moment a CIT panel rules against it. 
One commentator observed that "the trade court struck [Section 122] down May 7, the government appealed May 8, and the Federal Circuit stayed the ruling within days."
 Clients should build that precedent into their litigation timeline projections.

### II. CAPE Phase 3 — CIT Orders Reliquidation for Finally Liquidated Entries; August 4 Report Due


On July 17, 2026, CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued an order directing CBP to "reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, any and all of Plaintiffs' entries that have been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which Plaintiffs made estimated deposits pursuant to IEEPA." The order follows the transfer to Judge Eaton's docket of more than 3,700 cases filed at the CIT seeking refunds of amounts deposited as IEEPA duties.
 
CBP will issue instructions to plaintiffs' counsel describing how to make the required submission; once received and followed, plaintiffs may submit a CAPE declaration. CBP is next required to file a progress report on Phase 3 entries on August 4, 2026.


The August 4 report is the next hard checkpoint for the Phase 3 refund track. On the aggregate pipeline: 
as of CBP's July 10, 2026 court declaration, $86.3 billion has been repaid to importers (including statutory interest) out of roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties collected across approximately 330,000 importers and 53 million entries.
 
$121.75 billion in claims had been accepted for processing as of July 10, up from approximately $90 billion in mid-June; $104.29 billion had been authorized as of the June 29 filing; and $86.3 billion had actually been repaid.
 
June 2026 alone saw $49.1 billion in refund payments — the largest single month in the program's history.
 
Approximately $80 billion remains unpaid.


Phase 3 is the complicating layer. 
Phase 3 covers finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period — and critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 
The federal government continues to contest the legal basis for refunds on finally liquidated entries; as a result, it remains unclear whether refunds will actually flow once technical capability is in place.
 Counsel representing importers with finally liquidated entries who are not already plaintiffs in a CIT action should treat that gap as urgent. The July 17 order, in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* (Court No. 25-00066) before Senior Judge Eaton, 
directed that CBP be ordered to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries ahead of CAPE Phase 3 across roughly 3,700 IEEPA cases before the court.

### III. *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Fed. Cir. Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) — Section 122 Appeal, Merits Briefing Active

Section 122 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) is now moot as a prospective collection matter — the 150-day surcharge expired July 24 — but the Federal Circuit appeal carries significant backward-looking stakes for every importer that paid the 10% surcharge under Proclamation 11012 between February and July 24, 2026.


On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805), consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs under Proclamation No. 11012 and Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 
While the appeal proceeds on the merits, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 duties as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — pending further order of the Federal Circuit.



The consolidated appeals will address the proper interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" under Section 122, the nondelegation arguments raised by private plaintiffs, and whether Proclamation No. 11012 was validly issued.
 
The core legal dispute is the interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficits": the CIT ruled that the government's reliance on modern trade-deficit and current-account data exceeded the narrow, historically rooted scope of the statute.
 
The Federal Circuit, in granting the stay, stated that it did not "state a final conclusion as to the correctness of the CIT's judgment" but that "the federal government has made a sufficient showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits."
 That language is a meaningful signal; it should temper expectations for a swift affirmance of the CIT's May 7 ruling on appeal.

The standing truncation from the CIT phase also limits the downstream refund class. 
The CIT's ruling did not result in a universal injunction but applied only to named plaintiffs; the majority of plaintiff states were dismissed for lack of standing entirely, and only the direct importer plaintiffs (Burlap and Barrel, Basic Fun) and the State of Washington as an importer had Article III standing.
 Importers who were not parties to the CIT action and have not filed § 1514 protests or CIT complaints under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) should assess their preservation posture now, before the Federal Circuit issues its merits decision and any attendant window for protective filings closes.

### IV. USTR's Section 301 Industrial-Overcapacity Investigation — Next Tariff Tranche in Formation


USTR Greer confirmed on July 27 that USTR is continuing a separate Section 301 investigation targeting excess industrial capacity in 16 key trading partners, including China, Vietnam, Mexico, and the European Union.
 
Greer stated, "We're going to finish up that investigation hopefully soon, and we'll make a proposal," adding that this could lead to additional tariffs.
 
Greer also said the Section 301 forced-labor duties — the tranche already in place — will "likely not have an economic impact" in part because the rates of 10% or 12.5% are similar to prior global tariff actions.
 
On the question of whether the new duties would affect Federal Reserve monetary policy, Greer stated: "Well, I don't think it has an impact at all."


That framing is worth registering for refund claimants: if the administration's litigation posture is that the Section 301 rates are economically equivalent to what was previously in place, it correspondingly reinforces plaintiffs' "same policy, different statute" argument. 
The process for refunds on IEEPA tariffs, meanwhile, was only recently standardized, and some businesses are still waiting for their money back.
 A second Section 301 wave aimed at overcapacity — covering China, Vietnam, Mexico, and the EU — would substantially expand the universe of duty exposure for clients with sourcing in those jurisdictions and could dwarf the forced-labor tranche in revenue terms if rates are set higher. No rates have been proposed; the investigation is ongoing. Monitor USTR's Federal Register notices for notice of proposed action and comment periods.

## Analysis & Developments

### I. Section 301 Replaces Section 122: Two CIT Challenges Filed July 24

The dominant development of the past 72 hours is the administration's successful execution of a statutory pivot and the immediate legal response to it. 
The Section 122 tariff expired by statute at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, 150 days after it took effect on February 24 — the maximum duration Congress allows without an extension.
 Hours before that expiration, the administration moved. 
On July 24, the President imposed Section 301 tariffs on more than 60 countries, with rates of either 10% or 12.5%, and numerous exemptions.
 
Section 301 tariffs, including the previously announced Brazil Section 301 tariff, apply to $949 billion worth of imports in 2026.


Two lawsuits were filed at the U.S. Court of International Trade in New York on July 24 — the same day the new duties took effect. 
The first, *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. et al. v. Greer et al.*, was filed July 24, 2026 in the Court of International Trade; it asks the court to declare the tariffs unlawful, prevent their enforcement, and preserve plaintiffs' and proposed class members' ability to obtain complete relief for all affected entries, including refunds with interest.
 The Liberty Justice Center — which previously defeated the administration before the Supreme Court in *V.O.S. Selections v. Trump* — represents 
New York-based Burlap & Barrel, an online spices retailer, and California-based Collective Horology, a retailer supporting independent watchmakers.



A second lawsuit — *Learning Resources, Inc. v. United States* — was filed by a group of several businesses with Learning Resources, Inc. as lead plaintiff; Learning Resources is the same toy manufacturer whose case was consolidated with the IEEPA litigation that ultimately gave its name to the Supreme Court decision.
 
The second suit was filed on behalf of seven businesses in total, also including hand2mind Inc., which was involved in the earlier IEEPA tariff litigation that reached the Supreme Court.


The legal theory in both complaints is essentially pretext: 
on March 12, 2026 — less than three weeks after the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize presidential tariffs — USTR initiated 60 investigations into whether the identified economies effectively prohibited imports made with forced labor, and on July 23, only hours before the new duties became applicable, USTR imposed tariffs on products from all 60 economies.
 
The final action retained essentially the same two-tier structure — 10% for certain economies and 12.5% for others — and USTR's notice repeatedly states that the rates, scope, and exemptions were selected "in accordance with the specific direction of the President."
 On the structural argument, 
Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 has historically been used to target specific unfair trade practices by individual countries, not to impose sweeping duties across dozens of nations.
 
Jeffrey Schwab of the Liberty Justice Center argues that Section 301 does not include any authority "to tax substantially all imports from substantially all countries at preestablished rates."
 
The complaints also allege the tariffs run afoul of the major questions and nondelegation doctrines.


No judge assignment or docket numbers have been published in the sources reviewed for this run. 
The lawsuits will move through the U.S. Court of International Trade.


### II. USTR Greer: "No Economic Impact" — And the Next 301 Investigation Is Already in Motion

USTR Greer's public posture this week is significant both for its substance and its litigation framing. 
On July 27, Greer stated that Trump's new tariffs on the 60 trading partners over their alleged lax enforcement of forced labor bans will likely not have an economic impact, in part because the rates of 10% or 12.5% are similar to recent global tariff actions.
 
He told Fox News that the new Section 301 duties are imposed on a smaller group of countries than the now-expired 10% temporary tariff, which was universal.
 The "no economic impact" framing is a two-edged sword in litigation: it may insulate the administration from emergency-stay motions (a plaintiff seeking a TRO on economic harm grounds faces a harder showing if the government's own top trade official argues the duties are economically neutral), but it simultaneously undermines the "forced labor threat" justification — a record problem the plaintiffs will exploit.


Greer confirmed that USTR is continuing to work on another broad Section 301 investigation targeting excess industrial capacity across 16 key trading partners — including China, Vietnam, Mexico, and the EU — and said "we're going to finish up that investigation hopefully soon, and we'll make a proposal," signaling additional tariffs ahead.
 
Greer defended use of Section 301 by pointing out that the Supreme Court itself referenced the statute in its decision striking down the IEEPA tariffs
 — a notable and deliberate record-building move for the inevitable appellate contest over the new 301 authority.

### III. Section 122 Expiration: Refund Math and Rate-Reset Consequences

The Section 122 expiration on July 24 is a hard line for refund-recovery counsel. 
On May 7, a divided CIT panel ruled the Section 122 surcharge unlawful, finding that the proclamation cited trade deficits and current-account shortfalls — not the specific balance-of-payments metrics the statute requires.
 That ruling prompted an immediate government appeal to the Federal Circuit, which issued a stay. The statutory expiration on July 24 mooted the Section 122 litigation prospectively — there is no live duty to enjoin — but the backward-looking question of whether importers who paid the Section 122 surcharge between February 24 and July 24 can recover those duties remains unresolved on appeal. Counsel tracking the Burlap and Barrel / State of Oregon Section 122 dockets should note that the government's appellate position is now purely about preserving the prospective validity of Section 122 as authority (which affects the 2026 trade architecture argument) rather than defending a continuing revenue stream.


On July 15, the President announced a 25% tariff on certain imports from Brazil — approximately $24 billion worth in 2026 — effective July 22.
 Brazil, separately, faces the new Section 301 rates. Importers with Brazil-origin goods are now facing layered tariff exposure from both Section 232 (steel/aluminum) and the new Section 301 framework; the interaction of these rate stacks requires entry-by-entry analysis.

### IV. IEEPA Refund Pipeline: CAPE Phase 1 Status and the Class-Certification Overhang

The CBP CAPE refund pipeline is the operational core for Turnpage clients awaiting IEEPA duty recovery. 
On April 20, 2026, CBP launched Phase 1 of the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system to process refunds.
 
CBP deployed Phase 1 of CAPE within the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE); a substantial number of filings have been submitted since the system opened in late April 2026, a significant number of entries have been accepted for refund processing with liquidation and refund issuance under way, although a subset of submissions has been rejected for failing entry-specific validations.
 
CAPE Phase 1 covers approximately 82% of IEEPA entries and/or duty deposits, including unliquidated entries; entries with liquidation statuses of "Suspended," "Extended," or "Under Review"; warehouse and warehouse withdrawal entries; and entries liquidated within the preceding 80 days. CBP has not yet announced the scope of CAPE Phase 2.


The aggregate refund data as of mid-July is materially important: 
CBP's July 13 court filing shows $86.3 billion repaid and $121.75 billion accepted as of July 10.
 Against the approximately $166 billion in total IEEPA duties collected, 
an analysis by the Cato Institute found that the $104.29 billion approved as of June 29 covered only approximately 30% of all IEEPA-affected import entries — meaning the dollars processed so far are concentrated in a small number of large, high-value entries, while many smaller importers remain stuck in the queue.
 
The process for IEEPA tariff refunds was only recently standardized, and some businesses are still waiting for their money back.


The class-certification question is the most consequential near-term litigation variable for smaller importers. 
Counsel who argued the original Supreme Court case have filed a class certification motion at the CIT seeking to represent importers who are not currently eligible for refunds under CAPE; the government is opposing certification.
 If certified, the class mechanism would extend the refund entitlement to the full universe of payers — not just those whose entries fall within Phase 1. 
Only the importer of record is eligible to obtain refunds directly from CBP
 under the existing CAPE architecture, which means beneficial cargo owners, non-importer-of-record parties, and assignees of refund claims face structurally different — and narrower — recovery paths absent class certification or a Phase 2 expansion.

In *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States*, Senior Judge Richard Eaton at the CIT issued the foundational series of orders directing CBP to process refunds; that docket remains the operative procedural vehicle for the refund pipeline. 
On March 4, 2026, the CIT ordered that "all importers of record" that paid IEEPA-based duties are "entitled to the benefit of" the *Learning Resources* decision and directed CBP to determine final tariff assessments "without regard to" the IEEPA duties.


### V. The Section 301 Litigation Trajectory: Lessons from Section 122 Playbook

The pace of the Section 122 litigation cycle is the most instructive precedent for what comes next. 
Section 122 was struck down by the trade court on May 7, the government appealed May 8, and the Federal Circuit stayed the ruling within days.
 Importers should assume an identical pattern for Section 301: a CIT ruling on the merits (likely 6–12 months from filing given briefing schedules), immediate government notice of appeal to the Federal Circuit, and an emergency stay application that will almost certainly succeed given the revenue magnitude. 
If the Section 301 tariffs advance to the Supreme Court, the legal process will take time — the IEEPA tariffs were argued in November 2025 and decided in February 2026.
 
Unlike IEEPA or Section 122, Section 301 tariffs carry no expiration date — if they materialize at the scale the administration intends, they could approximate the breadth of the original IEEPA tariffs, and on far more durable legal ground.


The structural asymmetry that matters for recovery planning: Section 301 tariffs do not expire by their own terms. Importers who paid IEEPA duties now have a clear, CBP-administered refund path. Importers paying Section 301 duties today have no equivalent path unless and until a court invalidates the authority *and* a refund mechanism is ordered — which, based on the IEEPA experience, took approximately two months from Supreme Court decision to CAPE Phase 1 launch, and is still not complete more than five months later.
