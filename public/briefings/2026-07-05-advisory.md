### Bartz v. Anthropic PBC, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Taken Under Submission; Distribution Calculations Running

The fairness hearing went forward on May 14, 2026 before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín at 450 Golden Gate Avenue, Courtroom 12, 19th Floor, San Francisco. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench and indicated short orders might follow imminently — but the docket's last substantive entry date reflects July 1, 2026, and the court has not publicly filed the final approval order as of CourtListener's July 2 sync.
 The posture today is therefore: **final order pending**.

The settlement's economic particulars were sharpened at the hearing. 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson reported that the per-work payout will be approximately $3,100, and that 92.77% of the works in the works list had been claimed.
 The 91–93% claim rate is structurally notable: 
440,490 of 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a claim rate the typical class action would reach only at around 10%.
 The fee structure going into the hearing had already been revised downward: 
class counsel urged final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million.
 The original ask had been $375 million (25% of fund); the revised figure represents roughly 12.5%.


The order following the hearing directed Anthropic to file, by May 21, 2026, a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; plaintiffs were simultaneously directed to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the April 30, 2026 proposed order.
 Seven objectors were heard at the hearing. 
The Judge heard from seven objectors, each of whom spoke for two minutes, with five minutes allotted to attorney James Bartolomei who appeared for multiple objectors.


The underlying liability architecture that produced this settlement is critical to understand for rights-holders evaluating downstream exposure. 
The settlement does not address AI training as such. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and that ruling is undisturbed by the settlement.
 What the $1.5 billion resolves is Anthropic's liability for downloading and storing works from Library Genesis (LibGen) and Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi) — i.e., piracy-based infringement under 17 U.S.C. § 504, not training-use infringement under § 107. 
If Judge Martínez-Olguín grants approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and pay the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.


The payment structure is staggered: 
Anthropic agreed to fund the settlement in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
The first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 
Payouts will only issue after final approval and resolution of any appeals; the Authors Guild notes that payments will likely not commence until late fall at the earliest.
 Settlement administration is handled by JND Legal Administration via AnthropicCopyrightSettlement.com. Publisher-versus-author allocation follows copyright law's legal/beneficial ownership split: 
where a current publisher still holds an exclusive license, the author and publisher split the $3,000 per work.


The operative risk for claims investors and rights-holder counsel at this stage is appellate: 
for many authors, the litigation around AI training is not ending but shifting to other cases — including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, and Adobe.
 Any objector who preserved appellate rights could delay distribution if the Ninth Circuit grants a stay pending appeal — the same mechanic that has frustrated earlier class action distributions in this district.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic PBC — Two Parallel Dockets, Both Before Judge Eumi K. Lee (N.D. Cal.)

The music-publisher track is now materially more complex than a single-front litigation. Two cases are pending before Judge Eumi K. Lee, both referred to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen for discovery.

**Concord I** — No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK: 
The first-filed action has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues, including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use.
 
Dispositive motions were to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026.
 The case was initially filed in the Middle District of Tennessee, then transferred to N.D. Cal., where 
Judge Lee entered a stipulated order on January 2, 2025 requiring Anthropic to maintain guardrails preventing Claude from outputting infringing lyric content — an interim win for the publishers.
 The publishers moved for partial summary judgment on the position that Anthropic's use of their lyrics was not fair use because, among other reasons, 
it used those lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic drew sanction scrutiny during briefing: 
she acknowledged using Anthropic's own Claude.ai tool to help draft an expert's declaration that included an erroneous citation, arguing the error was "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 The magistrate judge 
partially stricken an expert report that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Claude — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."


**Concord II** — No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK: 
Major music publishers filed a second, $3 billion suit against Anthropic in early 2026, asserting newly discovered mass infringement of sheet music and songbooks.
 Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending resolution of Concord I. 
Anthropic's motion to stay Concord II was set for hearing on May 20, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. in Courtroom 7 of the N.D. Cal. San Jose Division, on the basis that the threshold legal issues in Concord I — including the fair-use question — would control most if not all claims in Concord II, and that Concord I's summary judgment rulings could have preclusive effect.
 The outcome of that motion hearing is not confirmed in the dockets synced this run; it should be tracked on PACER for Concord II (No. 5:26-cv-00880).

For rights-holder counsel, the fair-use ruling in Concord I (when it arrives) will be the first federal merits decision on AI-training use of musical compositions under § 107 — and given the commercial-product framing the publishers have advanced under § 107(4), it has direct precedential weight for the § 107 analysis running in parallel at the Third Circuit.

### Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.) — First Federal Appellate Argument on AI Training Fair Use


The Third Circuit heard argument on June 11, 2026 in Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence Inc., No. 25-2153 — the first federal appellate argument on whether using copyrighted material to train an AI system can be fair use.
 The underlying district court ruling — entered February 11, 2025, by the District of Delaware — found that 
Ross Intelligence's actual copying of Westlaw headnotes was not fair use, with the court granting partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters.
 The appeal is proceeding under an interlocutory stay: 
on May 23, 2025, the district court granted Ross's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard"; in its September 2025 appeal brief, Ross argued the decision would have "sweeping consequences for innovation."


The § 107 four-factor analysis at the Third Circuit diverges from Alsup's Bartz ruling in a key respect. In Bartz, the legally-acquired/piracy distinction did the dispositive work — Alsup found training on lawfully acquired books was "quintessentially transformative" fair use, while piracy was not. In Ross, the framing is different: 
Thomson Reuters frames the case as a straightforward commercial copying to build a substitute, a framing that aligns with a judicial instinct that fair use should not bless a competitor's decision to take the plaintiff's protected editorial work after being unable to license it.
 Ross attempted a fresh doctrinal angle at argument: 
Ross argued that the Third Circuit's April 7, 2026 decision in American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes — involving access to standards incorporated into law — confirms that law-adjacent factual and functional works receive a fair-use analysis sensitive to public access and thin protection.


The Third Circuit's ruling will be the first appellate-level treatment of AI-training fair use under § 107. Whether it endorses or rejects the § 107(4) market-harm reasoning that drove the Delaware district court will directly shape how Concord I (music lyrics), the OpenAI MDL (news publishers), and every subsequent generative-AI training dispute frames the fourth-factor argument.

### Cross-Docket Fair-Use Framework — What Bartz, Ross, and Concord Mean Together

The three dockets now in active motion — Bartz (final approval pending, Ninth Circuit appellate risk), Thomson Reuters/Ross (Third Circuit argument June 11), and Concord I (N.D. Cal., fully briefed for summary judgment) — are developing the § 107 four-factor test along different factual corridors that practitioners cannot treat as interchangeable.

Alsup's Bartz training-use ruling (legally acquired books = fair use) rests heavily on the transformative-use prong of § 107(1) applied to generative-AI output. It does not address lyrics reproduction or direct competitive substitution. The Concord publishers' partial-summary-judgment motion puts § 107(4) front and center: 
the publishers argue fair use does not apply because Anthropic used those lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 That is the same fourth-factor commercial-substitution logic that produced the Delaware district court ruling against Ross — and which the Third Circuit is now reviewing. 
AI developers will focus on transformative use in generative-AI fair-use battles, but the commercial use and market-impact factors will continue to favor content owners over AI companies.


The Supreme Court's March 2, 2026 cert denial in Thaler v. Perlmutter — 
confirming that material must have human authorship to be copyrightable — forecloses AI-generated output as a copyright asset for AI companies, which reinforces their incentive to settle or license rather than litigate training-data exposure further.
