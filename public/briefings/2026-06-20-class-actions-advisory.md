### 1. Roundup — $7.25B Missouri Settlement: Federal Remand Clears the Deck for July Hearing, But Objectors Signal Appeal


U.S. District Judge Henry Edward Autrey in St. Louis sent Bayer's proposed $7.25 billion Roundup settlement back to Missouri state court on Wednesday, overruling objections from plaintiffs who argued that the state court had no power to implement a nationwide resolution of lawsuits claiming Roundup causes cancer.
 
Judge Autrey granted Bayer's motion, concluding that the objecting plaintiffs did not have the power to transfer the case to federal court, because only the defendant holds that right.


The jurisdictional skirmish had been running for four weeks. 
Objecting plaintiffs transferred the new class action to federal court on May 22, disrupting the fast-track schedule pursued by Bayer and plaintiffs' attorneys who support the settlement.
 That removal triggered the risk that the case would land before U.S. District Judge Vince Chhabria in San Francisco, who oversees all Roundup cases filed in federal court. 
A federal judge in St. Louis denied the bid by settlement critics to have the case sent to Judge Chhabria, who has blasted the settlement's terms as "mind boggling."


The stakes of the forum fight were not procedural theater. 
The ruling is likely to bolster Bayer's efforts to win approval of the sweeping settlement by restoring the case to the state court where the deal is being fast-tracked and avoiding the risk that the settlement would be reviewed by a different federal judge who has already criticized the deal.


On the approval calendar: 
Missouri state court Judge Timothy Boyer in St. Louis granted preliminary approval in March and had been set to consider final approval of the deal in early July.
 
Plaintiffs were given a June 4 deadline to decide whether they would opt out of the class-action settlement.
 The June 4 opt-out deadline has passed, meaning the class composition is now substantially fixed going into the final-approval hearing.


Dozens of people have objected to the settlement, and attorneys who oppose the deal said they would file more objections before the settlement approval hearing.
 
Objecting plaintiffs have appealed the ruling
 — the appeal is unverified as to docket number, but the remand creates a parallel appellate track that could delay or complicate the state-court schedule.

Structural context: 
the deal, announced February 17, 2026, is structured to be paid out over a 21-year period, representing Bayer's effort to exit a legal quagmire stemming from its 2018 acquisition of Monsanto.
 
Bayer faces approximately 65,000 lawsuits alleging Roundup caused non-Hodgkin lymphoma and other cancers.
 
The initial approval triggered a settlement provision requiring Bayer to pay $500 million into a fund within 10 days to cover costs such as notifying class members of the deal.
 
The company can back out if too many plaintiffs decline to participate.


The claims-rate and opt-out-rate data from the June 4 deadline have not yet been published in any source verified this run. Recovery-side advisors should watch that data closely: the denominator math on a $7.25 billion, 21-year payout over ~65,000 claims is sensitive to opt-out volume, and any significant walkaway rate could trigger Bayer's termination right.

### 2. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — $1.5B Settlement Under Submission; Final Approval Delayed; Follow-On Opt-Out Suits Filed

The $1.5 billion *Bartz v. Anthropic* settlement—the largest copyright class settlement in U.S. history—remains in legal limbo as of today. 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined to grant final approval to Anthropic's $1.5 billion deal with a class of book authors, demanding more detail on how the money will actually reach claimants and how much will go to plaintiffs' attorneys, leaving the landmark settlement in legal limbo just weeks before payouts were expected to begin flowing to writers.



The fairness hearing took place Thursday, May 14, 2026, before Judge Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement, class counsel's fee request, and the objections and replies filed by class members and others.
 
After the 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly.


Following the hearing, the court issued a structured briefing order: 
by no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic was permitted to file a supplemental brief of no more than 2 pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; by that same date, plaintiffs were required to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1).


On claims and fees: 
class counsel Justin Nelson reported the payout per work will be $3,100, with the class reflecting a 92.77% participation rate for the works in the works list.
 
Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10%).
 On fees: 
authors urged Judge Martínez-Olguín to grant final approval to the settlement along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.



$1.5 billion sounds enormous — and it is the largest copyright settlement in history — but divided across nearly 500,000 works, the per-work payout of roughly $3,000 represents a small fraction of the statutory damages that could have been awarded at trial.
 This denominator problem is the central objector argument and the lens through which any comparable AI copyright settlement should be evaluated.

Critically for the follow-on litigation landscape: 
twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the *Bartz* settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. (3:26-cv-04482) demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works.
 
A follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.



The pause matters far beyond Anthropic. The *Bartz* settlement has been treated by the industry as a template — a number that other AI labs and rights holders are quietly using as a benchmark in their own negotiations. If Martínez-Olguín ultimately approves the deal as written, expect a wave of similar class settlements in the OpenAI, Meta, and Microsoft cases.


The settlement administrator had been scheduled to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026. 
After the fairness hearing, the settlement administrator was to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026; payment disbursement was expected to begin June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed.
 With the final approval order not yet entered as of today, that schedule is materially delayed.

### 3. Google Mass Arbitration — Keller Postman's $218B Advertiser Campaign: Mechanics, Exposure, and the Antitrust Predicate

The most significant structural development in mass arbitration during the past 40 days is Keller Postman's coordinated campaign against Google on behalf of advertisers. 
On May 11, 2026, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman announced it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.


The antitrust predicate is unusually solid by mass-arbitration standards: 
federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S.
 government on monopoly findings, establishing liability as adjudicated fact rather than contested allegation.

The damages theory: 
the $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist the firm has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.
 
Experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs, creating the potential for substantial recovery under federal antitrust law.


Claimant eligibility is broad: 
the firm is targeting companies of all sizes — from local businesses to large enterprises — that spent money on Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016.
 
In addition to representing private claimants in arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state AGs in a parallel case; claims are brought on a full contingency basis.


The strategic logic of the arbitration vehicle is deliberate. 
While prior public litigation has focused largely on publisher-side claims, advertiser claims represent a significantly larger portion of the digital advertising market; because these proceedings take place in private arbitration, they have remained largely outside the public record.
 
For many big advertisers with hundreds of millions of dollars spent on Google media per year, there might be extreme hesitation to be the face of an anti-Google movement — but in mass anonymous arbitration, big brands can pursue settlements without running afoul of delicate account etiquette or ruining such a key media relationship.


The FAA mechanics bear watching closely. The AAA Mass Arbitration Supplementary Rules — which govern batching, filing-fee allocation, and bellwether selection — will determine whether Google can exploit administrative bottlenecks to stall resolution or whether the filing volume compels a global settlement pivot. 
Ashley Keller is the architect of some of the most significant mass arbitration campaigns in history, including the Amazon Alexa campaign that prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands, and the TurboTax campaign that ran parallel to FTC enforcement and resolved in a confidential settlement alongside a $141 million FTC fund.
 Google's response to that playbook — fee-shifting, batching motions, or a preemptive class-action waiver revision — is the next procedural signal to track.

## Recommended Actions

Claimants with Google advertising spend from August 2016 forward should assess their eligibility for the Keller Postman mass arbitration campaign promptly — the firm is filing initial demands now, and statutes of limitations under Clayton Act § 4 (four-year federal antitrust limitations period, 15 U.S.C. § 15b) run from the date of injury, meaning pre-2022 overcharges are already at risk of being time-barred if not captured in a tolling agreement; check whether your retainer with Keller Postman includes a tolling provision or whether a separate preservation filing is needed. On *Bartz v. Anthropic* (No. 3:24-cv-05417): the final approval order is not yet entered — rights holders who opted out and filed follow-on suits (3:26-cv-04482; *Kwon v. Anthropic*) should expect Anthropic to argue settlement collateral-estoppel once approval issues, and should be proactive about distinguishing their claims on training-use theories rather than the piracy-only framing Judge Alsup certified; once approval is entered, the $3,100-per-work distribution will flow only after any appeals are exhausted, so plan for at minimum a 12–18 month liquidity lag. On the Roundup settlement: the July final-approval hearing before Judge Boyer in St. Louis is the next hard event; objectors with standing should file any additional objections now and monitor whether the Eighth Circuit accepts any interlocutory appeal of Judge Autrey's remand order, as that appellate track is the primary mechanism to reopen the forum question.
