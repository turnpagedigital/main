### I. BLMIS / Picard — 17th Distribution Complete; Fee Application Overhang

The seventeenth distribution cycle closed as reported. 
Trustee Irving Picard distributed more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims, bringing aggregate customer payouts to nearly $15.38 billion — including approximately $850.9 million in SIPC advances.
 
The seventeenth distribution represented 1.302% of each allowed claim and was paid on claims relating to 768 accounts; combined with the prior sixteen distributions, approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim has now been paid.
 The proceeding remains *In re BLMIS*, Bankr. S.D.N.Y., No. 08-01789 (LGB).

The current pending matter is the fee dispute overhang. 
On February 13, 2026, Trustee Picard and BakerHostetler filed the 50th Interim Fee Application with the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, covering work on the BLMIS global liquidation from August 1, 2025 through November 30, 2025. The application seeks approval of fees representing approximately 63,774.40 hours of professional and paraprofessional services billed at an average public-interest-discounted rate of approximately $588.88 per hour.
 
The 10% public-interest discount produced a voluntary reduction during the four-month compensation period of $4,172,812.21; Picard and BakerHostetler also voluntarily adjusted fees by writing off $2,434,818.57 and wrote off $91,839.67 in expenses.
 
The Bankruptcy Court hearing for approval of the 50th Fee Application was scheduled for April 29, 2026 at 10:00 a.m.
 — that hearing has now passed; court approval or objection status is not confirmed on the public record as of this run.

**Claims-pricing read-through:** At 72.848% recovery on allowed claims after 17 distributions over 17+ years, the remaining upside sits in unresolved adversary proceedings and ongoing litigation settlements. 
As of May 26, 2026, SIPC has committed approximately $850.9 million to the BLMIS liquidation, with advances continuing to increase as claims in litigation are allowed through settlements or concluded litigation.
 
SIPC has received $272.4 million in reimbursement from the Customer Fund for advances paid on fully satisfied accounts, leaving $578.5 million in SIPC advances outstanding.
 Secondary-market buyers pricing BLMIS claims should treat the remaining recovery trajectory — incremental adversary-proceeding settlements against feeder-fund subsequent transferees — as the primary upside variable. The Stanford receivership (SEC v. Stanford Int'l Bank, N.D. Tex., No. 3:09-cv-00298-N, Receiver Ralph S. Janvey) has entered what Janvey's office is calling its final distribution phase, and that is the operative fact for claim pricing.


On March 9, 2026, Janvey filed with the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, Dallas Division, the Schedule of Payments to Be Made to Eligible Investor CD Claimants Pursuant to the Final Distribution Plan.
 
Payments listed on the Final Distribution Schedule — totaling more than $339 million and including payments to over 14,000 claim groups — are being issued on a rolling basis beginning 14 days after the filing of the Schedule.
 
Per a Receiver update posted April 6, 2026, payments under the Final Distribution Plan began on April 3, 2026.


Operational execution risk is non-trivial. 
During the first two months of 2026, the Receivership issued payments to eligible claimants totaling more than $100 million pursuant to Distribution Plans 1-11.
 Claimants with Venezuelan addresses have faced an additional layer of friction: 
beginning December 11, 2025, the Receivership was unable to arrange delivery of distribution payments via physical checks to Venezuelan addresses; however, as of March 11, 2026, courier delivery of physical checks to Venezuela resumed.


The check-cashing window carries hard consequences. 
Payees must cash checks no later than 90 days after the date of issuance; if a payee does not cash the check or request reissuance within that window, the payee shall not be eligible to receive any other payment under the Final Distribution Plan.
 
All funds represented by void checks not subject to a timely reissuance request revert to the Receivership Estate.

These amounts are secondary to the $339 million+ in the domestic Final Distribution Schedule, but non-trivial for Latin American claimant groups.

**Claims-pricing read-through:** The Final Distribution Plan's filing and rolling payments beginning April 3 make this a wind-down, not an ongoing investment horizon. Time-value discount for secondary-market claim buyers is now compressed to the 90-day check window and the residual Antigua/Swiss upside — both of which are uncertain and likely to take additional years.

The gap is explained in part by the fugitive status of the primary defendant.

Cross-border asset recovery is contributing at the margins.

### V. Clawback Framework — Ponzi Presumption and § 548 Mechanics (Standing Reference)

For claims buyers and recovery-fund LPs tracking multiple proceedings: the operative clawback exposure framework is consistent across BLMIS, Power Block Coin, and analogous proceedings. Under 11 U.S.C. § 548(a)(1), the trustee must demonstrate actual fraudulent intent and faces a two-year look-back from the petition date for avoidable transfers. The Ponzi presumption — articulated across Second Circuit and Tenth Circuit jurisprudence — treats every transfer from a Ponzi scheme as made with actual fraudulent intent, collapsing the scienter inquiry. The practical effect is that net winners (those who withdrew more than they invested on a "money in / money out" basis) face real clawback exposure if their net withdrawals occurred within the § 548 look-back window. State UFTA/UVTA claims can extend the window to six years in some jurisdictions, but the § 546(e) securities settlement payment defense — heavily litigated in BLMIS — can cut off state-law avoidance claims when transfers were made through securities accounts. The Power Block Coin crypto context may present a cleaner § 548 path (no § 546(e) complication) if the transferred property was digital-asset value rather than securities.
