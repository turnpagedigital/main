### OneCoin / Ruja Ignatova — DOJ Remission Deadline: June 30, 2026


Between 2014 and 2019, Ruja Ignatova and Karl Sebastian Greenwood, co-founders of OneCoin Ltd., orchestrated an international cryptocurrency investment scheme through which victims invested over $4 billion worldwide in the fraudulent cryptocurrency.
 
The DOJ filed a number of OneCoin-related prosecutions in the Southern District of New York; several key figures have been sentenced, and the Department pursued criminal forfeiture — with more than $40 million in forfeited assets currently available for victim compensation.


The structure in place is a federal remission process, not a receivership. 
The Criminal Division's Money Laundering, Narcotics and Forfeiture Section is managing the process; Kroll Settlement Administration LLC serves as remission administrator.
 
Victims who purchased the fraudulent OneCoin cryptocurrency between 2014 and 2019 may be eligible for compensation, and the deadline for filing a petition is June 30.
 Petitions are available at onecoinremission.com. The gap between $4 billion in admitted losses and $40 million in available forfeited assets — roughly a 1-cent-on-the-dollar initial recovery — underscores the structural limitation of remission processes relative to fully administered receiverships: forfeited assets are distributed as a function of what the government successfully seized and retained, not the full fraud quantum.


Co-founder Karl Sebastian Greenwood was sentenced to 20 years in prison in 2023; more recently, OneCoin associate Irina Dilkinska was sentenced to four years in prison by U.S. District Judge Edgardo Ramos.
 
Co-founder Ruja Ignatova — known globally as the "Cryptoqueen" — remains a fugitive on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list, with a reward of up to $250,000 for information leading to her arrest.


On the cross-border asset-recovery front, a separate forfeiture action concluded in January 2026. 
The Royal Court of Guernsey seized more than £8 million in assets belonging to Ignatova, with the Court determining that she had £8.59m plus interest held at RBS International in Guernsey, under the name Aquitaine Group Limited.
 
The Royal Court upheld an Overseas Forfeiture Order sought on behalf of German authorities in Bielefeld, and the funds were transferred to Guernsey's Seized Assets Fund.
 That Guernsey action was a non-conviction-based asset forfeiture proceeding — no criminal conviction of Ignatova was required — illustrating the continued utility of civil forfeiture tools under domestic UK/Crown dependency regimes and, by analogy, the UK § 423 Insolvency Act 1986 / CPR 25 Mareva architecture for cross-border recovery. The German-Guernsey cooperation is also a useful proof-of-concept for 28 U.S.C. § 1782 practitioners seeking evidence or asset discovery abroad: mutual legal assistance frameworks remain operationally viable even when the primary defendant is a fugitive.

Claims-buying note: with Ignatova still at large and total forfeited assets in the low eight figures against $4 billion in losses, secondary-market pricing on OneCoin victim claims should reflect the asymmetric risk — remission processes do not preclude further government seizures, and each new jurisdictional action (Germany, Guernsey, and potentially other EU member states) incrementally rebuilds the distributable pool.

### BLMIS / Picard — Seventeenth Distribution Complete; 50th Fee Application Pending


SIPA Trustee Irving H. Picard commenced the seventeenth pro rata interim distribution from the BLMIS Customer Fund on February 27, 2026, distributing more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims.
 
Including that distribution, aggregate amounts sent to eligible customers total nearly $15.38 billion, including approximately $850.9 million in SIPC advances; the seventeenth distribution represents 1.302% of each allowed claim and will be paid on claims relating to 768 accounts, bringing cumulative recovery to approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim.


The case caption is *Securities Investor Protection Corp. v. Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities LLC*, Bankr. S.D.N.Y. No. 08-01789. 
As of May 26, 2026, the SIPA Trustee has recovered or reached agreements to recover approximately $15.411 billion.


Fee proceedings remain a live signal event for claims pricing. 
On February 13, 2026, Picard and BakerHostetler filed the 50th Interim Fee Application with the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, covering work on the global liquidation of BLMIS from August 1, 2025 through November 30, 2025, seeking approval of fees representing approximately 63,774.40 hours of professional and paraprofessional services billed at an average public interest discounted rate of approximately $588.88 per hour.
 
Subject to court approval, 100% of recoveries are allocated to the Customer Fund for distribution; none of the money recovered is used to pay administrative costs — all trustee, legal, and accounting fees, as well as administrative expenses, are paid by SIPC.


This fee-structure distinction matters operationally: unlike most SEC receiverships where receiver/counsel compensation is drawn from the recovery pool and directly impairs claimant distributions, the BLMIS/SIPC mechanism insulates the Customer Fund entirely. Claims buyers pricing BLMIS paper should not apply a fee-drag haircut to future distribution projections — the correct model is SIPC-subsidized recovery with incremental litigation settlements flowing 100% to the Customer Fund.


As of March 2026, the trustee had recovered $15.3 billion through legal action against Madoff associates, feeder funds, and scheme beneficiaries, and had made seventeen distributions to investors; the Department of Justice separately recovered an additional $4.3 billion.
 With total admitted claims at approximately $20.3 billion and cumulative distributions now at ~72.85% of allowed claims, the residual recovery gap sits at roughly $5.4 billion in remaining allowed-claim balances — though a portion of those accounts have already received SIPC advances and may require partial reimbursement before being declared fully satisfied.

### Stanford Receivership — Final Distribution Rolling; $339M+ in Play

The Stanford receivership — *SEC v. Allen Stanford et al.*, N.D. Tex. (Judge David C. Godbey presiding) — has transitioned from the interim-distribution cycle to final-distribution execution. 
On March 9, 2026, Receiver Ralph Janvey filed with the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, Dallas Division, the Schedule of Payments to Be Made to Eligible Investor CD Claimants Pursuant to the Final Distribution Plan, in accordance with the October 8, 2025 Order Approving the Final Distribution Plan and the November 25, 2025 Order Approving Joint Motion to Amend.
 
Payments on the Final Distribution Schedule total more than $339 million and include payments to over 14,000 claim groups; the process of issuing payments is expected to take several weeks.



On April 3, 2026, the Receiver issued payments to over 2,000 claimant groups under the Final Distribution Plan totaling approximately $47.5 million, with further updates on the progress of the Final Distribution Plan to follow as additional payments are issued.
 
As of May 4, 2026, the Receiver has issued payments to over 5,600 claimant groups
 pursuant to the Court-approved Final Distribution Plan.

The multi-jurisdictional dimension of the Stanford case closed an important chapter in December 2025. 
On December 17, 2025, the Antiguan Joint Liquidators informed the Receiver that the Swiss Ancillary Bankruptcy proceeding concerning Stanford International Bank had concluded, resulting in a transfer to the Receiver of $8.1 million in funds from the Swiss bankruptcy proceeding; the JLs further informed the Receiver that no further distributions of funds are expected.


One coordination gap remains unresolved as of today. 
COViSAL (Coalition of Stanford Victims in Latin America) sent a formal written request to the Receiver and his attorneys on March 11, 2026, regarding the status of the $8.1 million in Swiss assets released to the Receiver on December 17, 2025; as of the filing of that inquiry, the Receiver and his legal team had not responded.
 The question — whether the Swiss funds are folded into the Final Distribution Schedule or constitute a separate tranche — matters directly for claimant recovery math. Absent a public filing resolving this question, claims holders with Venezuelan or Latin American addresses should confirm receipt status directly with Gilardi / Verita Global and monitor the court docket in the N.D. Tex. proceeding.

### SEC Enforcement Infrastructure — Structural Context for New Receivership Watch


The SEC appointed David Woodcock, an SEC veteran in private practice, as the new director of the Enforcement Division; the SEC also announced it is standing up a new enforcement team, the "SOX Group," which will focus on identifying and pursuing bad actors in the auditing profession.
 
During fiscal year 2025, the Commission filed 456 enforcement actions, including 303 standalone actions and 69 follow-on administrative proceedings, obtaining orders for monetary relief totaling $17.9 billion.


From a receivership-watch perspective, the Atkins-era SEC has explicitly reoriented toward "core fraud" cases. 
Enforcement priorities will focus on standing up to fraud in its many forms, addressing fraudulent and manipulative conduct through appropriate remediation, and repaying investors' losses when harmed.
 That framework, combined with the formation of the Cross-Border Task Force in September 2025, 
which was established to help address the serious threat that fraudsters located abroad pose to U.S. investors and markets,
 suggests that new receivership appointments in 2026–2027 are most likely to emerge from: (a) international investment fraud targeting U.S. retail investors, (b) crypto-adjacent offering frauds, and (c) Ponzi structures run through registered investment advisers. 
A March 26, 2026 settled charge against Oklahoma resident Krish Kumar illustrates the pattern: the SEC alleged Kumar raised approximately $7.8 million through two investment funds — Future Fractal Investments LLC and Arcane Resonance Fund, LLC — and misappropriated nearly $7 million by transferring funds to personal accounts.
