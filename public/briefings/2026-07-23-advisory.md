## Analysis & Developments

### DELTA: Bartz v. Anthropic, 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Judgment Entered; 30-Day Appeal Window Now Running

The controlling development across this beat is the entry of final judgment in *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.). 
On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 The order is the operative instrument for every downstream step: distribution timing, objector appellate standing, and comparative fair-use posture across the parallel docket.

**Fairness finding and objection disposition.** 
The court called the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," finding that it "was negotiated at arm's length; the relief provided for the Class is adequate; and the proposed Settlement treats Class Members equitably relative to each other." Judge Martínez-Olguín overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds.
 The court issued a 23-page order resolving the full set of contested administrative and fee issues.

**Claims rate and opt-outs.** 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate, and 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 — or 0.03% — of the total number of Works.
 The 0.03% opt-out figure is notable: it substantially narrows the population of rights-holders who remain free to pursue independent infringement claims against Anthropic, though 
the court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but took care to remind those individuals that, precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.


**Fee award: court-imposed 3.75x multiplier.** The fee question received the most sustained treatment in the written order. 
The court reduced the attorneys' fee award from the requested $187.5 million to $101,561,111.
 
Rather than the 6.92x multiplier on class counsel's estimated lodestar of $27,082,963 that counsel sought, the court determined that a multiplier of 3.75 — for a total fee award of $101,561,111 — is reasonable.
 
The court also granted expense reimbursements of close to $3 million, with a cost reserve of $18.2 million in anticipated expenses for the claims administration process.
 
The court denied class counsel's requested 12.5% of the $1.5 billion settlement fund, which would have resulted in legal fees of $187,500,000, as too high.


**Service awards trimmed.** 
The court also trimmed the requested service awards for the three class representatives from $50,000 to $15,000 each, while recognizing the substantial work Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson performed on behalf of fellow authors.


**Distribution timeline: appeal window is the gating event.** This is the most operationally consequential point for rights-holders awaiting payment. 
Under the Settlement Agreement, payments begin one business day after the "Effective Date," defined as the date on which the time to file or notice an appeal of the Final Judgment expires. The Ninth Circuit generally allows 30 days to appeal.
 Final judgment was entered July 20, 2026. Absent a timely notice of appeal filed by any of the 53 overruled objectors, the Effective Date should fall on or about **August 19, 2026**, triggering distribution obligations on August 20. Any objector appeal to the Ninth Circuit would stay that clock and delay distribution for the duration of circuit review — a significant risk factor for claimants holding recovery positions.

**Non-reversionary structure; cy pres carve-out.** 
If money remains after all valid claims are paid, the parties anticipate redistributing it to class members; if a final balance remains that is economically infeasible to distribute, it will go — subject to court approval — to charitable purposes.


**Precedential architecture — limited.** Rights-holders and claims investors should internalize one structural constraint: 
because Anthropic settled rather than appeal, the Alsup fair-use ruling never reached the Ninth Circuit or the Supreme Court. Other federal courts are not bound by it.
 The settlement resolves approximately $1.5 billion in exposure for Anthropic on the specific piracy-sourced training data at issue, but it does not generate binding precedent on the § 107 fair-use question — a critical distinction for valuing recovery positions in the parallel litigation stream.

**Per-title comparator: $3,000 per work.** 
Under the agreed-upon terms, Anthropic will pay rights-holders roughly $3,000 per title, for each of the nearly 500,000 books used without authorization for the training of AI models.
 
Critics within the author community have argued that $3,000 is a small fraction of what a commercially successful book earns over its lifetime, and that the settlement effectively prices a market rate for wholesale ingestion by well-capitalized AI labs.
 For § 107(4) market-harm analysis in downstream cases, the $3,000 per-title figure now exists as a data point — though its weight is contested, and it reflects a litigated settlement discount, not a voluntary licensing rate.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Two Parallel Dockets; Summary Judgment Hearing October 21, 2026

The music-publisher front against Anthropic is structurally more complex than the Bartz author track, and today's briefing carries two distinct dockets.

**Concord I (the operative cross-MSJ proceeding).** 
*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811, is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee in the Northern District of California.
 
Music publishers have asked Judge Lee to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrighted song lyrics through its Claude large language model, arguing in a motion for partial summary judgment that fair use does not excuse the conduct because Claude used those lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 
Anthropic has asked the court to find that its use of copyrighted materials to train Claude is "transformative" fair use, comparing Claude's learning to how humans learn from reading and internalizing the themes of various works.
 
The summary judgment hearing is set for October 21, 2026 before Judge Lee.
 Dispositive motions were scheduled to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026 per the docket's case management order.

**Concord II (the piracy-torrent complaint).** 
Anthropic has moved to stay *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK ("Concord II"), pending final resolution of the substantially similar litigation in *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK ("Concord I"), or in the alternative, pending resolution of the parties' motions for summary judgment and any subsequent appeal in that litigation.
 The Concord II complaint, filed January 28, 2026, is premised on Anthropic's alleged torrenting of pirated books containing publishers' musical compositions — conduct that became public only when Judge Alsup issued his ruling in the Bartz case in July 2025. 
Anthropic allegedly concealed during Concord I discovery that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books — including hundreds or more books plainly containing publishers' musical compositions — from pirate library websites. Publishers did not learn of that torrenting until July 2025, when Judge Alsup's ruling publicly revealed it for the first time.
 
Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues governing these claims — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — which will determine the viability of most if not all of publishers' claims in Concord II.


The October 21 MSJ hearing in Concord I is therefore the most consequential near-term event for both dockets. A finding of fair use would effectively collapse the Concord II claims; a finding of infringement would energize both and likely precipitate settlement discussions.

### Thomson Reuters v. Ross — Third Circuit Appeal Argued; Decision Pending

The fair-use circuit split that Bartz deferred is being written at the Third Circuit. 
Three U.S. judges — Alsup (*Bartz*), Chhabria (*Kadrey*), and Bibas (*Thomson Reuters v. Ross*) — have all ruled on AI training and fair use. Alsup and Chhabria found for AI companies on training; Bibas found against. The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling — argued June 11, 2026 — is pending.
 
The district court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard."
 The Third Circuit's decision will be the first U.S. appellate ruling on whether AI training on copyrighted material constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. Whatever the panel holds on the § 107(1) transformativeness and § 107(4) market-harm factors will shape briefing in Concord I, *In re OpenAI* MDL (No. 25-md-03143, S.D.N.Y.), and any Ninth Circuit appeal that emerges from Bartz — even if not precedentially binding across circuits.

### In re OpenAI Copyright MDL, No. 25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Stein) — NYT Sanctions Motion

The S.D.N.Y. MDL before Judge Sidney H. Stein (Courtroom 23A, 500 Pearl Street) is the second major live front. The class action track within the MDL — captioned *David Baldacci et al. v. OpenAI Inc. et al.*, No. 25-md-3143 — survived OpenAI's motion to dismiss, with 
Judge Stein denying OpenAI's motion to dismiss class plaintiffs' claim of direct copyright infringement based on the outputs of ChatGPT, holding that plaintiffs' allegations satisfy the elements of a prima facie claim of infringement as to at least some ChatGPT outputs.


On the news-publisher track — *The New York Times Co. v. OpenAI*, No. 1:23-cv-11195 (also within MDL 3143) — 
a group of newspapers including the New York Times and New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan to sanction OpenAI in their high-stakes copyright dispute for allegedly lying to the court about its ability to search its systems for proof that it misused millions of their articles in AI training.
 
The newspapers told the court that OpenAI falsely stated it could not search its large language models for their copyrighted material while hiding that it had done so "even before the first News Plaintiff filed suit."
 That sanctions motion, filed July 9, 2026, is pending before Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang. If granted, it could generate adverse-inference instructions, cost-shifting, or records production that would materially advance plaintiffs' merits position — and, by extension, provide discovery templates for the book-author class cases.

### New Filing Alert: Hachette et al. v. Google (S.D.N.Y., filed July 10, 2026)

A new complaint entered the docket last week that warrants tracking. 
The Southern District of New York is now hearing a new class action filed by Hachette Book Group, Cengage Learning, Elsevier, and author Scott Turow against Google over alleged Gemini training-data piracy, filed July 10, 2026.
 
The Hachette-Google complaint contains details the Bartz case did not: Google allegedly removed or altered copyright information on ingested works to "conceal that its Gemini Models were trained on stolen materials" — a claim that goes beyond careless data sourcing toward alleged intentional concealment.
 Intentional concealment of copyright management information is a distinct theory under 17 U.S.C. § 1202(b), which carries its own statutory damage exposure under § 1203 independent of the § 107 fair-use defense. This new complaint should be added to the tracked-case inventory; its theory is structurally distinguishable from *Bartz* and positions plaintiffs more favorably on the § 107(4) market-harm prong.

### International Horizon: Circuit-Level Divergence Accumulating


The UK High Court ruled for Stability AI in *Getty v. Stability AI* (November 2025). The Munich Regional Court ruled against OpenAI in *GEMA v. OpenAI* (November 2025). The same technology, trained on the same type of content, produced opposite first-instance verdicts in two legal systems within days of each other.
 For rights-holders with works registered across jurisdictions — particularly music publishers with EU and UK repertoire — the divergence means that parallel litigation remains viable even where U.S. claims have settled.

Google as a comparator theory, particularly if their ingested works contain altered or stripped copyright management information cognizable under § 1202(b). Claims investors evaluating Concord I exposure should mark October 21, 2026 as the decision-critical date: Judge Lee's MSJ ruling will be the first appellate-quality judicial analysis of the lyrics-output infringement theory, and it will arrive shortly after the Third Circuit's *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* ruling — together, those two decisions will substantially clarify whether the § 107(1) transformativeness framework that Alsup applied in *Bartz* travels to the output, not just the training, context.
