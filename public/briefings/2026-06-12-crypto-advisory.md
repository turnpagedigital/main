### I. BlockFills (Case No. 26-10371 (TMH)): § 363 Sale Hearing Monday — Keyrock at the Gate, Plan Solicitation Live

The pace of *In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.* continues to outstrip every prior-cycle crypto restructuring on a petition-to-plan timeline. Filing petitions on March 15, 2026 before Judge Horan after years of cascading financial losses, two prepetition customer lawsuits with temporary restraining orders, and the collapse of multiple recapitalization attempts, the debtors then filed a joint chapter 11 plan, disclosure statement, and solicitation procedures motion by April 7 — less than four weeks post-petition — targeting a June 22, 2026 confirmation hearing. That confirmation date has since been extended to July 13, but the underlying trajectory has not changed.

The § 363 track is the immediate pressure point. Keyrock SA was declared the "Successful Bidder" per a bankruptcy court document filed on May 26, 2026; Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property. The sale consideration represents a severe enterprise-value haircut relative to the debtor's historical profile: trading volume exceeded $60 billion in 2025, a 28% increase from the prior year, and the firm served approximately 2,000 institutional clients, ranking among the more active desks in the institutional crypto lending and borrowing market. Against that operating footprint, a $3.25 million clearing price reflects both the commingling contamination of customer assets and the structural deterioration of the lending book.

On the balance sheet, the liability picture is stark. Court filings showed Reliz reporting assets between $50 million and $100 million against liabilities of $100 million to $500 million. More granularly, the debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations. The origins of the liquidity crisis are traceable to specific counterparty defaults: the company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius, with stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials compounding these balance sheet pressures.

The commingling question — the fundamental valuation dispute separating customer claims from general unsecured claims — was essentially decided at first-day hearings. At the first day hearing, debtors' counsel confirmed in open court that customer funds "have always been commingled with company funds and other customer funds," and that the debtors' position is that all such funds constitute property of the estate. That concession forecloses a constructive trust or tracing argument for most creditors; they are now voting creditors in the plan process, not bailment claimants standing outside it.

The prepetition litigation backstory informs both the adversary risk and the plan structure. Two customer lawsuits followed the withdrawal suspension, each alleging misappropriation of customer funds, commingling, and fraud-based claims — *Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd.* (S.D.N.Y., Case No. 1:26-cv-01672, filed February 27, 2026), in which Judge Mary Kay Vyskocil issued an ex parte TRO freezing 70.56 BTC (~$4.8 million) belonging to Dominion, barring BlockFills from moving assets outside the United States, and ordering an accounting and segregation of customer funds. The automatic stay mooted both TROs; those plaintiffs are now creditors in this case.

For Keyrock, the strategic rationale is clear: the acquisition will give Keyrock access to BlockFills' institutional client network, which includes hedge funds, asset managers, market makers, and mining companies. The acquisition comes months after Keyrock raised a Series C round led by SC Ventures, Standard Chartered's venture capital arm, at a $1.1 billion valuation. Critically, final completion of the transaction remains subject to final court approval and the appropriate regulatory approvals referenced in Keyrock's bid. Creditors opposing the sale have until the June 16 hearing to file sale objections. Parties who provided IP licenses, data-sharing agreements, or exchange connectivity agreements to BlockFills should confirm whether those contracts are being assumed or rejected — a decision with direct cure-cost and rejection-damage implications for the estate's pro rata recovery pool.

### II. Power Block Coin, LLC (Case No. 24-23041-CDP, Bankr. D. Utah, Judge Cathleen D. Parker): Celsius Preference War Continues, Motion for Relief from Stay Rescheduled

*In re Power Block Coin, LLC* — dba SmartFi — represents the most complex creditor-rights intersection on this tracker, sitting at the confluence of a crypto insolvency, a Celsius Network preference-avoidance campaign, and a fraud-recovery posture. The Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors has proposed a liquidation plan in Utah bankruptcy court, aiming to maximize value for creditors of the troubled cryptocurrency firm, with the plan addressing over $192 million in claims and establishing an independent Liquidating Trust to manage the company's assets and potential legal claims. Greenberg Traurig (Annette W. Jarvis) represents both the Committee and Celsius Network LLC as creditor — a dual representation that the docket reflects across multiple parties per the May 7, 2026 CM/ECF service list (Doc. 502).

Power Block Coin, which operated under the name SmartFi, filed for bankruptcy protection on June 20, 2024; the plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million, with a key component being the appointment of a disinterested Liquidating Trustee to manage the company's assets, including pursuing potential legal claims and overseeing distributions to creditors.

The Celsius preference litigation is the dominant value-recovery variable. The two adversary proceedings — *Meghji v. Power Block Coin, LLC*, Adv. Proc. No. 24-02093 (JTM) — seek damages of $79,032,563.75 and $54,256,669.86, respectively, not including potential defenses. The venue transfer motions seek to allow Celsius to prosecute the avoidance actions against Power Block Coin in its bankruptcy court in the Southern District of New York so that the procedures and schedule for the preference litigation, as well as any rulings, are consistent with the approximately 2,500 other avoidance actions that the Litigation Administrators have filed. Whether those venue motions were granted controls whether this case's preference exposure resolves on Utah-local procedures or the SDNY mega-consolidated preference docket (*In re Celsius Customer Preference Actions*, Adv. Proc. No. 24-04024 (MG), Bankr. S.D.N.Y.).

A significant contested asset is: the committee's filing raises concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions, including a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC, an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah — a loan the committee has expressed concerns about given its unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property.

The live docket shows Entry No. 519 filed June 9, 2026 (the most recent entry per the tracker's 72-hour sync window). A motion for relief from stay — previously scheduled for May 6, 2026 — was rescheduled and heard before Judge Cathleen D. Parker on May 26, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. via Zoom (Meeting ID 160 4196 1337) per Doc. 502. The outcome of that stay-relief motion will determine whether any secured creditor may proceed against estate assets outside the bankruptcy process, directly affecting distributions under the proposed Liquidating Trust. Creditors should obtain the ruling from the May 26 hearing transcript before taking any position on the plan.

### III. Bitcoin Depot Chapter 11 (S.D. Tex.): Regulatory Compression Fells North America's Largest Crypto ATM Operator

The most consequential new-filing development in the crypto-insolvency space is Bitcoin Depot's Chapter 11 filing in the Southern District of Texas. Bitcoin Depot, the largest bitcoin ATM operator in North America, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection and will wind down its network of more than 9,000 ATMs. The proximate cause is regulatory compression: the company disclosed an $80.7 million, or 49.2%, loss in revenue in 2026's first quarter compared with a year earlier, which it blamed on "a decrease in transaction volume driven by a combination of regulatory impacts and enhanced compliance controls." The bankruptcy will be supervised by the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas.

The state-by-state regulatory cascade is the structural story for creditors. Tennessee in April became the second U.S. state, following Indiana, to outlaw BTMs — a ban going into effect July 1 — and a similar ban is being considered across Canada, which Bitcoin Depot also serves. The enforcement backstory is equally damaging: the attorneys general of Massachusetts and Iowa sued Bitcoin Depot in February 2025, alleging the company played a role in bilking $20 million from consumers. For restructuring counsel and creditors in this case, the critical issue is whether the company's contracts with ATM-host retailers (convenience stores, grocery chains) are assumable or rejectable executory contracts, and what cure costs attach to any assumption — particularly where operators are located in states that have imposed active transaction-limit regimes or outright bans.

One restructuring advisor characterized the filing as "a preview of what the broader crypto ATM industry will face in the United States over the next several years," noting that "the traditional model depended on high transaction spreads and limited regulatory scrutiny to offset unusually high compliance, cash logistics, fraud remediation, and retail revenue sharing costs," adding that this equation "is breaking down as states increasingly impose consumer protection standards that compress fees, expand operator liability for scam related activity, and raise expectations around transaction monitoring and reimbursement." Bitcoin Depot's stock price fell 75% between Friday and Monday, to 75 cents per share.

### IV. Sector-Wide Macro: Customer-Asset Commingling Doctrine, Property-of-the-Estate Disputes, and the Lessons of BlockFills for Future Crypto Creditors

The BlockFills outcome — particularly counsel's first-day admission that customer assets were always commingled — has doctrinal significance that extends well beyond Case No. 26-10371. In virtually every major crypto-lending insolvency since *In re Celsius Network LLC* (Case No. 22-10964-mg, Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Martin Glenn), courts have confronted the question of whether customer deposits held in pooled omnibus accounts constitute "property of the estate" under 11 U.S.C. § 541(a)(1) or whether a trust, bailment, or constructive trust analysis takes them outside the estate. Judge Glenn's 2023 ruling in *Celsius* — finding that token deposits in yield-generating "Earn" accounts were estate property by operation of the Terms of Use — was the template; BlockFills counsel's admission in open court replicates that posture without the need for contested briefing.

For Power Block Coin, the same property-of-estate question is embedded in the Celsius preference litigation: the avoidance actions under 11 U.S.C. §§ 547 and 548 turn partly on whether Power Block Coin received a transfer of "an interest of the debtor in property" within the 90-day window. The Celsius Litigation Administrator's two adversary complaints seek aggregate damages of approximately $133 million when combined. The $17.4 million Solara Communities loan — unsecured, with encumbered real property collateral — is a potential fraudulent-transfer target under § 548 or its state-law analog (Utah Code Ann. § 25-6-202 et seq.), and creditors seeking to maximize estate value should press the Liquidating Trustee to confirm whether an action has been filed or is being investigated.
