### I. BlockFills / Reliz Technology (Bankr. D. Del. No. 26-10371 (TMH)): Sale Hearing Monday — Toggle Mechanism Resolves Toward Keyrock

On March 15, 2026, Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and three affiliated debtor entities — collectively operating under the trade name "BlockFills" — filed Chapter 11 petitions in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware before the Honorable Thomas M. Horan. The filing came at the tail end of a liquidity cascade that management attributed to compounding counterparty failures: the company's balance sheet had absorbed an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy, alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius. Compounding these pressures were stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials.

The petition disclosed a brutal balance-sheet gap: the Debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations, against assets of between $50 million and $100 million and liabilities ranging from $100 million to $500 million. The filings followed years of cascading financial losses, two prepetition customer lawsuits with temporary restraining orders, and the collapse of multiple recapitalization attempts.

Two prepetition litigation fronts had already frozen the estate before the petition date. Two customer lawsuits alleged misappropriation of customer funds, commingling, and fraud-based claims: Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd. (S.D.N.Y., Case No. 1:26-cv-01672, filed February 27, 2026), in which Judge Mary Kay Vyskocil issued an ex parte temporary restraining order freezing 70.56 BTC (~$4.8 million) belonging to Dominion, barring BlockFills from moving assets outside the United States, and ordering an accounting and segregation of customer funds. The first lawsuit was filed February 27, 2026 in the Southern District of New York; the second was filed March 5, 2026 in the Northern District of Illinois. The courts in both matters granted temporary restraining orders that restricted the Debtors' ability to conduct operations and exercise control over certain assets.

The commingling conduct underlying both lawsuits is structurally damning for unsecured creditors. On January 16, 2026, Alberta transferred 40.001 BTC and 650,050 USDC to BlockFills; these assets were swept from the deposit wallet within minutes and the wallet was empty within 1.5 hours. On February 11, 2026, the Debtors' representatives disclosed to clients that digital assets were "not segregated per client," "not segregated on separate wallets per customer," and were commingled into "one balance sheet."

**The § 363 sale to Keyrock SA is the dominant near-term event.** According to a bankruptcy filing, Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists and its proprietary technology and intellectual property. Keyrock SA has been declared the "Successful Bidder" for certain assets of Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and its affiliated debtors as set out in the official Bankruptcy Court document filed on May 26, 2026. Keyrock confirmed in a statement that its bid was recorded in an official Bankruptcy Court filing on May 26, 2026, and that a hearing to consider approval of the sale is scheduled for June 16, 2026.

The $3.25 million headline price requires context. The acquisition price tag represents a stark valuation collapse for an entity that claimed over $60 billion in trading volume just one year prior. Trading volume exceeded $60 billion in 2025, a 28% increase from the previous year, and the firm served approximately 2,000 institutional clients. The acquirer's rationale is institutional scale-acquisition: the acquisition comes months after Keyrock raised a Series C round led by SC Ventures, Standard Chartered's venture capital arm, at a $1.1 billion valuation.

**Plan solicitation is running concurrently with the asset sale.** On May 28, 2026, the Bankruptcy Court entered the Order (A) Approving Disclosure Statement; (B) Scheduling Hearing on Confirmation of Plan; (C) Establishing Deadlines and Procedures for Filing Objections to Confirmation of Plan, Claim Objections, and Temporary Allowance of Claims for Voting Purposes; (D) Determining Treatment of Certain Unliquidated, Contingent, or Disputed Claims for Notice, Voting, and Distribution Purposes; (E) Setting Record Date; and (F) Approving Solicitation Packages and Procedures, among other relief [Docket No. 388]. The Voting Deadline is July 1, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. (ET), and the Confirmation Objection Deadline is July 1, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. (ET).

The plan's architecture is a dual-track toggle: less than four weeks after the Petition Date, the Debtors filed a Joint Chapter 11 Plan, a Disclosure Statement, and a Solicitation Procedures Motion on April 7, 2026, establishing a compressed timeline targeting a June 22, 2026 Confirmation Hearing. The Plan features a dual-track "toggle" mechanism allowing the Debtors to elect between a customer-led NewCo Transaction and an Alternative Transaction through a Section 363 competitive sale process. The selection of Keyrock as Successful Bidder effectively activates the Alternative Transaction prong, though plan confirmation remains required to implement distributions through the contemplated Liquidating Trust.

A central contested issue before Judge Horan — and one that will determine whether general unsecured creditors recover anything meaningful — is whether commingled customer digital assets constitute property of the estate under § 541 of the Bankruptcy Code. The BlockFills Client Agreement contains three separate provisions explicitly disclaiming any fiduciary relationship, and the agreement authorizes BlockFills to transfer margin to exchanges and clearing houses at its sole discretion; customers "rank only as a general creditor" upon third-party default. If the court holds that customer digital assets are estate property — consistent with the Celsius Network precedent in the S.D.N.Y. where Judge Martin Glenn ruled that Earn account assets belonged to the estate — unsecured creditors benefit collectively but individual customers lose the ability to assert priority property claims. The Veritaglobal claims administrator (claims portal: veritaglobal.net/BlockFills) is administering the proof-of-claim process; creditors who have not yet filed should treat the July 1 voting and objection deadline as a hard backstop.

### II. Power Block Coin LLC / SmartFi (Bankr. D. Utah, No. 24-23041, Judge Cathleen D. Parker): Conversion Motion and Administrative Insolvency

Power Block Coin LLC, operating under the SmartFi brand, has requested to convert its Chapter 11 reorganization case to a Chapter 7 liquidation, citing administrative insolvency and failed reorganization efforts. The Utah-based cryptocurrency financial services company filed the conversion motion November 7, 2025, arguing that approximately $2 million in unpaid professional fees has rendered the estate "grossly administratively insolvent" and unable to confirm any reorganization plan.

Note on judicial assignment: the tracked-case ground truth designates Judge Cathleen D. Parker as the presiding judge, consistent with the later case posture; an earlier October 2024 decision in the same case (Case No. 24-bk-23041-JTM) was issued by Judge Joel T. Marker on the Subchapter V eligibility threshold dispute, reflecting a reassignment during the case's life. The docket registered Entry No. 519 on June 9, 2026, suggesting at minimum administrative activity is continuing — creditors should monitor the court's docket for a ruling on the pending conversion motion.

The case's procedural trajectory has been one of successive plan failures. Despite initial reorganization plans and extensive negotiations, SmartFi's attempts to confirm a Chapter 11 plan ultimately failed. The company initially proposed a plan based on accelerated payments from affiliate projects, particularly the Solara real estate development. Negotiations involved multiple parties, including Celsius, the largest creditor by claim amount, and culminated in an all-day mediation session with Judge Peggy Hunt that resulted in a joint plan agreement between the debtor and the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors — but that deal ultimately fell apart.

The claims universe is large and contentious. The plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million. A separate and significant asset-integrity concern runs through the estate: the committee's filing raises concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions, including loans made to affiliated entities at allegedly below-market rates. A significant asset under scrutiny is a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC, an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah, and the committee expressed concerns about this loan's unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property.

Under § 1112(a) of the Bankruptcy Code, SmartFi argues it has the statutory right to convert the case to Chapter 7 liquidation as a matter of course, since it remains in possession, the case was voluntarily filed under Chapter 11, and has never been previously converted. If conversion is granted, a Chapter 7 trustee would inherit an administratively insolvent estate with $17.4 million in potentially contested insider receivables and over $192 million in claims. Recovery prospects for general unsecured creditors — including the Celsius estate and its own distribution machinery — are deeply uncertain at any conversion rate.

### III. FTX Recovery Trust: Fourth Distribution Complete, Regulatory Implications Accumulating

The FTX Recovery Trust (FTX Trading Ltd., et al., No. 22-11068 (Bankr. D. Del.), Judge John Dorsey) continues its methodical wind-down. The FTX Recovery Trust announced plans on March 18, 2026, to start distributing approximately $2.2 billion on March 31 to eligible holders of allowed claims. Funds flow through chosen service providers — BitGo, Kraken, or Payoneer — and most recipients could expect money in their accounts within one to three business days. This round builds on earlier payouts and brings the cumulative total returned to roughly $10 billion since distributions began in early 2025.

In one notable adjustment earlier in 2026, officials cut the disputed-claims reserve by $2.2 billion, directly supporting this fourth payout. The FTX case's 100%+ creditor recovery outcome — history suggests recovery rates in crypto bankruptcies are rarely satisfying, though the FTX case's 118–142% creditor recovery stands as a rare outlier — continues to shape creditor expectations in subsequent cases such as BlockFills, where the asset-liability gap is far less favorable and the $3.25 million Keyrock bid covers only a fraction of the $145 million unsecured claim pool.

A broader regulatory development has emerged that will reshape future crypto insolvency proceedings. The GENIUS Act, signed into law on July 18, is one of the most significant changes to U.S. bankruptcy law since 2005. The law requires permitted stablecoin issuers to back their coins one-to-one with liquid assets and treat those reserves as property of the customer, not the issuer. Because the reserves sit outside the estate and function like a protected trust, they cannot be used to pay legal fees or pledged to secure financing, which may leave many issuers administratively insolvent on day one. Professionals warn that bankruptcy lenders may hesitate to fund cases under this framework, and some companies might issue stablecoins solely to access the bankruptcy system. While the Act aims to ensure stablecoin holders are made whole, it also risks making it harder for issuers to successfully reorganize or exit bankruptcy, fundamentally reshaping creditor repayment hierarchies in the process.

The valuation methodology for digital assets in bankruptcy also continues to develop. In the FTX case, the court applied significant discounts to the market prices of three utility tokens: MAPS (100% discount), OXY (99.9% discount), and SRM (18.6% discount), marking the first time a bankruptcy court had estimated the value of such claims and conducted a valuation of crypto assets. This framework is directly relevant to BlockFills creditors, as the estate holds digital assets whose liquidation value under a § 363 process may diverge substantially from spot prices — a valuation gap that will affect balloting in the July 1 vote.

### IV. Cross-Case Theme: Commingling, Asset Segregation, and the Property-of-the-Estate Question

A persistent structural issue runs through both active tracked cases and every prior cycle wave. Withdrawal clawbacks refer to the recovery of funds or a percentage of funds transferred from a distressed entity to another within a specified timeframe leading up to the bankruptcy filing; the purpose of these clawbacks is to prevent certain creditors from receiving preferential treatment at the expense of others. In the crypto context, a trustee examines the 90-day look-back period before filing for Chapter 7 or Chapter 11 to identify payments that conferred unfair advantage to a creditor; in the crypto context, however, the complexity is multiplied: token withdrawals, internal wallet transfers, algorithmic stablecoins, and cross-border custody all complicate the inquiry.

The BlockFills commingling facts — assets swept from customer wallets into a single balance sheet within 90 minutes of receipt — present a textbook § 547 preference-period scenario. In the FTX bankruptcy, the plan only looked at withdrawals that exceeded $250,000 and occurred within ten days of the petition date (FTX Trading Ltd., Case No. 22-11068, Bankr. D. Del.). By contrast, the Celsius plan cast a broader net, using the entire 90-day window and reviewing transactions over $100,000 (Celsius Network LLC, et al., No. 22-10964, Bankr. S.D.N.Y.). BlockFills' plan has not yet specified its clawback parameters; creditors who withdrew significant digital assets within 90 days of the March 15, 2026 petition date should retain restructuring counsel immediately.

The Harvard Bankruptcy Roundtable noted on June 9, 2026 that a motion by former Celsius customers to withdraw the bankruptcy court reference was rejected by the S.D.N.Y. — former Celsius customers' motion to withdraw bankruptcy court reference was rejected by SDNY on June 9, 2026 — an important signal that SDNY is not prepared to strip the bankruptcy court of jurisdiction over property-of-the-estate determinations in crypto cases. That ruling reinforces the likelihood that Judge Horan, applying similar principles, will retain jurisdiction over the BlockFills customer-asset questions through the plan confirmation process.
