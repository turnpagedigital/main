### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Still Outstanding; Late Opt-Outs the Remaining Friction Point

The last significant docket activity confirmed on CourtListener (last updated July 2, 2026) is a May 21, 2026 supplemental filing ordered by the court following the fairness hearing. 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín held the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing; her post-hearing order directed Anthropic to file by May 21 a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 
In that supplemental filing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should be denied for lack of any supporting record and because other rightsholders have not consented.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement, and final approval remains pending.


The economics of the settlement are worth restating precisely because the claims rate is unusually high and compresses individual recoveries relative to earlier projections. 
Class counsel filed papers showing 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate — which vastly surpassed the 54% rate reported in counsel's March 19 Motion for Final Approval, indicating a large volume of last-minute submissions.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson reported at the hearing that the payout per work will be approximately $3,100.
 
Attorneys' fees were sought at 25% of the $1.5 billion fund, or $375 million — though, per Law360, that figure was subsequently revised down to $187.5 million.
 
Authors urged the court to grant final approval along with the revised attorney fee request of $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.


The settlement's payment architecture is structured in four tranches. 
Anthropic will fund the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Under the Plan of Allocation and Distribution, Anthropic's first scheduled payment of $450 million (Schedule Payment 1) is due September 25, 2026, twelve months after issuance of the Preliminary Approval Order; a second wave of checks from the Settlement Administrator follows October 9, 2026.
 All downstream distribution dates remain conditioned on the issuance of a final approval order, meaning each additional week without the order slips the payment calendar commensurately.

The structural importance of the case is unchanged: 
Judge William Alsup ruled on June 23, 2025 that training Claude on legally acquired books was "exceedingly transformative," a clear win for AI developers, but drew a sharp line that acquiring and storing pirated books was not fair use regardless of downstream use.
 
That decision simultaneously held that Anthropic's use of legally acquired books was "quintessentially transformative" and protected as fair use — but that downloading and storing pirated copies was not.
 The settlement resolves the piracy-side liability; the training-side fair-use ruling stands as precedent against the class. Rights-holders who did not opt out by the January 15, 2026 deadline are bound by final approval once issued.

### II. *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.) — Tier-1 Wave Complete; Tier-2 Processing Underway

Distribution of the $2.67 billion subscriber settlement — finally approved by Judge R. David Proctor in August 2022 after nearly two years of appeals — entered its active payment phase in May and is now working through successive claimant tiers. 
After years of approval and appeals processes, the settlement reached the payment stage in May 2026; a court order directed the Settlement Administrator to begin issuing payments during the week of May 11, 2026.



Approximately $1.9 billion will be distributed to roughly six million claimants, with individual payouts varying based on coverage history and premiums paid during the class period.
 
Claimants with a calculated distribution of $200 or more receive 90% in the initial round, with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution; payment methods include check, PayPal, Venmo, and electronic debit card.



Tier-2 (Employee) notification emails began rolling out June 1 for a first batch covering approximately 15% of validated Tier-2 claims, with payments expected four to eight weeks after notification.
 
Estimated full Tier-1 wave completion is targeted for the end of July 2026.
 A separate provider-side track — a $2.8 billion settlement covering healthcare providers — received preliminary approval in December 2024 and is proceeding through a parallel claim-administration process administered separately.

The denominator math for this settlement illustrates the structural lesson for large MDL distributions: 
the $2.67 billion figure received final court approval in August 2022; the case alleged that BCBS's 35 independent plans illegally carved up the U.S. market geographically since 1948, keeping premiums artificially inflated; the covered damage period runs February 7, 2008 through October 16, 2020.
 The nearly four-year gap between final approval and distribution reflects the scale of the appellate backlog and claims-review pipeline, a pattern that any rights-holder in a similarly structured settlement should model explicitly.

### III. *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 1720 (E.D.N.Y.) — Second Distribution Approved June 15, Anticipated September 2026

The Visa/Mastercard interchange MDL before Judge Margo Brodie in the Eastern District of New York is now in second-wave distribution. 
On May 26, 2026, Class Counsel filed a motion requesting court approval of a second initial distribution of settlement funds; on June 15, 2026, the court approved the motion, finding that the proposed distribution is in the best interests of eligible claimants while protecting those whose claims are not yet ready for distribution.
 
The second initial distribution is anticipated to occur in September 2026.



Between October 1, 2023 and March 31, 2026, Visa made payments from its U.S. litigation escrow account of approximately $4.2 billion for settlements of U.S. covered litigation, reducing by more than 50% the estimated interchange reimbursement fees at issue in unresolved claims for damages.
 
The current U.S. litigation escrow account balance is approximately $650 million.
 That drawdown trajectory is material for any merchant with an opt-out claim still in litigation: the remaining escrow balance against still-open interchange claims is narrowing, which creates settlement-timing pressure for defendants seeking finality.

Separately, 
Visa and Mastercard face a new class action filed by three New York merchants seeking removal of the legal immunity the card networks received from the 2019 damages settlement; the request for summary judgment was put to U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan in Brooklyn, and the underlying lawsuit was filed the same day in the Southern District of New York.
 That challenge does not disturb the distribution schedule approved June 15 but is a watch item for the outer boundary of the networks' settlement bar.

### IV. Keller Postman / Google Mass Arbitration — Claimant Assembly Phase, No Settlement


Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google.



Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 The campaign's structural advantage over prior mass arbitration campaigns is precisely that liability has been adjudicated — the arbitration proceeds on damages, not a mixed liability/damages exposure. 
The $218 billion figure is an estimate, not a filed demand amount; in mass arbitration, the leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger, not from any single damages number.



Google's advertiser contracts specify arbitration before AAA or JAMS, both of which have mass arbitration rules triggered at 25 or more simultaneous coordinated claims; for each individual claim, Google is typically required to pay the forum's per-case initiation fees, which at AAA's current rates run into the thousands of dollars per case, meaning the fee exposure alone creates enormous settlement pressure before a single hearing occurs.



Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad-tech case remains outstanding; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines, with the firm's published guidance noting that deadlines vary by state.
 
Potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law (Clayton Act § 4).
 
In addition to representing private claimants in the arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state AGs in a parallel case.
 No settlement has been announced; the campaign is in the claimant-assembly and initial-filing phase.

### V. Carry-Forward: Fee Awards, Appellate Exposure, and Distribution Mechanics

Three structural dynamics bear watching across all active settlements this quarter.

**Fee-award compression.** The Bartz fee trajectory — from a 25% percentage-of-fund request ($375 million) revised down to $187.5 million in advance of the fairness hearing — reflects judicial and objector pressure on megafund fee arithmetic. The Ninth Circuit's lodestar cross-check doctrine is operative in N.D. Cal., and the revision to $187.5 million (12.5% of the $1.5 billion fund) suggests counsel anticipated pushback from the court on the gross multiple. Rights-holders should note that fee reductions at this stage do not increase per-work distributions — they reduce only what comes out of the fund before per-work calculations are made.

**Appellate exposure as the dominant distribution delay.** In BCBS, the gap between final approval (August 2022) and first payment (May 11, 2026) was driven almost entirely by appellate proceedings. In Bartz, 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 Given the objector record — seven objectors spoke at the May 14 hearing — an appeal to the Ninth Circuit is plausible, which could push distribution well into 2027 even if the final approval order issues this month.

**Claims-made vs. opt-out structure and the denominator effect.** The 91.3% claims rate in Bartz is anomalous by class action standards — 
the typical class action claim rate is around 10 percent.
 That high participation rate compressed per-work recoveries from early projections above $3,100 toward the $3,100 figure itself. In contrast, the BCBS subscriber settlement's approximately six million valid claims against a $2.67 billion fund (net of fees and administration) produced individual payouts in the $100–$1,500 range. The lesson for claimants evaluating a tradeable claims position: a high-awareness class with a motivated constituency (authors, publishers) will fill up near 100% of eligible works, while a diffuse consumer class (insurance subscribers) will see much lower participation — and correspondingly higher per-claimant recoveries for those who do file.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and claimants with active positions across this quarter's tracked settlements should prioritize three tasks immediately: (1) In *Bartz*, monitor the N.D. Cal. docket (CourtListener No. 69058235) for the final approval order — once entered, the clock starts on the September 25, 2026 first scheduled payment tranche ($450 million) and a Ninth Circuit appeal window that could delay all distributions; any claimant who did not submit a claim by March 30 has no path into the first distribution and should evaluate whether the non-filing claimant track (deadline extended to June 1 per the Plan of Allocation) was timely exercised. (2) In *In re BCBS Antitrust*, Tier-2 (Employee) claimants who received June 1 notification emails should confirm payment receipt within the four-to-eight week window; Tier-3 (Employer) payments have not yet commenced, and counsel with self-funded plan clients should confirm those accounts were properly segregated in the claim submission, as the self-funded class period runs from September 1, 2015 — a narrower window than the subscriber-side coverage period. (3) Businesses that purchased Google advertising on or after August 2016 should engage Keller Postman or independent counsel immediately, as state-law statutes of limitations on Clayton Act § 4 antitrust claims vary materially and state-specific deadlines may already be running; the treble-damages multiplier available under 15 U.S.C. § 15 makes this the most economically leveraged recovery opportunity currently in the mass arbitration pipeline, particularly for large enterprise advertisers who have been reluctant to file class actions due to public-profile concerns.
