# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Saturday, July 25, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Section 122 Is Gone — Replaced Overnight by Section 301 Forced-Labor Duties**

The single most consequential tariff development of the week — arguably of the post-*Learning Resources* era — occurred yesterday at 12:01 a.m. EDT: 
the 10 percent global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) expired by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 
Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation was ever pending. Absent congressional action, the surcharge terminated on its own terms.


The administration did not wait for a gap. 
On July 23, 2026 — one day before the Section 122 sunset — USTR announced a new Section 301 trade action addressing forced labor concerns. As of 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, imports from 60 designated countries and economies are subject to additional duties of 10% or 12.5%, unless specifically exempted.
 
The action covers the top 60 U.S. trading partners, representing 99.4% of U.S. imports, and took effect July 24, 2026.
 The substitution is nearly total in geographic reach, but the legal architecture is materially different — Section 301 carries no 150-day cap and no congressional extension requirement.

The rate structure is differentiated: 
10 percent is the rate for economies that impose a forced labor import prohibition, have committed to impose and enforce such a prohibition through an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade, or have imposed a partial regime with the effect of preventing the importation of certain forced labor goods.
 
Trading partners that have failed to adopt a forced labor import prohibition are subject to a 12.5% additional duty.
 
For five trading partners — the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, and Taiwan — the action applies a combined rate structure: if a product's column 1 duty rate already equals or exceeds the applicable Section 301 rate, no additional duty is assessed; if it falls below, duty is assessed so the combined column 1 and Section 301 rate equals the threshold.


**What Section 301 Means for the Refund Math**

The IEEPA refund pipeline is not affected by the Section 301 announcement — those are distinct legal instruments covering distinct collections — but the regime change recasts the forward-looking tariff environment that GCs and CFOs must plan around. 
USTR initiated 60 investigations under Section 302(b)(1) of the Trade Act of 1974 on March 12, 2026, examining whether economies had failed to impose and effectively enforce forced-labor import prohibitions. On June 2, USTR determined that actionable conduct existed in each investigation and published proposed remedial tariffs.
 
USTR received and reviewed over 1,600 written comments on the proposed responsive action. From July 7 to July 9, USTR held public hearings at which over 100 witnesses provided testimony.
 
CBP has issued entry filing guidance in CSMS #69326983, including the applicable Chapter 99 tariff headings (9903.05.20 through 9903.06.21).


An in-transit carve-out is available, but only briefly: 
a limited exemption applies to goods loaded onto a vessel at the port of loading and in transit on the final mode of transit before 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on July 24, 2026, provided those goods are entered for consumption or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption before 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on July 28, 2026.
 That four-day window closed this morning. Any importer who did not enter goods in that window — and who had been banking on the Section 122 expiration as a rate-reduction event — is now looking at indefinite Section 301 exposure rather than the MFN reversion many had modeled.

**CAPE Phase 3: Technical Readiness, Legal Paralysis**

The IEEPA refund process continues to grind forward on its own separate track, but the gap between CBP's technical capacity and its legal authorization to pay is widening. The universe remains large: 
approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties, spread across more than 53 million entries.
 As of CBP's July 13 status declaration to Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* (Court No. 25-00066), 
CBP reported that as of July 10, 2026, $86.3 billion in IEEPA refunds had actually been repaid, with $121.75 billion accepted as of that date.


Phase 3 — the critical tranche covering finally liquidated entries — remains technically imminent but legally frozen. 
In a July 15, 2026 order, Judge Eaton stated that, in connection with the upcoming launch of CAPE Phase 3, the court will direct CBP to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries under a procedure to be spelled out in a forthcoming order, to be entered in each of the approximately 3,700 IEEPA cases currently assigned to the court.
 
CBP is required to file a short progress report on CAPE with the court by 5:00 p.m. EDT on August 4, 2026.
 That filing will be the next meaningful public data point on Phase 3 timing.

The legal obstruction is DOJ's June 2 Federal Circuit appeal. 
On June 2, 2026, DOJ formally appealed the CIT's order requiring refunds of all IEEPA tariffs and simultaneously characterized the refund orders as impermissible universal injunctions, arguing that CIT lacks authority to grant relief extending beyond parties before the court.
 
Most notably, CBP asserted for the first time since the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs that it lacks the legal authority to issue IEEPA refunds on finally liquidated entries without an importer-specific court judgment. For importers who had been counting on CAPE to eventually cover their finally liquidated entries, CBP's new position is a significant setback: if CBP's reasoning prevails, those importers may need to file individual cases in federal court to obtain refunds.


The scale asymmetry is stark. 
Analysis of CBP's own data found that the $104.29 billion approved as of June 29 covered only about 30% of all IEEPA-affected import entries — meaning the dollars processed so far are concentrated in a small number of large, high-value entries, while many smaller importers remain stuck in the queue.
 
Separately, counsel who argued the original Supreme Court case have filed a class certification motion at the CIT seeking to represent importers who are not currently eligible for refunds under CAPE; the government is opposing certification.


**Section 122 Litigation: The Federal Circuit Appeal That Outlived the Tariff**

The Section 122 appeals did not terminate with the surcharge's statutory expiration. 
The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality. The CIT held the surcharge unlawful and the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling pending the government's appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, so CBP continued collecting through July 23. The July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period; the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the pending appeal.


The underlying CIT ruling was decided May 7, 2026 in the consolidated *State of Oregon v. Trump* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. Trump* (Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606), assigned to a three-judge panel of Chief Judge Mark A. Barnett, Judge Claire R. Kelly, and Senior Judge Timothy C. Stanceu. 
The CIT held Proclamation 11012 unlawful but limited relief: it entered a permanent injunction only as to the two private importer plaintiffs (Burlap & Barrel and Basic Fun!) and the State of Washington, while declining to enjoin collection on a nationwide basis.
 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805), consolidated appeals from the two CIT decisions. While the appeal proceeds on the merits, the government continued collecting Section 122 tariffs as to the covered parties, including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc., pending further order of the Federal Circuit.


The Federal Circuit merits briefing on Section 122 validity now proceeds against a surcharge that has already expired — but the stakes remain material. If the circuit court affirms the CIT's invalidity finding, importers who paid Section 122 duties between February 24 and July 23, 2026 will have a refund claim. The government will argue mootness and that the now-terminated surcharge warrants expedited dismissal; plaintiffs will press for a merits ruling that preserves refund rights under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest mechanics. 
The 180-day protest window from liquidation runs independently of the CAPE portal and is unaffected by the pending Federal Circuit appeal. Filing protests now preserves refund rights through a separate channel regardless of how the appeal resolves.


**IEEPA Litigation Posture: Foundational Architecture**

For new readers: the IEEPA refund saga is anchored in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250). 
Both the CIT and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, sitting en banc, held that IEEPA did not authorize the tariffs and granted summary judgment to plaintiffs. In August 2025, the Federal Circuit characterized the tariffs as "unbounded in scope, amount, and duration."
 
In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, argued November 5, 2025, and decided February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs.
 The CAPE refund system was built in direct response to those rulings and the subsequent CIT orders in the remanded V.O.S. Selections proceedings before Judge Eaton.

**Warehouse Entry Rule Change: CAPE Phase 1 Restriction**

A procedural note with real filing consequences: 
effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on a CAPE Declaration. Any warehouse entry submitted on a CAPE Declaration on or after that date is rejected with an "ENTRY TYPE NOT ALLOWED" error.
 Customs brokers who have been batching warehouse entries into CAPE submissions need to route those separately and verify with their broker the correct liquidation protest pathway under 19 U.S.C. § 1514.

## Recommended Actions

GCs and customs counsel with IEEPA exposure should focus the next two weeks on three discrete items. First, verify CAPE Phase 1 filing status against the $121.75 billion accepted figure — if your entries are not reflected, confirm whether the delay is a queue position or a Phase 3 finally-liquidated problem; for the latter, the August 4 CBP progress report to Judge Eaton (Court No. 25-00066) is your next data point, but do not wait to consult litigation counsel about whether you need an individual CIT action under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) to preserve finally-liquidated refund rights given DOJ's June 2 appeal posture. Second, audit every import entry from February 24 through July 23, 2026 for Section 122 exposure and file 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests before the applicable 180-day liquidation windows close — the Federal Circuit's merits ruling in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) is the predicate for any Section 122 refund, and an unfiled protest forfeits that claim. Third, immediately classify all open purchase orders against the Section 301 forced-labor duty matrix effective July 24 under CSMS #69326983 and Chapter 99 headings 9903.05.20 through 9903.06.21 — the in-transit window to July 28, 2026 has now closed, so any goods not yet cleared are subject to the 10%–12.5% additional duty stack on top of existing Section 232 and column 1 rates.
