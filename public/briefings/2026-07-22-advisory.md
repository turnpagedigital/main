### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Order, July 20, 2026

**What changed.** 
The U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the parties' proposed class settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, requiring Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to the authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from notorious pirate sites.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín, who inherited the approval stage from Judge Alsup, granted final approval on July 20, months after she pressed plaintiffs' counsel for a clearer account of their fees.


**Fee award — the court's principal intervention.** The fee question received the most substantive treatment in the order. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then trimmed that to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million.
 The arithmetic: 
the court applied a multiplier of 3.75 on class counsel's estimated lodestar of $27,082,963, concluding that total was reasonable
 — declining class counsel's requested 6.92x multiplier. 
By one calculation, the award works out to approximately $2,953 per hour across 34,381.6 hours of attorney time the court credited.
 
In contrast, the court also reduced the service award for each named author plaintiff from the requested $50,000 to $15,000.
 
The order also disallows opt-outs submitted after the February 9, 2026 deadline, with narrow exceptions for two named individuals whose untimely requests were allowed, and sets expense reimbursement at $2,635,197.46 plus a cost reserve of $18,220,000 in anticipated expenses.


**Claims rate and per-work recovery.** 
In aggregate, this marks the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77 percent.
 
Class counsel had filed papers showing 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate, against a typical class-action claim rate of around 10 percent.
 
That 91% rate represented a large increase over the 54% figure reported in March 19 papers, and with the higher claim rate the per-work payout will be closer to the $3,000 per work estimated at filing rather than the $4,876 figure that was projected at the 54% level.
 
Note that Anthropic is paying into the $1.5 billion settlement fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.


**Non-class-member objectors.** 
The most substantial objections came from people outside the settlement looking in; the court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but took care to remind those individuals that, precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.
 This is operationally significant: publishers who opted out of the *Bartz* class — including, by public report, Chicken Soup of the Soul and others — retain live claims, and the court's language is not a stray comment but an explicit preservation signal.

**Settlement posture — what counsel and Anthropic said publicly.** 
Anthropic's deputy general counsel Aparna Sridhar stated: "We reached this settlement in 2025, after the court's landmark ruling that training AI on books is fair use under copyright law, which remains the law today."
 That framing is contested and should be read carefully: the *Bartz* fair-use ruling applied only to the three named plaintiffs, not to the certified class. 
Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy, not the act of AI training, and as such the fair-use ruling on training applies only to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers.
 Counsel and claimants in downstream proceedings should not treat Anthropic's public characterization as settled law.

**What to watch next.** The 30-day appellate clock on the July 20 order is now running. Any objector who preserved rights below has until approximately August 19 to file a notice of appeal to the Ninth Circuit. Distribution cannot begin while any timely appeal is pending. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after any subsequent appeals are fully resolved, and payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest.
 Monitor the docket at CourtListener No. 69058235 and the settlement administrator — JND Legal Administration, operating AnthropicCopyrightSettlement.com — for distribution scheduling notices.

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.) — Cross-MSJ Set for October 21, 2026

The music-publisher track is now the primary live merits proceeding in the AI-copyright system. 
The docket — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.) — is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, with referrals to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen.
 
The summary judgment hearing is scheduled for October 21, 2026, with the core questions being: (1) whether partial summary judgment on copyright infringement should be granted to plaintiffs, and (2) whether summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement should be granted to Anthropic.


The two-sided MSJ posture maps directly onto the § 107 four-factor framework. 
Music publishers have asked the court to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrighted song lyrics through Claude, arguing that fair use does not excuse conduct because it used those lyrics to build a competing product.
 
Anthropic has argued that its use of copyrighted materials to train Claude is "transformative" fair use, comparing Claude's learning to how humans learn from reading and internalizing works.
 The transformativeness argument under § 107(1) is the same one Anthropic pressed — and won — on the training-data question in *Bartz*, but the *Concord* record adds a distinct output-reproduction dimension: publishers allege that Claude reproduces entire song lyrics verbatim on user request, which engages § 107(4) market-harm analysis in a way the book-training question did not.

A parallel second-generation suit — *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal.) — was filed January 28, 2026 and assigned to Judge Lee and Magistrate Van Keulen as well. 
The Concord II complaint alleges that Anthropic concealed during discovery in *Concord I* that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books — including hundreds or more books that contain publishers' musical compositions — from pirate library websites, and that publishers did not learn of this until July 2025 when Judge Alsup's ruling in *Bartz* publicly revealed Anthropic's torrenting conduct for the first time.
 *Concord II* effectively pleads a discovery-fraud theory that, if credited, has implications beyond copyright: it could reopen questions about litigation conduct in *Concord I* and potentially affect whether the *Concord I* summary judgment record is complete.

For rights-holder counsel, October 21 is the date to watch. A plaintiffs' win on output-reproduction infringement would be the first judicial holding that AI inference — not just training — constitutes actionable copyright infringement, a result that would reframe licensing negotiations across the entire AI industry.

### III. *In re OpenAI Copyright MDL*, No. 1:25-md-03143-SHS-OTW (S.D.N.Y.) — Sanctions Motion, Discovery Standoff


The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein in the Southern District of New York, with referrals to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang.
 The case continues to consolidate constituent actions. 
A case against OpenAI and Microsoft for allegedly scraping the copyrighted works of approximately 400 small newspapers will remain on hold pending resolution of motions filed in the broader MDL.
 
Those news outlets, led by Richner Communications, sued in June alleging AI scraping without authorization, and the suit has since been consolidated into the MDL per a joint stipulation and order filed in the S.D.N.Y.


The discovery front has sharpened materially. 
A filing on July 9 in the Manhattan federal courthouse alleged that OpenAI "chose obstruction" over releasing data sets and ChatGPT logs that could show how the AI system used copyrighted news content; the NYT, Daily News, and other media outlets are asking Judge Stein to impose sanctions on OpenAI.
 A sanctions motion in an MDL of this scale is not a routine procedural skirmish — it signals that discovery is not proceeding in good faith and, if granted, could result in adverse inferences that would be felt in every constituent case. The underlying theory is straightforward: without the training logs, plaintiffs cannot establish that specific works were ingested and that Claude outputs are causally connected to that ingestion. OpenAI's resistance to that production is therefore not merely a discovery dispute but a potential liability-limitation strategy.


The court had ruled on March 26, 2025 that all copyright infringement claims survive OpenAI and Microsoft's motion to dismiss, while dismissing the unfair competition claim with prejudice and most DMCA claims without prejudice.
 The case is now fully in discovery, and the sanctions motion is the leading indicator of how that phase will resolve. A ruling on sanctions — and any attendant disclosure order — is likely before year-end and will reprice risk across the AI-copyright docket.

### IV. System-Level Read-Across: What *Bartz* Final Approval Changes for Downstream Claimants

The closing of *Bartz* on the merits does three things for the broader AI-copyright system. First, it sets a recoverable-value floor: 
at approximately $3,000 per book, the *Bartz* payout dwarfs the going rate in most AI copyright disputes, and it lands while comparable cases against Meta, Google, and OpenAI grind on.
 Rights-holder counsel in those parallel proceedings will use the $3,000-per-work figure as a floor, not a ceiling, in licensing and settlement discussions — particularly where the defendant's conduct is more recent and the fair-use record is less favorable than Anthropic's training-data argument.

Second, 
Anthropic denied any wrongdoing in agreeing to the settlement, and the infringement question was not adjudicated; the settlement does not create binding precedent that future courts must follow.
 Defendants in *Concord*, the OpenAI MDL, *Kadrey v. Meta*, and the Getty matters can and will argue that the *Bartz* settlement reflects business pragmatism, not legal concession. That argument is technically correct — which is precisely why the *Concord* October MSJ matters so much.

Third, the fee-reduction signal from Judge Martínez-Olguín is worth noting: a court willing to cut a requested fee from $187.5 million to $101.5 million — declining a 6.92x lodestar multiplier in favor of 3.75x — is signaling that copyright class counsel in AI cases face real fee risk at the approval stage. Counsel in future AI settlements should price that risk into fee structures from the term sheet forward.

On the *Concord* track, the October 21 summary judgment hearing warrants close attention: a holding that Claude's output-reproduction of lyrics constitutes infringement would be the first judicial ruling that AI inference — independent of training — violates 17 U.S.C. § 106, fundamentally repricing AI licensing across the industry; rights-holders with live claims against any LLM defendant should monitor the *Concord I* briefing record and consider whether amicus participation is available or appropriate. In the OpenAI MDL, the July 9 sanctions motion is the most consequential near-term development: if Judge Stein or Magistrate Wang grants sanctions and compels production of training logs and ChatGPT inference data, that disclosure will function as a discovery unlock for every constituent case; claimants' counsel should track the docket at CourtListener No. 69879510 and be prepared to move quickly on related discovery once any production order issues.
