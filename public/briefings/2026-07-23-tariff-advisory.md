## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: The Section 122 Clock Expires in Hours — and the Refund Question Runs Forward, Not Back


The 10 percent global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2132, expires by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24. Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending. Absent congressional action, the surcharge terminates on its own terms.
 The administration cannot extend it unilaterally. As of this writing, Congress has shown no movement. The deadline is not theoretical — it is a statutory hard stop embedded in the Trade Act of 1974.


The surcharge's statutory expiration is analytically distinct from the pending litigation over its legality. The Court of International Trade held the surcharge unlawful, and the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling pending the government's appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, so CBP has continued to collect the duty on entries through July 23. The July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period — the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the pending appeal.


That is the critical framing for today: the surcharge dying tomorrow is not the same as importers recovering what they paid. The two questions now run on separate tracks — the forward rate question (what replaces Section 122?) and the backward recovery question (who gets refunds, and when?).

### Track One: The Post-§122 Rate Landscape and the Section 301 Handover


Section 301, Section 232, AD/CVD, MPF, and product-specific duties still remain in place after the Section 122 expiration.
 The operative question is whether USTR finalizes a Section 301 replacement before or after midnight. 
USTR's Section 301 investigation, with a determination issued June 2 and a public hearing held July 7, proposes replacement duties of 10 to 12.5 percent that carry no statutory expiration — unlike Section 122's built-in 150-day sunset.
 
USTR has proposed Section 301 forced-labor duties of 10% and 12.5% — under USTR's categories, 14 economies fall in the 10% tier and 46 in the 12.5% default tier — covering most major U.S. sourcing countries. Unlike Section 122, which is capped by statute at 15% and roughly 150 days, Section 301 duties carry no expiration date and no rate cap.



The window for USTR to publish the final Section 301 forced-labor rule runs July 24 through August 24.
 
Any gap beyond 30 days would signal legal or political complications.
 For importers, the planning problem is acute: 
for certain target countries such as China, Thailand, Vietnam, and India, the administration intends to replace Section 122 tariffs with Section 301 tariffs in late July. To qualify for the current Section 122 10% rate rather than a potentially higher Section 301 rate, goods will likely need to arrive before July 24.
 
Based on the timeline, the additional Section 301 tariffs may be imposed before the end of July.



Section 301, unlike Section 122 or IEEPA, requires an administrative record, public comment, and formal determinations. That makes the tariffs more durable in court — but it also means every rate change must be justified and documented. The era of presidential tariff-by-tweet is replaced by something more bureaucratic, more methodical, and ultimately more permanent.
 That durability cuts both ways: harder to challenge, but also harder to rescind through bilateral deal-making without a formal record.

The White House has signaled new trade action is imminent — CNBC reported this week that the administration is working to re-create a global tariff regime — though specifics on timing and statutory authority remain unannounced as of this writing.

### Track Two: IEEPA Refund Pipeline — CAPE Progress, Phase 3 Timeline, and the Class Certification Wildcard


CBP's July 13 court filing shows $86.3 billion repaid and $121.75 billion accepted as of July 10.
 Against the $166 billion total IEEPA refund obligation created by *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 607 U.S. ___ (2026), that means roughly $44 billion in accepted-but-not-yet-paid claims remain outstanding — and a substantial tail of entries has not cleared even the acceptance stage.

The distribution of completed refunds is structurally skewed. 
A Cato Institute analysis of CBP's own court filings found that the $104.29 billion in refunds approved as of June 29 covered only about 30% of all import entries on which IEEPA duties were paid, even though it represents roughly 60% of the total dollar value owed. In practice, the largest, highest-value entries — concentrated among large importers — have moved through CAPE fastest, while the much larger number of smaller-dollar entries, more likely held by small and mid-sized importers, remain in the queue.



CBP launched CAPE Phase 1, enabling electronic filing of IEEPA tariff refunds through the ACE Portal starting April 20, 2026.
 
CBP has not yet shared guidance related to Phase 2 IEEPA refunds. Importers are advised to file protests against all entries that timed out of Phase 1 to protect their right to refunds. Protests should generally be filed around 150 days after liquidation, but before the 180-day post-liquidation deadline under 19 U.S.C. § 1514.



Phase 3, covering finally liquidated entries estimated at over $30 billion, is targeted for late July 2026 but applies only to importers who filed litigation at the Court of International Trade.
 That eligibility restriction is the key friction point for the broader class of SME importers. 
Separately, counsel who argued the original Supreme Court case have filed a class certification motion at the CIT seeking to represent importers who are not currently eligible for refunds under CAPE; the government is opposing certification.
 If class certification is granted, it could structurally expand the refund-eligible universe; if denied, smaller importers without active CIT litigation face real risk of missing the recovery window entirely.


Many importers are filing protests out of an abundance of caution to preserve their rights to IEEPA refunds, given that the timetable for Phase 2 of CAPE has yet to be announced by CBP.
 The protest mechanism under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 remains the primary defensive tool for importers whose entries have liquidated outside the CAPE timeline.

### Track Three: *State of Oregon v. Trump* Federal Circuit Appeal — What the Stay Means for Section 122 Refund Claims


On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel of the CIT held that President Trump exceeded the statutory authority under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 when he imposed the 10 percent "balance-of-payments" tariffs. *State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026). Although the court invalidated the tariff action on the merits, it declined to issue nationwide relief, instead limiting its injunction to the specific importer plaintiffs.



The majority held that Proclamation 11012 exceeded the President's authority under Section 122 because the administration's interpretation of the statute was overly expansive and the proclamation failed to identify the type of balance-of-payments deficits the statute required. However, the court limited its relief to parties that had demonstrated standing as importers: the State of Washington (whose state university imported goods subject to the tariffs), Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.
 
Senior Judge Stanceu dissented, arguing that Section 122 did not limit "balance-of-payments deficits" to the historical measures the majority identified.



On June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805, consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs imposed under Proclamation No. 11012 and Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. While the appeal proceeds on the merits, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 "balance-of-payments" tariffs as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions, including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc., pending further order of the Federal Circuit.



The panel found that the government made a sufficient showing of likely success on the merits, but it expressly declined to adopt any final interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" at this stage.
 That hedge is significant: a merits panel — potentially en banc — could reach a different conclusion on the statutory construction question. The stay order is not a signal of how the Federal Circuit will rule; it is a procedural ceiling while the court does the work. 
Another potential avenue for the administration is simply to issue a new proclamation grounded in the specific balance-of-payments metrics the majority identified as permissible, although it is unclear if this would retroactively "cure" the deficiencies of the proclamation for past entries.
 Given that the §122 surcharge expires tomorrow, any cure proclamation would have no prospective effect — it would be solely litigation positioning on the backward-looking refund question.

The practical implication: 
the CIT emphasized that requiring plaintiffs to continue paying deemed unlawful Section 122 surcharges, only to wait for potential refunds over an extended period, constitutes a meaningful harm.
 That finding does not bind the Federal Circuit on merits, but it does frame the equitable posture for any future refund order.

### Track Four: The Importer-vs.-Consumer "Windfall" Litigation — Nintendo's Motion to Dismiss

A new downstream front opened this week with direct implications for how IEEPA refunds are characterized once received. 
Nintendo has requested the dismissal of a proposed class action lawsuit filed by customers seeking tariff refunds. In April 2026, plaintiffs Gregory Hoffert and Prashant Sharan claimed Nintendo benefited from tariffs imposed by the U.S. government by choosing to raise the price of console hardware and peripherals — including the original Switch console and Switch 2 controllers — instead of absorbing the cost itself, and then seeking refunds when the Supreme Court deemed those tariffs illegal.



In its latest filing in the class action, Nintendo argues it doesn't owe gamers anything, saying "plaintiffs are not entitled to a rebate simply because of intervening legal developments related to tariffs."
 
Nintendo claims consumers were not forced to pay for its products after price hikes were implemented, and could have chosen to "abstain from purchasing the product or seek out competing products." The company believes the case should be handled through private arbitration, but has requested it be dismissed outright in the event arbitration is denied.



Sony is facing a similar lawsuit over claims it is set to receive a "double recovery windfall" in relation to tariffs.
 
Nintendo of America filed its own CIT lawsuit on March 6, 2026 targeting the tariffs levied under IEEPA, demanding a full refund of all duties paid with interest and attorney fees, naming as defendants the U.S. Department of the Treasury, the Department of Homeland Security, USTR, CBP, and the Department of Commerce.


The legal question at the core of the consumer class action is whether an importer who raised consumer prices in response to an ultimately-unlawful tariff, and who then receives a refund from CBP, has been unjustly enriched or is otherwise obligated to pass through the recovery. Nintendo's contractual framing — that consumers received exactly what they bargained for at an agreed price — is the standard common-law answer. Whether courts accept that framing, or whether state consumer-protection statutes impose a different duty, is unresolved. The outcome here could meaningfully affect how publicly-traded importers account for expected IEEPA refunds in their financial statements and shareholder disclosures.

### Background: The IEEPA Invalidation — *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *V.O.S. Selections*

For context carried forward from prior advisories: 
in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, argued November 5, 2025 and decided February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's August 2025 en banc ruling.
 
Refund claimants secured a procedural victory on March 2, 2026, when the Federal Circuit denied the government a stay and ordered *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, Case No. 25-1812, remanded back to the CIT.
 
Chief Justice Roberts wrote that reading IEEPA's power to "regulate" imports as including a taxing power would render the statute partly unconstitutional.



The DOJ expects "hundreds, if not thousands" of importers to file their own challenges at the CIT.
 As of the March 6 Nintendo filing, that wave is ongoing, with the CAPE pipeline as the primary administrative channel and § 1514 protest practice as the fallback.

## Recommended Actions

For importers and recovery counsel: (1) confirm that goods currently on the water are timed to enter before 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24 if the Section 122 rate is preferable to anticipated Section 301 replacement rates — entries after the sunset shift to a different duty landscape, likely at 12.5% under the proposed Section 301 framework, with no expiration; (2) if you have unliquidated entries not yet processed under CAPE Phase 1, file Post-Summary Corrections now — CBP's July 13 filing confirms $86.3 billion repaid and $121.75 billion accepted, but the distribution is heavily skewed toward large-entry importers, and smaller importers risk being stranded in the queue absent active protest filings under 19 U.S.C. § 1514; (3) for entries that have liquidated outside the CAPE window, file protests within 180 days of liquidation — Phase 2 guidance has not yet been published, and waiting for CBP to set the schedule risks expiry of the protest deadline; (4) monitor the class certification motion at the CIT — if granted, it could provide a recovery vehicle for importers without active CIT litigation; if denied, those importers face a materially narrowed path; (5) for importers who raised consumer prices and are now pursuing IEEPA refunds, retain state consumer-protection counsel to assess pass-through exposure before the refund hits the books — the Nintendo and Sony litigation establishes that the plaintiff theory is live and the motion-to-dismiss ruling, whichever way it goes, will set the early template; and (6) watch for a USTR Federal Register notice on the Section 301 forced-labor final rule — the July 24–August 24 window is the operative publication target, and any gap beyond 30 days signals complications that could affect rate continuity for sourcing from Vietnam, India, Thailand, and South Korea.
