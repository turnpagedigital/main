### I. Bartz v. Anthropic — Final Approval Under Submission; Fee Reduction and Distribution Posture


The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417, took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement.
 The hearing ran 75 minutes. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement, though given the tone of the hearing most observers expect her to give final approval relatively quickly. Seven objectors were each given two minutes to present their objections.


The attorneys' fees issue dominated. Class counsel had originally sought 25% of the fund — $375 million. 
Plaintiffs urged the court to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 The reduction to $187.5 million (12.5% of the $1.5 billion fund) reflects post-hearing pressure and expands the pro rata per-work pool. 
At one point during the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín asked counsel to identify three leading cases that support the size of the fee request.
 
Whether the court will accept those justifications in full, in part, or with modifications is one of the things final approval will tell us.


Claim economics are now locked. 
Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed the payout per work will be $3,100, with 92.77% participation on the works list.
 
440,490 of 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a 91.3% rate, far above the typical class action claim rate of around 10%.
 That participation surge, driven by last-minute filings before the March 30, 2026 claims deadline, drove the per-work payout to the floor of original estimates. 
The high claim rate means forecasts of a larger payout based on the March 19 report of 54% participation will not happen, and the actual payout will be closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.


Post-hearing, the court issued a structured order. 
By May 21, 2026, Anthropic could file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; plaintiffs were to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026. The court stated it would not consider any further submissions from objectors.


For claims investors and rights-holder counsel, the critical unknown is now the objector appeal window. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 If any of the seven objectors who appeared at the May 14 hearing notice an appeal to the Ninth Circuit after the final approval order issues, distribution could slip into 2027. The settlement's installment payment structure — with $450 million not due until September 25, 2026 and the final $450 million not until September 25, 2027 — means Anthropic's cash position does not fully fund the class until that date regardless of appeal posture. 
Upon final approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and to pay the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.


One framing point that matters for any cross-case analysis: 
the scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood — Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training.
 The liability kernel is Anthropic's use of LibGen and Pirate Library Mirror — wholesale torrenting of pirated content — not the legal-acquisition-plus-training-data question that Judges Alsup (in *Bartz* on pre-settlement summary judgment) and other California judges addressed. That distinction controls how far Bartz travels as precedent.

### II. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Parallel Torrenting Theory; Second Action Filed January 2026

The music-publisher track against Anthropic has bifurcated into two parallel N.D. Cal. dockets before Judge Eumi K. Lee, both referred to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen.

**Concord I** (*Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK, N.D. Cal., filed Oct. 18, 2023) covers Anthropic's direct training on lyrics and output reproduction. 
Plaintiffs — led by Concord Music Group, Inc. and Universal Music Corp. — brought claims for direct and secondary copyright infringement and removal of CMI, alleging Anthropic infringes their exclusive rights in musical lyrics by using their works to train Claude and by distributing the lyrics in Claude's outputs.
 The court has already trimmed the secondary liability claims. 
The court found in favor of Anthropic in two consecutive decisions: denying the preliminary injunction as overbroad and failing to establish irreparable harm, and granting Anthropic's motion to dismiss contributory and vicarious infringement claims. The court also found that plaintiffs did not sufficiently allege that Anthropic acted intentionally as to the removal of CMI.
 Cross-motions for summary judgment on the surviving direct-infringement and fair-use theories remain pending; the docket confirms the last update was June 22, 2026.

**Concord II** (*Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880, N.D. Cal., filed Jan. 28, 2026) is the Bartz-theory music case. 
The January 28 complaint alleges that Anthropic concealed during discovery in Concord I that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books — including hundreds or more books that plainly contain publishers' musical compositions — from pirate library websites, and that publishers did not learn of this until July 2025 when Judge Alsup's ruling in Bartz publicly revealed Anthropic's torrenting for the first time.
 
On January 28, 2026, Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO Music filed this amended complaint against Anthropic claiming $3.1 billion in damages, on the theory that Anthropic acquired its music training data by torrenting — the same piracy theory that drove the Bartz settlement.



Anthropic filed a motion to stay in Concord II, with a motion hearing set for May 20, 2026, in San Jose, Courtroom 7, 4th Floor before Judge Eumi K. Lee.
 The stay argument almost certainly tracks the Bartz settlement — Anthropic would argue that pending final approval of a $1.5 billion settlement covering the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy conduct moots or substantially narrows the Concord II torrenting claims. The outcome of that motion, combined with Judge Martínez-Olguín's still-pending Bartz final approval order, creates a procedural pinch point with direct consequences for $3.1 billion in additional exposure.

The $1B+ § 504 statutory damages arithmetic for Concord II is worth noting: 
the complaint alleges Anthropic violated plaintiffs' copyrights by copying 499 of their works as training input and output, and separately identifies 20,517 works in Exhibit B for the broader infringement theory.
 At statutory damages ceilings of $150,000 per work for willful infringement under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2), the 20,517-work catalog alone could support exposure far in excess of the $3.1 billion stated damages figure.

### III. In re OpenAI Copyright MDL — Log Production Escalates; SDNY-N.D. Cal. Circuit Split Deepens


*In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.), before Judge Sidney H. Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, was updated as recently as June 26, 2026.
 
The consolidated action combines 16 copyright lawsuits
, including *The New York Times Co. v. OpenAI, Inc. & Microsoft Corp.*, No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y.). 
Lead cases include the New York Times (filed December 27, 2023), the New York Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting; authors including John Grisham, George R.R. Martin, Jonathan Franzen, and Jodi Picoult are plaintiffs in the consolidated author suits.


The discovery posture is now materially expanded beyond what earlier advisories tracked. 
On January 5, 2026, Judge Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20 million-log sample — not just cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' works — marking a significant discovery victory for news organizations and authors, and offering a window into how courts treat user-privacy claims when AI companies face copyright liability.
 That was not the ceiling. 
On March 9, 2026, the court granted plaintiffs' motion to compel and ordered OpenAI to produce "the reservoirs of 78 million and 10 million logs," on top of the 20 million-log sample already ordered to be produced.
 Discovery is ongoing.

The analytical significance for rights-holders: 
Judge Wang found that even output logs without reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's fair use defense. Fair use analysis examines, among other factors, how the challenged use affects the market for the original works. Logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal patterns relevant to whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works.
 This is the § 107(4) market-harm factor — the fourth and frequently decisive fair-use factor — being built out through massive-scale output discovery rather than anecdotal sampling. 
If plaintiffs demonstrate that ChatGPT routinely generates outputs that compete with or substitute for copyrighted content — even when users aren't specifically requesting plaintiffs' works — OpenAI's fair use defense becomes considerably harder to sustain.


The SDNY-N.D. Cal. divergence is a live circuit-level risk. 
Judge Stein's ruling allows The Times to continue pursuing claims that OpenAI's outputs included copyrighted content and resulted in user infringement, which diverges from the stance of California judges who have dismissed similar allegations.
 Judge Alsup's June 2025 summary judgment ruling in *Bartz* found legally acquired training data was fair use; Judge Stein in the MDL has permitted output-infringement theories to proceed. 
Courts splitting on this issue may encourage appellate courts to weigh in on whether training generative AI models on pirated works can ever be fair use.
 Summary judgment briefing in the NYT matter was tracking a close-out around April 2026; how Judge Stein rules will be the first federal appellate-grade merits signal on news-publisher output claims.

Privacy safeguards did not override discovery obligations. 
The court acknowledged that ChatGPT users have "sincere" privacy interests in their conversations, but Judge Stein found those interests adequately protected by three safeguards: reducing the sample from tens of billions to 20 million logs, OpenAI's de-identification process, and the existing protective order governing discovery materials.
 The subsequent March 9 order compelling an additional 88 million logs suggests the court views de-identification plus protective orders as the durable framework — not a reason to limit log production.

### IV. Concord I Fair-Use Framework — Relevance to Cross-Case Analysis

The Concord I fair-use cross-motions are the most consequential undecided § 107 proceeding in the music-publisher AI litigation. Unlike *Bartz*, which was resolved before a trial on the piracy theory, Concord I presents the output-reproduction theory on its merits — specifically, whether Anthropic's reproduction of lyrics in Claude's outputs constitutes fair use or direct infringement. 
One day after denying injunctive relief, the court granted Anthropic's motion to dismiss the contributory infringement claims, holding that allegations that unidentified users "might" use Claude to seek copyrighted lyrics were insufficient to state a claim.
 What survived is the direct infringement theory on training-data input and the live output-reproduction claim backed by evidence of actual Claude outputs. The fair-use ruling on those surviving claims will either compress or expand the litigation space for every music publisher with a pending or contemplated AI training claim.

Disney/Universal Studios v. Midjourney (C.D. Cal.) presents the visual-art analog. 
Disney and its subsidiaries, including Lucasfilm and Marvel, along with other studios, allege that Midjourney unlawfully copied their copyrighted works to train its AI image-generation service, and that it continues to infringe by reproducing, publicly displaying, and distributing derivative images of the companies' iconic characters.
 The output-derivative theory in that case is structurally parallel to the Concord I output-lyrics theory — both turn on whether AI-generated content that resembles protected expression is itself an infringing derivative work under 17 U.S.C. § 106(2).

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should take four immediate steps. First, confirm client positions in the Bartz class as of today: 
payouts will only be issued after final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved
, so any claim-trading thesis that assumes near-term distribution must price in the objector-appeal window (30 days post-order to the Ninth Circuit) and the installment payment schedule — Anthropic's next fund contribution is due 
September 25, 2026
, making a pre-appeal-resolution distribution of more than the first installment mathematically impossible for most claimants. Second, evaluate Concord II exposure as an independent recovery vector: the $3.1 billion damages claim in No. 5:26-cv-00880 is not released by the Bartz settlement, and the torrenting theory — identical in kind to the Bartz piracy finding — survived the motion-to-dismiss phase in *Bartz*; the only question is whether Anthropic's stay motion before Judge Lee succeeds in suspending the case pending Bartz final approval and appeal. Third, treat the OpenAI MDL log-production orders as the leading indicator for settlement leverage: 
legal analysts suggest the logs, once analyzed, will effectively dictate settlement terms for the remainder of the consolidated litigation
 — any client with registered works in the MDL class should be building a damages model now, before the log analysis concludes and the leverage window closes. Fourth, monitor the Concord I fair-use ruling as the cross-case circuit signal: a ruling against Anthropic on the surviving direct-infringement training-input or output-reproduction theories would shorten the litigation runway for every pending AI-copyright defendant significantly and raise the floor on licensing-rate discussions across the market.
