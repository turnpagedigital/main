## Analysis & Developments

### The 17-Day Clock: Section 122 Expires July 24 — and the Government Still Has No Replacement in Place

The single most time-sensitive development in the tariff-refund landscape is the imminent hard expiration of the Section 122 surcharge. 
The 10% ad valorem surcharge imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) is scheduled to expire on July 24, 2026 — 17 days from today.
 
The surcharge will expire in any event on July 24, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely.
 That formulation — offered by Skadden in a May 20 client alert — captures the practical reality: expiration of § 122 does not mean zero tariffs. It means a gap in coverage while Section 301 investigations mature.


The administration recently initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices. The administration reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.
 
Historically, Section 301 probe timelines have ranged between 6 and 18 months before a recommendation phase, with tariffs typically implemented a few weeks later — though these may proceed faster, given that the 10% Section 122 tariff is set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 Whether USTR can compress that schedule to bridge the gap remains unverified; watch for a preliminary determination or accelerated public comment closure as a signal.

The significance for refund-claimants: a successful § 301 rollout at or before July 24 would replace the § 122 surcharge with a new duty layer, reset landed-cost exposure, and create a fresh body of potential refund claims — but only if those § 301 rates are themselves subsequently challenged and invalidated. Unlike the IEEPA and § 122 experience, § 301 has withstood prior judicial scrutiny. 
The Supreme Court's February 20 decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Courts have rejected prior attempts to limit the scope of Section 232 and Section 301.

### CAPE Phase 1 Progress — and the $30B+ Finally-Liquidated Category That Phase 1 Won't Touch

The IEEPA refund pipeline now has a functioning mechanical structure, but material amounts of money remain stranded outside it. The status as of Holland & Knight's late-June update — the most recent firm-level compilation this desk has confirmed:


The government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.
 For context, 
in all of last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


The Flexport-reported figure as of the May 11 status filing with the CIT adds granularity: 
as of May 11, 2026, 126,237 CAPE declarations had been submitted since the April 20 launch; 86,874 of those declarations passed file validations; 15,123,221 individual entries with IEEPA duties passed entry-specific validations and were accepted for removal of IEEPA duties; 8,338,081 of those accepted entries had been liquidated and/or reliquidated without IEEPA duties; and the anticipated refund and interest amount for those approximately 8.3 million entries was approximately $35.46 billion.


The critical exposure that Phase 1 cannot resolve: 
a large category of entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund clock started (the "finally liquidated" category) — remains contested, with the government estimating the dollar exposure at potentially more than $30 billion.
 
Phase 3, targeted for late July 2026, will cover finally liquidated entries — but, critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.


This is the defining action-forcing constraint for non-plaintiff importers with early-cycle entries: filing a CIT complaint is now a prerequisite to Phase 3 recovery, not merely an alternative track. 
Given these uncertainties, importers wishing to obtain refunds of their IEEPA tariffs should pursue a dual-track strategy of filing suit at the CIT and fulfilling administrative requirements, including timely filing of administrative protests with CBP under 19 U.S.C. § 1514.


The DOJ has appealed the CIT's underlying universal refund order — the order that authorized refunds to non-plaintiffs in the first place. 
On June 3, 2026, DOJ filed notices of appeal at the Federal Circuit. At the June 9 hearing, DOJ reiterated that the CIT erred and the court cannot order the government to refund money to non-plaintiffs. Whether the CIT or DOJ is correct will be resolved at the Federal Circuit.
 
The government is anticipated to argue that the CIT's refund orders function as impermissible universal injunctions.
 If DOJ prevails on that theory, non-plaintiff importers who have not filed individual CIT actions could be cut out of the Phase 1 and Phase 2 pipeline retroactively — not just Phase 3.

### Section 122 Appellate Posture: CAFC Stay Granted June 11 — Surcharge Collected Through Expiration

The Section 122 track — *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 — has reached a stable pre-expiration posture, but the appellate record will matter for post-July 24 refund litigation.

Recapping the procedural sequence: 
On May 7, 2026, the CIT rejected President Trump's invocation of Section 122 to impose a temporary global 10% tariff on imports from nearly every country for the 150-day period.
 
In a 2–1 decision, CIT majority Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted and, instead, improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 
Judge Stanceu dissented, arguing the majority read too much into legislative history and resolved the dispute prematurely on an expedited record.



The government appealed on May 8, the day after the CIT's opinion. On May 12, the CAFC granted the government's motion for an immediate administrative stay such that the CIT's permanent injunction is temporarily stayed while the appellate court considers a more prolonged stay during the duration of the appeal.
 
On June 11, 2026, the CAFC granted the federal government's motion to stay the lower court's injunction. This ruling allows the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 surcharges from the plaintiffs who had previously won relief — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. The CAFC applied the traditional four-factor test and concluded that three of four factors favored the government, while the fourth was neutral. The CAFC's decision effectively pauses relief, meaning the 10% surcharge will continue to be collected from all importers throughout the duration of the appeals process.


The net result: even the three named plaintiffs who won at the CIT are currently paying the surcharge, because the CAFC stay wiped out their injunctive relief. 
The Section 122 decision is significant for importers and other companies affected by the surcharge, but it does not automatically end tariff exposure, stop collections for all importers, or create immediate refund rights.


One practical note on the statutory-expiration interaction: 
the fact that the CIT did not grant a universal injunction may indicate that — even if the CIT's decision is upheld on appeal — further litigation could be required for additional importers to obtain tariff refunds. Importers considering such litigation must weigh the potential for obtaining an injunction against the possibility that follow-on litigation will be stayed pending the CAFC appeal and that the Section 122 tariffs are set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 In other words, by the time the CAFC reaches a merits ruling, the underlying surcharge will have sunset by operation of law — making the refund question the only live relief available.

### Atmus Filtration — CAPE Statutory Mechanics and the CBP Refund Architecture

The refund-architecture case, *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* (CIT), was the vehicle through which Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton constructed the operative refund framework. 
In *Atmus Filtration*, Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued a series of orders directing CBP to process refunds. On March 4, 2026, the CIT ordered that "all importers of record" that paid IEEPA-based duties are "entitled to the benefit of" the *Learning Resources* decision and directed CBP to determine final tariff assessments "without regard to" the IEEPA duties.
 
On March 27, Judge Eaton issued an amended order expanding the directive to cover all entries — including entries for which liquidation had already become final — and ordering CBP to liquidate or reliquidate all such entries without regard to IEEPA duties.
 
*Atmus Filtration* was voluntarily dismissed on April 6, 2026.


The CBP official portal (cbp.gov) confirms the current CAPE mechanics: 
effective June 29, 2026, CAPE accepts entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, 06) for which the reconciliation entry (type 09) has not been filed, limited to unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation.
 
Importers and authorized brokers should anticipate that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.
 A framework complication of immediate practical relevance: 
framework agreements with the EU, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland/Liechtenstein required that the Column 1 general duty be paid along with IEEPA duties such that the combined rate equaled 15%. Importers under those arrangements will not receive a refund of the full 15% because a portion accounts for the Column 1 general duty, which remains in effect.

### The Statutory Backbone: *Learning Resources* — What the February 20 Ruling Locked In

For the benefit of any client working through an IEEPA refund claim for the first time: the controlling authority is 
the Supreme Court's February 20, 2026 decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250), holding 6-3 that IEEPA does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs.
 
The CIT now faces the formidable task of overseeing what could be the largest government refund obligation in American history: an estimated $175 billion in IEEPA tariff collections.



Following the ruling, the government ceased assessing and collecting importer deposits of IEEPA tariffs as of February 24, 2026.
 
On January 8, 2026, the administration had stipulated that it would refund IEEPA tariffs — including IEEPA tariffs imposed on Brazil and India that were not at issue in the Supreme Court litigation — for all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs following a "final and unappealable decision" ordering the government to issue refunds.
 That stipulation, read alongside the DOJ's June 3 Federal Circuit appeal of the universal refund order, reveals the government's current posture: it agreed to refund plaintiffs but is litigating vigorously against universal non-plaintiff refunds.

### Forced-Labor Section 301 Track — Nations Push Back

The Politico headline in today's scan — "Nations push back on forced labor findings" — maps directly to the second of the two sweeping § 301 investigations USTR initiated on March 11, 2026. 
A Section 301 investigation covering 60 economies, including Mexico, is tasked with determining whether insufficient forced-labor enforcement is creating an unfair cost advantage and burdening U.S. commerce.
 
These investigations are designed to create the legal foundation for a new long-term tariff regime that can avoid the kind of constitutional vulnerabilities that voided the IEEPA tariffs and threaten the current Section 122 action.
 Specific article text for the Politico forced-labor piece was not available in today's scan; full article content was not reproduced in the feed. What is verifiable: pushback from trading partners on forced-labor findings is a routine feature of the § 301 public-comment process and, to the extent it delays or narrows the USTR's final determinations, it extends the window before any § 301 replacement tariff takes effect — which in turn extends the gap between § 122 expiration and the next duty regime.

## Recommended Actions

Clients with IEEPA duty exposure should treat the next 72 hours as the effective deadline for three concurrent tasks. **First**, confirm CAPE filing status for every affected entry: 
once an entry is liquidated or reliquidated through CAPE, the importer has up to 180 days to file a protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514; entries outside CAPE Phase 1 should have their final-liquidation dates carefully monitored.
 **Second**, assess whether any unliquidated or recently liquidated entries fall in the reconciliation category that became eligible for CAPE on June 29 — those are claimable now. **Third**, and most urgently before July 24: clients who paid Section 122 duties and are not among the three named plaintiffs in *Burlap and Barrel* / *Oregon* should evaluate whether filing an individual CIT action is warranted to preserve refund rights, given that the CAFC's June 11 stay means refund relief for non-plaintiffs can come only through new litigation or a favorable Federal Circuit merits ruling — and the surcharge sunsets in 17 days, after which protest windows begin running. 
Companies should promptly assess whether they paid, absorbed, or passed through the surcharge and whether any protest, litigation, or contract-related steps are needed to preserve potential recovery.
 On the IEEPA track, 
Phase 3 refunds — covering finally liquidated entries with potential exposure above $30 billion — will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT; approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected, if CBP proceeds as planned, to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined, regardless of liquidation status.
 Any client that has not yet filed should consult counsel immediately; the gap between plaintiffs and non-plaintiffs in Phase 3 is not a procedural technicality — it is the difference between receiving a check and writing a brief.
