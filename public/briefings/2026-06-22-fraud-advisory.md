### I. OneCoin / DOJ Remission Process — $40M Fund Open; Claims Gap Is the Story


The DOJ has opened a compensation process for victims of the OneCoin crypto Ponzi scheme, drawing from a $40 million fund of seized assets.
 The recovery-to-loss ratio is stark: 
OneCoin, co-founded by fugitive "Cryptoqueen" Ruja Ignatova and Karl Sebastian Greenwood, defrauded as many as 3.4 million investors worldwide through a fake cryptocurrency that never operated on a blockchain, with the scheme operating between 2014 and 2019.
 
As a result of misrepresentations, victims invested over $4 billion worldwide in the fraudulent cryptocurrency.
 At $40 million recovered against $4 billion in losses, the current remission pool covers less than 1% of admitted victim losses — a recovery rate that, at this stage, is far below even the most distressed historical benchmarks.


The DOJ filed a number of OneCoin-related prosecutions in the Southern District of New York; several key figures have been sentenced and the Department pursued criminal forfeiture of property derived from scheme proceeds, resulting in more than $40 million in forfeited assets currently available for victim compensation.
 
Kroll Settlement Administration LLC is serving as the remission administrator.
 Victims may file petitions at www.onecoinremission.com. The claims deadline and pro-rata methodology have not yet been publicly released as of this writing.

The international tracing picture has continued to develop in parallel. 
Following international legal cooperation between Guernsey and Germany, on January 16, 2026, the Royal Court of Guernsey ordered the registration and enforcement of an overseas forfeiture order against funds totaling GBP 8,590,200.92, found in German legal proceedings to be the proceeds of Ignatova's criminal conduct.
 
German prosecutors in Bielefeld, working with the Royal Court, traced two of Ignatova's London properties through shell companies registered in the Channel Islands; after Ignatova ignored a 28-day ultimatum, the Royal Court registered the forfeiture order and released those funds into the Seized Asset Fund for return to Bielefeld for victim compensation.
 A further development on the enforcement perimeter: 
Frank Schneider, a former deputy chief of Luxembourg's foreign-intelligence service who advised Ignatova through his firm Sandstone, fled French house arrest in May 2023, was returned, and in April 2026 saw the Nancy Court of Appeal side with Washington's extradition request; he has indicated he will appeal.
 Ignatova herself 
remains an FBI Top Ten Fugitive, with the FBI and IRS-CI continuing to pursue her alongside the U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York.


**Claim-pricing implications.** The combination of a minimal current remission pool (sub-1%), an open extradition track for a key associate, and ongoing multi-jurisdictional tracing means OneCoin claims remain highly speculative but directionally improving. The Guernsey forfeiture and Bielefeld proceeds both flow to German victim-compensation channels, not to the U.S. DOJ remission process — buyers of U.S.-side claims should confirm which recovery pool their claims map to before pricing. Cross-border § 1782 discovery applications remain available to sophisticated creditors seeking to map remaining asset flows.

### II. Stanford Receivership — Final Distribution Underway; Swiss Ancillary Closed

The Stanford receivership (SEC v. Stanford International Bank Ltd., No. 3:09-CV-0298-N, N.D. Tex., Dallas Div., Judge David C. Godbey) has moved into its final distribution phase. 
On November 25, 2025, the Court approved an amendment to the Final Distribution Plan originally approved on October 8, 2025, under which the Receiver will make payments totaling more than $339 million to over 14,000 claimant groups.
 
On March 9, 2026, the Receiver filed with the Northern District of Texas the Schedule of Payments pursuant to the Final Distribution Plan, in accordance with the October 8 and November 25, 2025 Final Distribution Orders.
 
On April 3, 2026, the Receiver issued payments to over 2,000 claimant groups under the Final Distribution Plan totaling approximately $47.5 million.


The Swiss ancillary proceeding is closed. 
On December 17, 2025, the Antiguan Joint Liquidators informed the Receiver that the Swiss Ancillary Bankruptcy proceeding concerning Stanford International Bank had concluded; as a result, the JLs transferred to the Receiver, pursuant to the Cross-Border Protocol, $8.1 million in funds from the Swiss bankruptcy proceeding, with no further distributions of funds expected from that track.


Claimants should note a 90-day check-cashing deadline. 
Payees must cash checks issued pursuant to the Final Distribution Orders no later than 90 days after the date of issuance; reissuance may be requested in writing within that same 90-day window, and reissuance may be requested only once — if a payee fails to cash or seek reissuance within 90 days, that claimant forfeits further payment under the Final Distribution Plan.
 
All funds represented by void checks not subject to a timely request for reissuance shall revert to the Receivership Estate.
 For Venezuelan-address claimants, 
as of March 11, 2026, the Receivership has resumed courier delivery of physical checks to Venezuela.


**Clawback posture.** Receiver Ralph Janvey's prior UFTA-based clawback actions against net winners produced significant litigation, including a near-$125 million judgment. Those proceedings are substantially resolved and no active clawback campaigns appear outstanding at this stage, consistent with the Final Distribution posture. Stanford claims now price primarily on distribution-timing risk and residual reversion risk (uncashed checks reverting to estate), not litigation risk.

Approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim has now been paid when combined with all prior distributions, unless that claim has been fully satisfied.

As of the seventeenth distribution, SIPC has received $272.4 million in reimbursement from the BLMIS Customer Fund for advances paid on fully satisfied claims, leaving $578.5 million in SIPC advances outstanding.


The Fiftieth Fee Application was noted on the record.

Fee applications at this stage of the BLMIS liquidation are relevant to claims pricing: SIPC pays all trustee and legal fees (not the Customer Fund), so fee orders do not directly reduce distributions; however, the pace and quantum of the fee record is a proxy for ongoing litigation activity that will produce future recoveries and further distributions.

For claim-trading purposes: at 72.848% paid on a ~$20.3 billion admitted claims base, the residual uncovered exposure of roughly $5.5 billion (gross) implies continued recovery litigation targeting the remaining ~27% recovery gap. The trustee's own characterization — "approximately 75 cents on the dollar" — suggests the ceiling on ultimate recovery under current litigation posture is within reach, but not certain. Secondary market pricing for BLMIS claims should reflect both the 90%+ probability of at least one further distribution and the time-value drag of uncertain litigation timelines.

### V. Cross-Border Recovery Mechanics — Persistent Structural Context

For completeness, the following structural tools remain active across the dockets above and bear repeating for any reader pricing recovery timelines:

**11 U.S.C. § 548 / State UFTA / UVTA (Ponzi presumption).** The *Ponzi scheme presumption* — that all transfers made in furtherance of a Ponzi scheme are made with actual intent to defraud, allowing avoidance under § 548(a)(1)(A) — remains controlling in the Second, Fifth, and Tenth Circuits. Net winners who received fictitious profits remain the primary clawback targets; the Madoff proceeding has litigated this across thousands of adversaries over 17 years and the jurisprudence is now well-settled. Stanford clawback litigation has been substantially concluded on the net-winner front.

**28 U.S.C. § 1782 and MLAT requests.** The OneCoin and Stanford matters both illustrate the multi-jurisdictional tracing toolkit: the Guernsey forfeiture resulted from a German Bielefeld mutual legal assistance request, not from a § 1782 proceeding, but U.S.-side creditors in complex schemes can and do use § 1782 to compel discovery from U.S. financial institutions holding records relevant to offshore asset tracing.

**Mareva / CPR 25 and § 423 Insolvency Act 1986 (UK).** The Ignatova Kensington properties were restrained under Guernsey law enforcement of a German order — the Channel Islands route, not directly CPR 25, but the underlying principle (freezing assets pending forfeiture via cross-border cooperation) operates similarly to English Mareva practice. Any U.K.-facing recovery action against OneCoin-related asset pools should consider both CPR 25 injunctions and § 423 IA 1986 (transactions defrauding creditors) as parallel tools.
