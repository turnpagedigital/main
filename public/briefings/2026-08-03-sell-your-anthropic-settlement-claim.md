# Can You Sell Your Bartz v. Anthropic Settlement Claim?

Yes. The Bartz v. Anthropic settlement agreement has no "anti-assignment" clause that would bar claimants from assigning their right to payment as is common in may class actions. A right to payment from a class settlement is property, and property that isn't expressly restricted can be sold like any other asset. How does it work? The buyer pays a fixed amount to the claim holder now in exchange for all of their rights under the settlement and collects from the settlement fund directly as it pays out. 

## What about those claim selling "scam" warnings I've heard about? 

Some searches about selling your Anthropic settlement claim surface warnings that selling a class-action claim is barred, invalid, or fraudulent. Those warnings trace back to a company called ClaimsHero that a federal judge described as "a fraud of immense proportions." ClaimsHero allegedly pressured authors to **opt out** **of the settlement** entirely before the March 30, 2026 filing deadline on the promise of a bigger potential payout based on filing a separate, speculative lawsuit. ClaimsHero hoped to capture a share of recoveries from that larger potential payout and allegedly misled customers into believing they were guaranteed to get more than under the settlement. ClaimsHero wasn't paying claimants consideration to actually purchase their claims. It was all a ruse to capture a portion of litigation fees. 

Selling a filed claim to an independent buyer is exactly the opposite of the scheme that ClaimsHero was allegedly running.  Instead of giving up their claim in exchange for an even *more speculative* *asset*, the claimholder sells their rights to payment for a *less speculative asset* —namely cash, paid today.* While the cash value may be less than the expected recovery, the discount in a claim sale is generally justified by the duration risk and other factors (like appeals) that could impact recoveries. The buyer absorbs these risks as part of the trade. 

**Why are Anthropic class action claim holders selling?** 

There are multiple reasons that claim holders may want to sell their recovery rights. First, the settlement fund won't be fully funded until September 2027  For most authors, waiting is fine and selling makes no sense: you keep every dollar. Sellers are usually in a specific situation:

- **Estates and trusts** that need to convert assets to cash to close accounts
- **Authors with many eligible works**, where the wait applies to a five- or six-figure sum
- **Small presses** that would rather redeploy the capital now
- **Anyone with a present need** that a 2027 completion date doesn't serve

## How a sale works, step by step

1. **You describe what you own.** Which works, whether you filed a claim, and whether rights are shared with a publisher or co-author — under the Plan of Allocation, most traditionally published titles split 50/50 with the publisher, while self-published authors keep the whole amount.
2. **The buyer verifies and quotes.** A serious buyer checks your works against the settlement's records and quotes a fixed price — a percentage of the expected recovery, discounted for the wait and the risks being transferred.
3. **You sign an assignment agreement.** It transfers the right to receive the settlement payments. The mechanics depend on the settlement's terms and the administrator's process; handling that is part of what the buyer takes on.
4. **You are paid now.** Whatever the fund later pays, and whenever it pays it, is the buyer's concern.

## What you give up

The discount. A buyer pays less than the projected payout — that gap is the price of certainty, and it is real money. If distributions arrive sooner or larger than expected, the buyer benefits, not you. The fair trade in return: in a properly structured sale, delay and shortfall risk move to the buyer. If the fund pays late — for instance, if [an appeal pauses distribution](/briefings/2026-08-03-anthropic-settlement-appeal-risk) — or pays less than projected, that is the buyer's loss. An agreement that leaves those risks with you after you've sold deserves a hard second read.

## Questions to ask any buyer

1. Is the price fixed at signing, or can it change afterward?
2. Who bears the risk of delay, and of the fund paying less than projected?
3. Are there fees beyond the discount — processing, documentation, anything that compounds over time?
4. Does the buyer understand this settlement specifically — the Works List, the allocation splits, the installment dates — or are they pricing it like a generic lawsuit?
5. What happens if your claim turns out to be smaller or larger than you both believed?
