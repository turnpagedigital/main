# Can You Sell Your Bartz v. Anthropic Settlement Claim?

Yes. The Bartz v. Anthropic settlement agreement does not bar claimants from assigning their right to payment — there is no anti-assignment clause. A right to payment from a class settlement is property, and property that isn't expressly restricted can be sold like any other asset: to a buyer who pays you a fixed amount now and collects from the fund as it pays out. This page explains how such a sale works, what you give up, and the questions worth asking anyone who offers to buy, including us. Turnpage Digital Markets buys these claims; that is disclosed up front so you can weigh what follows accordingly.

## This isn't the "claim selling is a scam" warning you may have seen

Some searches about selling an Anthropic settlement claim surface warnings that selling a class-action claim is barred, invalid, or fraudulent. Those warnings trace back to a real scheme — a company called ClaimsHero that a federal judge described as "a fraud of immense proportions," for pressuring authors to opt **out** of the settlement entirely, before the March 30, 2026 filing deadline, on the promise of a bigger payout from a separate, speculative lawsuit. That asked people to abandon their claim in this settlement on a gamble — a fundamentally different transaction from a sale.

Selling an already-filed claim is the opposite. You keep your claim exactly as filed and approved inside the Bartz settlement — nothing is abandoned, nothing is opted out of, and the deadline that mattered (March 30, 2026) has already passed with your claim intact. You simply assign your right to receive the payment the settlement already owes you, in exchange for a fixed price now instead of waiting for the installment schedule to finish in 2027.

## Why some claimants sell

The settlement pays on its own schedule — in waves, with the fund not fully financed until September 2027 ([the full timeline](/briefings/2026-08-03-anthropic-settlement-payment-timeline)). For most authors, waiting is fine and selling makes no sense: you keep every dollar. Sellers are usually in a specific situation:

- **Estates and trusts** that need to convert assets to cash to close accounts
- **Authors with many eligible works**, where the wait applies to a five- or six-figure sum
- **Small presses** that would rather redeploy the capital now
- **Anyone with a present need** that a 2027 completion date doesn't serve

## How a sale works, step by step

1. **You describe what you own.** Which works, whether you filed a claim, and whether rights are shared with a publisher or co-author — under the Plan of Allocation, most traditionally published titles split 50/50 with the publisher, while self-published authors keep the whole amount.
2. **The buyer verifies and quotes.** A serious buyer checks your works against the settlement's records and quotes a fixed price — a percentage of the expected recovery, discounted for the wait and the risks being transferred.
3. **You sign an assignment agreement.** It transfers the right to receive the settlement payments. The mechanics depend on the settlement's terms and the administrator's process; handling that is part of what the buyer takes on.
4. **You are paid now.** Whatever the fund later pays, and whenever it pays it, is the buyer's concern.

## What you give up

The discount. A buyer pays less than the projected payout — that gap is the price of certainty, and it is real money. If distributions arrive sooner or larger than expected, the buyer benefits, not you. The fair trade in return: in a properly structured sale, delay and shortfall risk move to the buyer. If the fund pays late — for instance, if [an appeal pauses distribution](/briefings/2026-08-03-anthropic-settlement-appeal-risk) — or pays less than projected, that is the buyer's loss. An agreement that leaves those risks with you after you've sold deserves a hard second read.

## Questions to ask any buyer

1. Is the price fixed at signing, or can it change afterward?
2. Who bears the risk of delay, and of the fund paying less than projected?
3. Are there fees beyond the discount — processing, documentation, anything that compounds over time?
4. Does the buyer understand this settlement specifically — the Works List, the allocation splits, the installment dates — or are they pricing it like a generic lawsuit?
5. What happens if your claim turns out to be smaller or larger than you both believed?
