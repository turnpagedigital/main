### I. Myers / Sterling Capital: Dual Criminal-Civil Track Opens in the Northern District of Illinois

On June 5, 2026, the Securities and Exchange Commission charged John Sterling Myers and his two companies — Sterling Capital, LLC ("Sterling Capital") and Sterling Capital Management, LLC ("SCM") — with allegedly engaging in a multi-year fraud. According to the SEC's complaint, from January 2022 through at least July 2025, the defendants misappropriated investor money, falsified investor account statements, and engaged in other misconduct while acting as investment advisers to a pooled investment vehicle, Sterling Capital Investments, LLC (the "Fund").

The civil complaint, filed in the **U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois**, charges a comprehensive statutory sweep. The SEC charges the defendants with violating Section 17(a) of the Securities Act of 1933; Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and Rule 10b-5 thereunder; and Sections 206(1), 206(2), and 206(4) of the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and Rule 206(4)-8 thereunder. The complaint also charges Myers with control person liability pursuant to Section 20(a) of the Exchange Act. The complaint seeks permanent injunctive relief, disgorgement with prejudgment interest, and civil penalties against all defendants.

On the investor-harm metrics: The complaint alleges that Myers began operating this so-called "premier" and "exclusive investment pool" in 2022, raised approximately $4 million from approximately 28 investors over several years, and that over $3.6 million of investors' money is gone. The fund's only brokerage account was closed in the scheme's later phase, forcing Myers to reroute investor funds through personal accounts. His own tax returns reported more than $890,000 in deductible trading losses in 2023 and more than $1 million in 2024. When investors requested redemptions in spring 2025 totaling approximately $560,000, Myers told them the fund's assets had been frozen due to the SEC's investigation — a claim the complaint characterizes as false.

Myers raised approximately $4 million from at least 28 investors across five states, many of whom were family members, friends, or personal acquaintances. He distributed fabricated quarterly account statements depicting annual returns ranging from 16% to 54%, depending on the investor — a classic affinity-fraud pattern layered on top of a structurally insolvent pooled vehicle. As of the end of 2025, the defendants had only repaid investors approximately $398,000.

The criminal track is equally aggressive. A grand jury returned an indictment on four counts of federal wire fraud after alleging Myers ran a Ponzi scheme that raised approximately $4 million from at least 28 investors across five states and left more than $3.6 million of their money gone. Arraignment is set for June 12, 2026, at 10:00 a.m., before U.S. District Judge Edmond E. Chang. Each of the four wire fraud counts carries a maximum penalty of 20 years in federal prison. Recovery counsel should note that the criminal and civil dockets are running in parallel — standard posture for USAO N.D. Ill. and the SEC's Chicago Regional Office — which means asset-freeze and disgorgement proceedings in the civil case may move faster than criminal restitution.

The unregistered-adviser dimension is significant for investor-facing claims: none of the three defendants — Myers, Sterling Capital LLC, or Sterling Capital Management LLC — has ever been registered with the SEC as an investment adviser. Unregistered-adviser status strips Advisers Act Section 205 safe-harbor arguments and broadens the SEC's Section 206 reach. Claimants who invested after FINRA BrokerCheck or SEC EDGAR searches would have revealed the absence of registration may be positioned to assert independent negligence claims against any introducing parties or custodians who facilitated onboarding.

### II. Power Block Coin, LLC (Bankr. D. Utah, No. 24-23041, Judge Cathleen D. Parker): Docket Activity June 9; Liquidating-Trust Plan on Creditor Radar

The Power Block Coin proceeding — a crypto-financial-services insolvency operating as SmartFi — remains one of the most consequential mid-market creditor-rights cases in the Intermountain West bankruptcy bench. Power Block Coin, which operated under the name SmartFi, filed for bankruptcy protection on June 20, 2024. The case carries cross-track significance for fraud-recovery practitioners because the committee's plan papers have flagged pre-bankruptcy insider transactions and unexplained cryptocurrency-asset losses as litigation targets for a prospective Liquidating Trustee.

The tracked-case ground truth confirms ECF No. 519 filed June 9, 2026 as the most recent docket activity — the only new entry in the 72-hour window. The sparse description in the docket feed does not reveal whether ECF 519 is a scheduling order, a status report, or a plan-related filing, but the timing is notable: it follows a May 7, 2026 noticing order (ECF 503) that reached Celsius Network LLC (in its own SDNY proceeding, Case No. 22-10964) and Eckert Seamans attorneys appearing on behalf of creditors Lynn Brown, Jason Brown, and Robert L. Brown, as well as Daniel Stadelmann — a creditor-side litigation bloc worth watching as the plan moves toward confirmation.

The operative framework for creditor recovery remains the committee's liquidating plan. The Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors of Power Block Coin, LLC filed a comprehensive liquidation plan in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah, proposing to wind down the cryptocurrency financial services company under Chapter 11 protection (Case No. 24-bk-23041). Filed by Greenberg Traurig, LLP on January 30, 2025, the plan outlines a strategy to maximize value for creditors through the establishment of a Liquidating Trust overseen by an independent trustee.

The plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million. A key component involves the appointment of a disinterested Liquidating Trustee to manage the company's assets, including pursuing potential legal claims and overseeing distributions to creditors. The Celsius exposure is a critical variable: the pending Celsius SDNY proceeding (Case No. 22-10964) and its own distribution waterfall will affect what, if anything, Celsius can receive on its Power Block Coin claim — and thus the distributable estate available to all other general unsecured creditors.

The committee's filing raises concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions, including loans made to affiliated entities at allegedly below-market rates. A significant asset under scrutiny is a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC, an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah. The committee expressed concerns about this loan's unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property. That $17.4 million real-property exposure is a live litigation target for the Liquidating Trustee and should be treated as a potentially recoverable claim — subject to avoidance analysis under 11 U.S.C. §§ 547–550 (preferential transfer), 548 (fraudulent transfer), and applicable Utah fraudulent-transfer law.

The case's procedural history bears emphasis: the court initially struck the Subchapter V election in October 2024. The Court determined that the Debtor was not eligible to proceed under Subchapter V because the Debtor's aggregate noncontingent liquidated debts as of the petition date exceeded $7,500,000. Accordingly, the Court struck the Debtor's Subchapter V designation, and the case proceeded as a standard Chapter 11 case. That ruling — issued when the case was still assigned to Judge Joel T. Marker — is the procedural foundation on which the committee's full Chapter 11 liquidating plan rests. The case is now before Judge Cathleen D. Parker, per the tracked-case ground truth, and practitioners should ensure all filings bear the CDP suffix (24-23041-CDP).

### III. A.G. Morgan / Camarda (E.D.N.Y., No. 26-CR-51, Judge Nusrat J. Choudhury): Sentencing August 2026; Recovery Gap Widens

The Camarda matter remains the largest near-term restitution event in this advisory's tracked universe and warrants continued monitoring as the August 2026 sentencing date approaches. Vincent Camarda pleaded guilty on April 3, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York to one count of securities fraud and one count of investment adviser fraud. He faces up to 20 years in prison, $160 million in restitution, and $6.6 million in forfeiture. The charges stem from a scheme spanning January 2017 through December 2024 in which he defrauded roughly 400 clients.

The same morning, the Securities and Exchange Commission filed a civil complaint naming Camarda, his firm A.G. Morgan Financial Advisors, LLC, and company president James E. McArthur as defendants in an offering fraud that raised at least $138 million from 431 investors, a majority of whom were elderly and financially unsophisticated.

The SEC alleges that from about mid-2020 through the end of 2023, Camarda and McArthur steered advisory clients into promissory notes issued by five private funds they controlled, telling investors the products were conservative and diversified. Instead, four of the funds invested entirely in a single high-risk mining venture, while the fifth funneled money into a startup coffee shop owned by Camarda's son.

The Par Funding / Complete Business Solutions Group link is a critical chain-of-causation point for downstream recovery claimants. A.G. Morgan raised more than $75 million from over 200 investors through Par Funding's unregistered securities offering between 2017 and 2020. Par Funding was later determined to be a Ponzi scheme — its CEO Joseph LaForte was sentenced to 15.5 years in federal prison in March 2025. In March 2026, Par Funding itself pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and securities fraud. Clients who suffered losses through the Par Funding channel have independent claims arising from A.G. Morgan's sale of unregistered securities — distinct from, and potentially additive to, claims arising from the later Camarda Funds phase.

The asset-recovery picture is sobering. The asset picture today is stark. Both Camarda's office building in Massapequa and his personal residence in Amityville are in foreclosure proceedings. Schemes of this scale rarely yield full recovery for victims. McArthur, who served as the firm's president and chief compliance officer throughout the scheme, remains a defendant in the SEC's civil case. He has not entered a criminal guilty plea. McArthur's continued civil exposure is an active litigation front that recovery counsel should monitor; any settlement in the civil case could establish a secondary recovery layer for the 431-investor class.

### IV. Macro Enforcement Context: DOJ National Fraud Division and Budget Signal

The structural backdrop for all three matters is the administration's sustained investment in fraud-recovery capacity. The White House's fiscal year 2027 budget request seeks $30 million for the DOJ's newly established National Fraud Division, which would support 140 positions including 100 attorneys. The budget document states that these resources would "equip the department to investigate and prosecute fraudsters and provide much needed relief to those harmed by their schemes." The DOJ's Criminal Division announced in early 2026 that its Fraud Section had brought white collar charges against more than 200 individuals and secured 140 criminal convictions. The SEC is seeking a similar budget enhancement to its Enforcement Division compared to what was allocated in 2026, and the U.S. Department of the Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) is seeking $25 million more in 2027 to bolster its Bank Secrecy Act enforcement program.

For recovery-posture purposes, the enforcement capacity build-out has a concrete implication: parallel civil-criminal tracks (as in both the Myers/Sterling Capital and Camarda matters) are now the preferred DOJ-SEC coordination template, not an exception. Claimants who file proofs of claim or civil actions concurrent with a criminal indictment benefit from criminal asset-freeze orders that may lock assets before the defendant can dissipate them through litigation delay. The Myers arraignment today before Judge Chang — the same morning as the advisory's publication date — provides the triggering moment for any victim seeking to intervene or coordinate with the USAO N.D. Ill. victim-notification process.
