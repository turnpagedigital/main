### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Under Submission

The $1.5 billion settlement — the largest copyright class action settlement in U.S. history — remains in a critical procedural interstitial. 
The fairness hearing took place May 14, 2026 before Judge Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion settlement.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench and indicated that short orders may follow — this is one of the last major procedural steps before the court decides whether to grant final approval.


The post-hearing procedural posture is consequential. 
The court ordered that by May 21, 2026, Anthropic could file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and that plaintiffs file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.
 
In that supplemental filing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any supporting record.


On fees: 
class counsel urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised downward from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 The reduction matters structurally — the originally sought $375 million (25% of the $1.5 billion fund) drew sustained judicial scrutiny and likely contributed to the downward revision. 
Anthropic separately argued that the $75 million in fees that nonclass counsel requested is "far too high" and lacking "scant justification."


The claims administration picture is now complete and exceptionally favorable for the administrator but compresses per-work economics. 
Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% participation rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the motion for final approval filed March 19, 2026, indicating a large volume of claims arrived at the very end of the March 30 deadline.
 
The high claim rate means actual per-work payouts will likely be closer to the original $3,000-per-work figure
 rather than the higher projections that circulated when participation stood at 54%. 
Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed the payout per work will be approximately $3,100.


The payment waterfall is staged: 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments — October 2, 2025 (first tranche already paid); April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Under the plan of allocation, Anthropic's $450 million Schedule Payment 1 is due September 25, 2026, with the Settlement Administrator issuing a second wave of checks by October 9, 2026 — all contingent on final approval becoming final and any objector appeals being resolved.


The critical unresolved issue for claims investors and rights-holder counsel is timing: the court has not issued the final approval order, and 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 Any Ninth Circuit objector appeal — even a non-meritorious one — inserts a delay buffer that disrupts the September 2026 distribution schedule.

Importantly, 
the scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood: Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training.
 
Judge Alsup ruled on summary judgment in June 2025 that using books without permission to train AI was fair use if acquired legally, but denied summary judgment on piracy — finding piracy was not fair use.
 The settlement resolves the piracy-sourced dataset liability (LibGen/PiLiMi), not the broader training-use question. 
For many authors implicated in Bartz, the litigation around AI training and book piracy is not ending; it is shifting to other cases.

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (Concord I), No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.) — Cross-MSJ Hearing October 21

This is the active front on training-use fair use for commercially licensed content. 
The case is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, referred to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen, captioned *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.).



Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO have asked Judge Lee to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrights and to reject the AI company's fair use defense.
 
The motion for partial summary judgment was filed March 23, 2026 in the Northern District of California and is accompanied by a 47-page statement setting out 218 "undisputed facts" supported by deposition testimony, internal Anthropic documents, and Anthropic's own admissions.


The publishers' § 107 market-harm argument (fourth factor) is the sharpest edge of the brief. 
On market harm, the filing argues that when Claude outputs lyrics in response to user requests, "Anthropic provides the same service to its users as Publishers' licensees, without Publishers' authorization or any restrictions on that use," citing data showing AI-generated songs flooding streaming platforms.
 On infringement, 
Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works" and "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."


On the commercial nature prong (first factor), 
the publishers argue Anthropic's use was purely commercial, noting the company is "a for-profit technology company valued at $380 billion or more" with a revenue run rate approaching $14 billion, that it copied lyrics in full, and that its output directly competes with licensed lyrics services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch.


The case has also produced a significant discovery sanction. 
A California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged she used Anthropic's own Claude.ai tool to help draft the expert's declaration containing the erroneous citation, but argued the error was "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 The episode is a live sanction-risk data point that the court will carry into the summary-judgment record.


Briefing proceeded with the publishers' motion for partial summary judgment filed March 23, 2026, and Anthropic's cross-motion filed shortly after.
 
Under the Concord I scheduling order (Dkt. 587), dispositive motions were to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026.
 The October 21 SJ hearing date is now the nearest hard inflection point for the entire AI-copyright docket.

A parallel second action adds litigation pressure. 
Major music publishers filed a second, $3 billion suit against Anthropic, alleging newly discovered mass infringement of sheet music and songbooks covering more than 20,000 songs.
 
Anthropic moved to stay *Concord II* (No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK) pending final resolution of Concord I — or at minimum pending resolution of the Concord I summary judgment motions and any subsequent appeal.
 The stay motion, heard May 20, 2026, presented the straightforward argument that threshold legal issues in Concord I — including the fair-use training-data question and DMCA §1202 CMI claims — will be preclusive or at least materially narrowing in Concord II.

### III. *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143-SHS-OTW (S.D.N.Y.) — Discovery Phase Continues


The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, captioned *In Re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.).
 The docket was last updated July 10, 2026, reflecting ongoing activity.

The MDL covers multiple consolidated tracks. 
The district court denied OpenAI's motion to dismiss plaintiffs' claim of direct copyright infringement based on AI outputs, holding that plaintiffs adequately pled that OpenAI actually copied their text and that a reasonable jury could find that the allegedly infringing outputs are substantially similar to plaintiffs' works.
 That ruling — on the *Baldacci* consolidated complaint — means output-based infringement survives pleading stage and moves to discovery.

On scope management: 
Judge Stein granted OpenAI's motion to stay claims as to models not already in the MDL — specifically the o1, o1 mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 models.
 That is a meaningful litigation-risk boundary: newer model families are quarantined from MDL discovery pressure pending resolution of the core training-use issues.

One significant discovery sanction has already been entered: 
the district court found that OpenAI waived attorney-client privilege and granted plaintiffs' motion to compel OpenAI to produce documents.
 Privilege waivers in MDL discovery typically accelerate the production of internal deliberations about training practices — precisely the materials that will drive § 107(4) market-harm analysis and any eventual settlement valuation.

The structural posture of the MDL tracks Concord I closely: both will turn on judicial analysis of the § 107 four factors as applied to large-scale commercial LLM training. Judge Lee's October 21 ruling in Concord I will arrive first and carries real weight as persuasive authority for Judge Stein, even across circuits — particularly on the commerciality and market-substitution prongs.

### IV. Settlement Precedent, Licensing Comparables, and § 107(4) Market Harm

The Bartz settlement structure — $3,100 per registered, piracy-sourced work at 92.77% participation — now functions as a floor comparator for any licensing negotiation or litigation posture across the AI-copyright docket. 
To the extent Bartz sets the pattern for future litigation, the settlement creates an odd position: Judge Alsup's ruling says training on legally purchased books is fair use — a green light — but the massive payout for downloading books from LibGen raises questions about websites scraped online without obvious permission.



With statutory damages in play under 17 U.S.C. § 504, the downside risk for AI companies is catastrophic — some will likely sign licensing deals they don't strictly need, just to avoid a class action.
 That dynamic is already playing out: rights-holders and claims investors should be tracking any AI licensing deal announced in the next six to twelve months as a market-harm evidence data point under § 107(4), directly probative of whether a licensing market for training data exists and at what price.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should treat the Bartz final approval order — when it issues — as the starting gun for two parallel timelines: (a) the 9th Circuit appeal window, which, if triggered by any objector, will delay the September 25, 2026 Anthropic $450 million Schedule Payment 1 and the October distribution to claimants, and (b) the settlement's use as a pricing comparator in licensing negotiations with other LLM developers. Monitor DE 679 (Anthropic's July 13 opposition brief) and watch for the court's final order, which remains under submission with no public ruling date confirmed; the absence of a ruling since the May 14 hearing is the operative risk variable today. On Concord I, the October 21 summary-judgment hearing before Judge Lee is the single most consequential near-term date on the AI-copyright docket: a ruling granting the publishers' partial MSJ on infringement (even if leaving fair use contested) would be a material adverse development for all LLM developers with unlicensed commercial training sets, while a ruling for Anthropic on fair use would depress settlement values across the board. Clients with exposure in either the OpenAI MDL (No. 1:25-md-03143) or Concord II (No. 5:26-cv-00880) should model both outcomes now. The Latham & Watkins Claude-assisted hallucination episode in Concord I is a standalone professional responsibility risk worth tracking — it produced a partial expert-report strike and remains a live credibility deficit for Anthropic's expert testimony heading into the SJ record.
