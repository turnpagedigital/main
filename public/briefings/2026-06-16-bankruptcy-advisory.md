### I. BlockFills / Reliz Technology Group — Sale Hearing Today; Confirmation Clock Running

On March 15, 2026, each of the four Debtors filed voluntary petitions for relief under Chapter 11; the cases are jointly administered under Case No. 26-10371 before the Honorable Judge Thomas M. Horan in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware. The estate presents an acute asset-liability mismatch: court filings show Reliz reporting assets between $50 million and $100 million against liabilities of $100 million to $500 million. The dominant unsecured exposure is on the customer side: the capital structure is concentrated in customer and trade exposure rather than traditional funded debt, with the first-day record identifying roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and approximately $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.

The sale track reached its decisive moment this morning. As set out in the official Bankruptcy Court document filed on May 26, 2026, Keyrock SA was declared the "Successful Bidder" for certain assets of Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and its affiliated debtors; according to the bankruptcy filing, Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property. A hearing to consider approval of the sale was scheduled for June 16, 2026, with parties continuing to collaborate on the administrative process to complete the transaction. Final completion of the transaction remains subject to final court approval and the appropriate regulatory approvals referenced in Keyrock's bid.

The plan track runs in parallel. On April 7, 2026, the Debtors filed (i) the Joint Chapter 11 Plan of Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and its Debtor Affiliates [Docket No. 128] and (ii) the Disclosure Statement Relating to Joint Chapter 11 Plan of Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and its Debtor Affiliates [Docket No. 129], along with a motion seeking approval of the Disclosure Statement and related procedures. The Disclosure Statement was subsequently approved: on May 28, 2026, the Bankruptcy Court entered the Order (A) Approving Disclosure Statement; (B) Scheduling Hearing on Confirmation of Plan; (C) Establishing Deadlines and Procedures for filing objections to confirmation and claim objections. Reliz is operating under final cash-collateral authority while pursuing a dual-track restructuring built around a sale process and a plan path, with confirmation targeted for July 13, 2026.

Creditors must understand the distress genealogy. The company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius, with compounding balance sheet pressures from stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials. Pre-petition, the company faced concurrent litigation pressure: in the weeks immediately preceding the Chapter 11 filing, certain Debtor entities and current or former directors and officers were named in two lawsuits asserting misappropriation of customer funds, conversion, breach of contract, and other fraud-based claims — *Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd.*, Case No. 1:26-cv-01672 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 27, 2026) and *1548199 Alberta Ltd. and Robert J. Bertram v. Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.*, Case No. 26-cv-02451 (N.D. Ill.). Dominion is suing Reliz for alleged misappropriation of funds and concealing losses, charging in the lawsuit that BlockFills "admitted to having commingled customer assets and having a balance sheet shortfall" in a February 7, 2026 call. In a February 27, 2026 lawsuit, Dominion Capital asserts damages of $77 million.

The $3.25 million sale price relative to $145 million in unsecured claims implies a recovery ceiling of approximately 2.2 cents on the dollar from sale proceeds alone, before any additional plan-level distributions funded from litigation recoveries, avoidance actions, or residual estate assets. Creditors holding general unsecured claims — particularly institutional trading counterparties and former customers — should treat the July 13 confirmation hearing as the definitive recovery-calculus moment; the plan documents filed at Docket Nos. 128-129 and the claim-objection procedures authorized by the May 28 order govern allowance of competing claims. The September 11, 2026 deadline flagged on the Veritaglobal case-administration page likely corresponds to a bar date or plan-related deadline that creditors tracking distributions must calendar immediately. ### II. Sleep Number Corporation — Fresh Chapter 11 Filing; $415M Stalking-Horse Sale; § 363 Timeline Compressed

On June 12, 2026, Sleep Number Corporation and its subsidiaries filed voluntary petitions for relief under Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York. The cases are pending joint administration under Case No. 26-11399 and are being administered by the Honorable Kyu Young Paek. The filing is a heavily pre-packaged § 363 sale process: the filing aims to effectuate a value-maximizing, going-concern sale of substantially all of the Debtors' assets under § 363, backstopped by a stalking-horse bid from SNBR Inc., an affiliate of Sleep Country Canada Inc., and pursuant to the Stalking Horse APA dated June 12, 2026, the Stalking Horse Bidder has committed — subject to Court approval — to acquire substantially all of the Company's assets for $415 million in cash plus the assumption of certain liabilities.

The balance sheet: Sleep Number Corporation reports $642.3 million in assets and $1.3 billion in liabilities, and the filing indicates that there will be funds available for distribution to unsecured creditors. The filing of the bankruptcy petitions constituted an event of default under the documents governing the Amended and Restated Credit and Security Agreement, which accelerated the Company's obligations under the Prepetition Credit Agreement. Counsel of record is Davis Polk & Wardwell LLP; Brian M. Resnick of Davis Polk & Wardwell LLP serves as lead counsel.

The pre-petition marketing process is material to the § 363 sale procedures motion. In March 2026, Guggenheim contacted 33 parties in connection with an active solicitation of recapitalization and bridge financing proposals, 26 of which executed non-disclosure agreements and were granted access to diligence materials; ultimately, only one written recapitalization proposal was received, which the Company determined was not actionable, and while several proposals for senior bridge financing were received, the existing lender group would not consent to being primed, and no actionable proposals for unsecured or junior financing were received. The Company concluded that the only viable pathway was to raise incremental bridge liquidity from its existing lenders while launching a marketing process to identify a purchaser for its assets as a going-concern sale.

The real estate portfolio is an immediate flashpoint for landlord creditors. The Debtors filed a rejection schedule covering 44 locations — all under the Select Comfort Retail Corporation entity — with rejection effective as of the Petition Date of June 12, 2026, representing approximately 8% of the total store portfolio. The landlords of these locations are immediately left with capped rejection-damages claims under Bankruptcy Code § 502(b)(6) — limited to the rent reserved for the greater of (i) one year or (ii) 15% of the remaining lease term (the latter not to exceed three years' rent), plus any unpaid pre-petition obligations. With Sleep Number operating 572 stores and having tried to sell itself in the months leading up to the filing, the company is seeking a 26-day sale process with competing bids due July 8 and the sale closing by July 31 under its proposed timeline.

The key hearing calendar: a hearing on the Debtors' First Day Motions was held on June 12, 2026 at 3:00 p.m. ET before Judge Paek; a final hearing on certain First Day Motions will be held on July 9, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET; and a hearing to consider the Bidding Procedures Motion is set for July 2, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET.

Sleep Number blamed its bankruptcy, in part, on "the unpredictable shifting of trade rules imposed by the current U.S. government, on top of an already vulnerable global supply chain." Sleep Number announced in May that it recorded a $50 million net loss in the first quarter of the year, driven by decreased sales. The § 502(b)(6) cap-exposure analysis is especially important for the 44 already-rejected stores; additional store rejections are expected through the July 31 sale-close timeline. Unsecured trade creditors and warranty obligees — the company is heavily dependent on customer smart-bed warranty and service agreements — face a scenario where the stalking-horse buyer elects which executory contracts to assume, leaving non-assumed warranty and service obligations as general unsecured claims against the estate.

The case originally proceeded under Subchapter V but converted to a standard Chapter 11 track after the committee filed a competing liquidation plan. The claim quantum is the defining creditor-rights issue in this case. ### IV. Supreme Court Jurisprudence — June 2026 Term Activity with Bankruptcy Implications

Two Supreme Court decisions from June 11, 2026 carry forward-looking implications for creditor-rights practitioners. First, in *Keathley v. Buddy Ayers Construction, Inc.* (June 11, 2026), the Court held that to determine whether an omission of a claim in the bankruptcy context was inadvertent or mistaken for purposes of judicial estoppel, courts should look to the totality of the circumstances surrounding the omission. This represents a meaningful win for plaintiffs who failed to schedule claims in prior bankruptcy proceedings — the prior-majority "potential motive = bad faith" rule that several circuits had applied is now replaced by a fact-intensive totality test, which significantly raises the procedural bar for defendants seeking to use judicial estoppel as a shield against suits brought by parties with prior bankruptcy histories. Litigation counsel and creditors prosecuting avoidance or tort-theory claims should reassess any pending judicial-estoppel motions in light of *Keathley*.

Separately, the foundational avoidance-action framework continues to be shaped by *United States v. Miller*, 145 S. Ct. 839 (2025): the Supreme Court issued a significant decision limiting a bankruptcy trustee's ability to recover alleged fraudulent transfers from the federal government; in *United States v. Miller*, the Court held that a trustee may not use § 544(b) of the Bankruptcy Code to pursue state law fraudulent transfer claims against the United States where sovereign immunity would bar those claims outside of bankruptcy. The decision underscores the importance of evaluating whether recovery claims involve sovereign defendants, whether § 548 provides an alternative federal cause of action, and whether other defendants remain viable targets for avoidance litigation. In the Power Block Coin context — where the Committee has flagged avoidance-style insider claims against affiliated entities — the *Miller* limitation is less directly applicable (no governmental defendant), but the Court's broader textualist narrowing of trustee powers signals continued judicial skepticism of expansive estate-recovery theories.

The *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma* rule (June 27, 2024 — 5-4) remains the governing constraint on plan architecture: the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize courts to enjoin or release claims against third parties without the consent of the creditor, and in a 5-4 decision, the United States Supreme Court held that nonconsensual third-party releases — releases and injunctions that, as part of a Chapter 11 Plan, effectively discharge claims against a non-debtor without the consent of affected claimants — are categorically impermissible. In *Sleep Number*, where management and sponsor interests are presumably carved out of the § 363 sale rather than addressed through a plan-level release, the *Purdue* prohibition is less immediately operative — but any residual plan elements addressing D&O or lender liability will need to be structured around consensual release mechanics.

### V. Carry-Forward Monitoring: § 502(b)(6) Exposure and DIP Mechanics in Sleep Number

The Sleep Number DIP structure warrants close monitoring by trade creditors. The proposed DIP Credit Agreement is subject to approval by the Bankruptcy Court and will be subject to customary conditions precedent. The lender group backing the prepetition credit facilities is also the DIP lender — a "roll-up" structure that effectively grants prepetition secured lenders priming-lien protection over the DIP advances. This is precisely the structure that Guggenheim's process surfaced as a constraint: the existing lender group would not consent to being primed. Trade creditors and lease counterparties who are not part of the secured class must evaluate their administrative priority exposure carefully: if the going-concern sale closes at $415 million (stalking-horse floor) and the prepetition secured debt substantially exceeds that figure after roll-up, the residual for unsecured claimants will be thin. The filing indicates that there will be funds available for distribution to unsecured creditors — but the "available" quantum is, as of today, unquantified pending the DIP order, bid procedures order, and a fully-loaded claims triage.
