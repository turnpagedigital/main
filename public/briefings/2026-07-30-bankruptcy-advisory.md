### I. Alkegen (ASP Unifrax Holdings) — Prepackaged Chapter 11, N.D. Tex. (Filed July 26, 2026)

This is the dominant new filing on the docket. 
ASP Unifrax Holdings, Inc. — the Irving, Texas-based specialty materials manufacturer operating under the Alkegen brand — filed for Chapter 11 protection on July 26 in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Northern District of Texas.
 The case is styled as a prepackaged restructuring designed to execute a balance-sheet fix, not an operational turnaround.

The debt architecture explains why the case arrived on the docket when it did. 
The company's September 2024 out-of-court refinancing extended funded debt maturities to 2028 and 2029 and provided approximately $350 million of incremental liquidity, but did not meaningfully reduce the overall debt burden. With PIK-toggle options on the Senior Facilities and Second Lien Notes set to expire September 30, 2026 — adding approximately $110 million of incremental annual cash interest — forecasted 2026 annual debt service was projected at approximately $320 million, up from $297 million in 2025.
 The October cliff was never survivable without a structural fix.


The prepackaged filing implements a comprehensive, consensual balance-sheet restructuring pursuant to an RSA executed with an ad hoc group holding First Lien Term Loans, First Lien Notes, and Second Lien Notes, and sponsor Clearlake Capital Group, L.P.
 The creditor endorsement is broad: 
the prepackaged plan carries 99% of first-lien holders and 80% of second-lien holders, clearing the voting thresholds a prepackaged case needs.


The economic terms are straightforward. 
Under the RSA, Alkegen will eliminate approximately $3.1 billion of total debt and emerge with approximately $200 million of liquidity. The company also secured $315 million in new-money DIP financing to support ordinary-course operations, with no anticipated disruption to employees, customers, vendors, or partners.
 On July 28, the court approved interim access to that DIP facility after overruling opposition to the size of the interim package. (__[Law360, July 28, 2026](https://www.law360.com/articles/2506871/alkegen-can-draw-on-ch-11-financing-in-prepack-case)__)

The plan is purely equity-for-debt: 
the plan contemplates the equitization of substantially all of the company's funded debt.
 
Having secured the support of the overwhelming majority of its financial partners, Alkegen is well-positioned to progress through the process in approximately 60 days
 — targeting emergence around late September, before the PIK cliff would have hit. 
Alkegen's plan leaves trade creditors unimpaired and paid in the ordinary course.


For distressed investors, the case presents a clean set-up: pre-negotiated, well-supported, short runway. The principal watchpoints are (a) whether unsecured creditors below the trade line receive any recovery or are administratively convenient zero-recovery classes, and (b) any third-party releases the prepack may carry — a live issue across every major Texas filing right now given the U.S. Trustee's posture in *Harvest Sherwood* next door. (__[Business Wire / Alkegen, July 26, 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260726574083/en/Alkegen-Commences-Prepackaged-Chapter-11-Process-to-Implement-Previously-Announced-Restructuring-Support-Agreement)__)

### II. Harvest Sherwood Food Distributors — Confirmation Hearing Aug. 5 (N.D. Tex. No. 25-80109, Judge Jernigan)

The confirmation hearing in *In re Harvest Sherwood Food Distributors, Inc.*, Bankr. N.D. Tex. No. 25-80109 (Judge Stacey G.C. Jernigan), is now five days out. The objection posture has sharpened materially since last week.

**The U.S. Trustee objection.** 
The U.S. Trustee filed an objection to confirmation of Harvest Sherwood's Third Amended Plan, filed on July 20, and adopted the arguments from an earlier Disclosure Statement objection at Docket No. 797.
 Two provisions survive the consensual resolution of prior disputes: the opt-out third-party release mechanism, and a proposed charitable donation of residual trust funds. 
The plan confirmation hearing is currently scheduled for August 5, 2026.


The release fight matters structurally. Post-*Purdue* (Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P., 603 U.S. 204 (2024)), opt-out — rather than opt-in — non-debtor releases in liquidating Chapter 11 plans occupy contested territory. The U.S. Trustee's position is that absent clear statutory authority, passive creditor inaction cannot be construed as consent to release non-debtor parties. This will put Judge Jernigan in a position that has divided courts across circuits since *Purdue* issued: whether the opt-out mechanism satisfies *Purdue*'s consent requirement or whether it is impermissible under 11 U.S.C. § 524(g) and the general discharge-prohibition rule. (__[BankruptcyData.com, July 20, 2026](https://www.bankruptcydata.com/article/harvest-sherwood-food-distributors-inc-a-us-trustee-objects-to-plan-confirmation-over-optout-thirdparty-releases-and-proposed-charitable-donation-of-residual-trust-funds)__)

**The Atlas Grove financing and the litigation-asset thesis.** The deeper investor story in this case is the estate's litigation portfolio. 
Atlas Grove Management received court approval on July 8, 2026, and funded a $150 million DIP facility to the Harvest Sherwood estate, and is also the stalking horse for the exit funding process.
 
As plan funder, Atlas Grove would seek to maximize value of more than $1 billion in antitrust litigation claims against more than 25 defendants, primarily across the chicken, pork, and beef producer industries.



Major unsecured creditors include National Beef Packaging ($15.5 million), Tyson Foods ($13 million), MegaCorp Logistics ($3.25 million), Dutch Farms ($3.67 million), Nebraska Beef ($1.92 million), and Wholestone Farms ($820,000).
 The presence of Tyson Foods on both sides of the ledger — as a major unsecured creditor and presumably a defendant in the antitrust claims — creates a governance dynamic worth tracking at confirmation. Creditors should determine whether the plan's release provisions sweep any antitrust defendants and, if so, whether that trade is being made on commercially reasonable terms or is simply a convenience for Clearlake-adjacent counterparties.

The charitable-donation provision is less doctrinally interesting but politically sensitive with the U.S. Trustee. Courts have permitted residual-fund charitable donations in prior cases where the amounts were de minimis and creditors were adequately compensated, but the U.S. Trustee's objection signals it views this as an impermissible distribution mechanism.

The docket activity as of July 29 suggests the case continues to generate filings, but the nature of those entries is unverified in this run. We carry forward the prior advisory's substantive positions subject to that caveat.

What is verified: 
a Delaware bankruptcy judge approved the $3.25 million sale of BlockFills to Keyrock SA, a Belgian digital asset investment group, as BlockFills prepared for its Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.
 
Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume substantially all of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property.


The capital structure remains dominated by customer-creditor exposure: 
the capital structure is concentrated in customer and trade exposure rather than traditional funded debt, with roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and about $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.

### IV. Structural Theme: Third-Party Releases in Liquidating Plans Post-*Purdue*

Three of the four most active cases on this desk — *BlockFills*, *Harvest Sherwood*, and the broader prepack wave now landing in Texas — share a common pressure point: post-*Purdue* release mechanics. *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024), eliminated non-consensual third-party releases in Chapter 11 plans but left significant ambiguity around what constitutes "consent" in an opt-out structure. The U.S. Trustee's office has operationalized that ambiguity as a standard confirmation objection in cases from Delaware to Texas. Courts are splitting.

The practical consequence for distressed investors: plans carrying opt-out releases are now pricing a meaningful confirmation-litigation premium. Plan negotiators in prepackaged cases are increasingly moving toward opt-in mechanics or narrowing the release universe to parties who have actually contributed value — the *Sackler* family payment structure being the implicit template — in order to reduce confirmation risk and litigation duration. The Alkegen prepack is the most relevant near-term data point: if it confirms without a release fight in 60 days, that will be evidence that adequately consented prepackaged releases can survive Texas court scrutiny post-*Purdue*.

## Recommended Actions

Distressed investors with exposure to the Alkegen prepack should immediately map their position in the capital structure against the RSA's equitization waterfall — the 99%/80% first- and second-lien endorsement levels confirm the plan will clear voting thresholds, but the recovery profile for any unsecured claims below the funded-debt line requires case-specific analysis before the ~60-day confirmation window closes. In *Harvest Sherwood*, the August 5 hearing is the next dispositive event: creditors should file or coordinate objections to the opt-out release and the charitable-donation provision now if they have not done so already, or evaluate whether to file a Bankruptcy Rule 3018 temporary allowance motion to secure voting rights on the plan before the hearing. The U.S. Trustee's July 20 objection to the Third Amended Plan at Docket No. 797 provides the doctrinal framework — any follow-on objector should anchor to that record and the *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma* holding rather than re-litigate from scratch. On *BlockFills*, creditors holding the $145 million in general unsecured customer claims should be in contact with the Cleary Gottlieb ad hoc group to understand post-confirmation distribution mechanics; recovery rates from a $3.25 million technology sale against $145 million in unsecured claims imply residual value depends almost entirely on whatever the liquidating trust recovers from litigation and remaining assets — that is the number worth modeling now.
