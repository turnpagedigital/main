### I. BlockFills / Reliz — § 363 Sale Hearing Today; Dual-Track Plan Structure

Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and three affiliated debtor entities — collectively operating under the trade name "BlockFills" — filed Chapter 11 petitions on March 15, 2026 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware before the Honorable Thomas M. Horan. The filings followed years of cascading financial losses, two prepetition customer lawsuits with temporary restraining orders, and the collapse of multiple recapitalization attempts.

The petition-date balance sheet is deeply distressed. Court documents list BlockFills' assets at between $50 million and $100 million, with liabilities ranging from $100 million to $500 million — a gap that means creditors face significant losses. More specifically, the Debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations. The counterparty history explains the deterioration: the company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius; compounding those balance sheet pressures were stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials.

The case structure deployed a dual-track mechanism. The filing was supported by a pre-negotiated term sheet with an Ad Hoc Group of the company's largest customers that provides the framework for a reorganization plan centered on the creation of a new operating entity and a liquidating trust. Less than four weeks after the Petition Date, the Debtors filed a Joint Chapter 11 Plan, a Disclosure Statement, and a Solicitation Procedures Motion on April 7, 2026 — Docket Nos. 128, 129, and 130 respectively — establishing a compressed timeline targeting a June 22, 2026 Confirmation Hearing. That confirmation timeline was subsequently extended, with the Bankruptcy Court entering its Disclosure Statement approval order on May 28, 2026 (Docket No. 388), which simultaneously scheduled the Confirmation Hearing, established July 1, 2026 voting and objection deadlines, determined treatment of unliquidated and disputed claims for voting purposes, approved solicitation packages and ballot forms, and set the record date.

On the § 363 track, the sale process has now culminated. According to a bankruptcy filing, Keyrock SA agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property; Keyrock confirmed it was declared the "Successful Bidder" in an official Bankruptcy Court document filed on May 26, 2026. BlockFills' institutional client network — which includes hedge funds, asset managers, market makers, and mining companies — is a primary driver of the acquisition value.

The purchase price warrants scrutiny in the creditor-recovery calculus. The $3.25 million acquisition price represents a stark valuation collapse for an entity that claimed over $60 billion in trading volume just one year prior. Against $145 million in general unsecured debt and a maximum asset ceiling of $100 million, the § 363 sale proceeds — standing alone — suggest nominal recovery on unsecured claims absent material additional distributions from the liquidating trust or the plan's litigation recovery provisions. Final completion of the transaction remains subject to final court approval and the appropriate regulatory approvals referenced in Keyrock's bid. Creditors who have not yet filed proofs of claim through the Veritaglobal claims portal at veritaglobal.net/BlockFills should note that the July 1 voting deadline is also a critical claims-status checkpoint.

### II. Celsius Network — Preference Clawback Wave Persists; SDNY Reference-Withdrawal Ruling Forecloses Forum Shift

The post-emergence Celsius litigation complex remains one of the most consequential ongoing recovery programs in crypto insolvency, with direct cross-exposure to multiple tracked cases. The Litigation Administrator has filed more than 2,000 adversary proceedings targeting account holders who withdrew cryptocurrency from the platform during the 90-day preference period preceding the petition, seeking the return of more than $2 billion in such withdrawals from large customers. A Fourth Distribution of approximately $344.4 million commenced in February 2026, with the Bankruptcy Court having authorized service of summons and complaints via airdropped NFTs to anonymous wallet owners — an alternative-service mechanism keyed to the technology of the underlying transactions.

A significant procedural ruling last week cuts off one avenue of defendant resistance. A group of 174 former Celsius customers had sought to withdraw the reference of bankruptcy avoidance proceedings from the Bankruptcy Court, aiming to shift those claims to the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. On December 9, 2025, District Court Judge John G. Koeltl denied the motion to withdraw the reference, rejecting both the mandatory and permissive withdrawal arguments. On mandatory withdrawal, the court found the motion untimely — it was filed more than a year after defendants first indicated their intention to seek withdrawal and only after receiving an adverse ruling on preliminary issues — and concluded it may constitute tactical forum shopping. The Harvard Bankruptcy Roundtable flagged this ruling on June 9, 2026 as among the most significant recent crypto-insolvency procedural developments. The practical effect: defendants in the preference adversary proceedings will litigate in the Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, before Judge Martin Glenn, without the ability to re-route to an Article III court at the district level. For those named in the Administrator's 2,000+ adversary proceedings, the dismissal of the reference-withdrawal strategy materially narrows available defenses and accelerates exposure to judgment.

The Celsius / Power Block Coin nexus remains an important downstream data point. The Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors of Power Block Coin, LLC has filed a comprehensive liquidation plan in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah (Case No. 24-bk-23041), with Greenberg Traurig, LLP serving as committee counsel on a plan filed January 30, 2025 proposing to wind down through a Liquidating Trust overseen by an independent trustee. That plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million. Per the tracked-case ground truth, the case now sits before the Honorable Cathleen D. Parker, Bankr. D. Utah, Case No. 24-23041. The docket reflects no new activity in the 72 hours ending June 12, 2026 — the last filing was Docket No. 519 on June 9, 2026 — indicating the case is in a between-hearing administrative posture pending plan confirmation proceedings. Under the proposed structure, secured and priority claims would be paid in full, while general unsecured creditors would receive pro rata distributions from liquidated assets; the plan provides no recovery for equity interests, which would be cancelled.

### III. Structural Issues: Digital Asset Custody, Customer Property Classification, and the §363 Discount Problem

The BlockFills sale hearing today crystallizes a legal question that has run through the 2022–2026 crypto insolvency wave without definitive resolution: whether customer digital assets held in omnibus wallets constitute property of the estate under 11 U.S.C. § 541, or segregated customer property under a custodial trust theory. The Debtors filed a joint Chapter 11 plan featuring a dual-track toggle mechanism, with the central question of whether customer digital assets constitute property of the estate forming the analytical core of the restructuring. The Disclosure Statement (Docket No. 129) and the Amended Plan (associated with Docket No. 388) address this question in the context of the customer-led NewCo transaction and competitive § 363 sale process.

BlockFills focused solely on institutional, high-net-worth, and sophisticated customers and did not offer services to retail traders; its product suite included a proprietary trading platform, API connectivity, over-the-counter cryptocurrency trading, and derivatives trading for eligible contract participants. This exclusively institutional counterparty profile distinguishes BlockFills from retail-focused predecessors like Celsius and Voyager, but does not insulate it from the core custody classification debate. Courts in both the Southern District of New York and the Northern District of Illinois granted temporary restraining orders that restricted the Debtors' ability to conduct operations and exercise control over certain assets; the automatic stay afforded by Chapter 11 was intended to halt further litigation and prevent individual customers from pursuing assets outside the bankruptcy process.

From a practitioner standpoint, the § 363 sale price of $3.25 million for what was a $60B+ annual-volume trading platform raises the same deep discount concern that defined the FTX, Voyager, and Genesis proceedings. The valuation of cryptocurrency assets in bankruptcy proceedings is a complex and uncertain area of law, and with the rapid growth and volatility of the crypto market, bankruptcy courts have faced increasingly difficult questions about how to value digital assets. The FTX estate's recovery of between 118% and 142% on creditor claims stands as the outlier rather than the norm — and required two years of litigation, fraudulent conveyance recoveries, and favorable market timing. BlockFills' creditors face a condensed timeline with a fraction of that estate's litigation upside.
