**Today's delta: the Bartz final-approval order landed yesterday.** On July 20, 2026, 
the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the class settlement in *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, requiring Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to the authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from notorious pirate sites.
 
The court's order — Document 680, filed July 20, 2026, in Case No. 4:24-cv-05417-AMO — followed the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing and granted both the motion for final approval and, in part, the motion for attorney's fees, after carefully considering submissions from fifty-four class members and putative class members.
 This is the operative development for rights-holders with outstanding claims and for any claimants or claims investors monitoring the distribution waterfall.

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal. 4:24-cv-05417, Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín) — Final Approval Entered; Distribution Clock Running

The final-approval order confirms a settlement that, on the confirmed metrics, is without precedent in U.S. copyright history. 
The decision marks the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77 percent.
 That participation figure carries direct financial consequence: 
440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works were claimed
 — a remarkable result against a typical class-action claim rate of approximately 10 percent, 
with class counsel Justin Nelson projecting a per-work payout of approximately $3,100.



The settlement requires Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to the authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from LibGen and PiLiMi, and further requires Anthropic to destroy all original files of works torrented and downloaded from those sites and any copies that originate from the torrented copies.
 On the fee question, 
the attorney fee request was revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million
 before the court granted it in part. The fee reduction from the original 25% ask ($375 million per the settlement terms) is notable — it narrows net-to-claimant dilution and modestly increases per-work distributions above counsel's prior projections.

The distribution schedule is structured in installments. 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four tranches: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The first two tranches are already deposited. The September 25, 2026 payment ($450 million per the timeline modeled in the settlement agreement) is the next hard milestone. 
The settlement administrator is projected to issue a second wave of checks on approximately October 9, 2026, 120 days after issuance of the final approval order (assuming the order issues July 20, 2026).
 Any objector appeal to the Ninth Circuit would not automatically stay distributions but could complicate the timing of later tranches.

**Class definition for claims-recovery purposes:** 
The class covers legal or beneficial copyright owners — specifically, owners of the exclusive right to reproduce copies — of any book downloaded by Anthropic through LibGen in June 2021 or from PiLiMi in July 2022, bearing an ISBN or Amazon Standard Identification Number
 and registered with the U.S. Copyright Office within five years of first publication (or within three months of first publication under the timely-registration track). The § 411 registration prerequisite is thus a threshold gate for every residual claimant dispute. 
Judge Alsup's June 2025 summary judgment ruling had held that using books to train AI was fair use if acquired legally but denied summary judgment as to piracy — the piracy finding drove the settlement's entire theory of liability.
 That holding is now administratively final as between the settling parties and Anthropic, though it binds no other defendant in the broader AI-copyright docket.

**Docket carry-forward:** Docket Entry 679 (July 13, 2026) was Anthropic's opposition brief to a pending objection — the last substantive pre-order filing. Document 680 is the final approval order itself. The objector record (seven objectors, five minutes combined per the May 14 hearing) does not suggest a viable Ninth Circuit appeal on the merits, but rights-holder counsel should monitor the 30-day appeal window running from July 20.

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal.) — Two-Track Structure; October Summary Judgment Hearing Confirmed

The music-publisher litigation against Anthropic now runs on two parallel N.D. Cal. dockets before the same judge, and their relationship is the most consequential structural question in AI-copyright litigation outside the Bartz settlement.

**Concord I** — 
Case No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL, N.D. Cal., assigned to Hon. Eumi K. Lee, last updated June 22, 2026
 — is the original action filed October 18, 2023 by Concord Music Group, Universal Music Corp., ABKCO Music, Songs of Universal, Capitol CMG, and related entities. 
The summary judgment hearing is calendared for October 21, 2026 before Judge Lee; the questions presented are whether partial summary judgment on copyright infringement should be granted to plaintiffs, or whether summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement should be granted to Anthropic.
 Cross-briefing in Concord I closed June 8, 2026 per the court's scheduling order (Dkt. 587). At that stage, the fair-use framework under 17 U.S.C. § 107 is fully joined on both the training-input and output-reproduction theories.

**Concord II** — 
Case No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK, N.D. Cal., also assigned to Judge Lee, filed January 28, 2026
 — arose when 
Anthropic concealed during Concord I discovery that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books, including hundreds of works containing publishers' musical compositions, from pirate library websites; publishers did not learn of this until July 2025 when Judge Alsup's ruling in *Bartz* publicly revealed the torrenting conduct for the first time.
 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay Concord II, with a hearing set for May 20, 2026 before Judge Lee.
 Anthropic's stay theory: 
Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — that will determine the viability of most if not all of publishers' claims in Concord II, and Concord II should be stayed to resolve claims in an orderly and consistent manner.


The October 21 Concord I summary judgment hearing is, in functional terms, the next live dispositive event in the entire AI-copyright system. A ruling that Anthropic's training use of lyrics is not fair use — splitting from Alsup's training-data holding in *Bartz* — would create an intra-district conflict that pressures the Ninth Circuit to take up the question on appeal.

**Output vs. training distinction:** The music publishers' output claim (Claude verbatim-reproducing lyrics) is analytically distinct from the training-input question. 
The publishers contend that Claude's chatbot regurgitates significant portions, and in some cases the entire portion, of original copyrighted lyrics when prompted by users.
 That is a § 506 / § 504 exposure independent of the training fair-use defense, and it is not resolved by any of the Alsup or Chhabria rulings.

### III. *In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation* (S.D.N.Y. 1:25-md-03143, Hon. Sidney H. Stein) — Discovery Midstream; 20-Million-Log Production Ongoing


The OpenAI MDL — No. 1:25-md-03143, S.D.N.Y. — is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang managing discovery.
 
The action consolidates 16 copyright lawsuits.


The governing discovery order from January 5, 2026, remains the most consequential data-production directive in any AI-copyright case. 
Judge Wang ordered production of the 20-million ChatGPT log sample in November 2025; Judge Stein affirmed on January 5, 2026, ruling that Judge Wang's decision was "neither clearly erroneous nor contrary to law," finding user privacy interests adequately protected by limiting discovery to 20 million logs rather than tens of billions, OpenAI's de-identification of the data, and the existing protective order.
 
On relevance, Judge Wang held that even output logs without reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's § 107(4) market-harm defense — logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal patterns relevant to whether outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works.


The market-harm nexus is significant for § 107 factor four. Production of 20 million logs creates an evidentiary record that could directly undercut the "no harm to the market for the original" prong of OpenAI's fair-use defense — and that evidentiary record will be available to the *New York Times* (1:23-cv-11195-SHS) and the Authors Guild component of the MDL alike. 
Summary judgment briefing in *NYT v. OpenAI* concluded April 2026
; no argument date has been publicly set.

### IV. *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* (No. 25-2153, 3d Cir.) — First U.S. Appellate Ruling Pending


On June 11, 2026, the Third Circuit heard oral argument in *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153 — the first federal appellate case to squarely address whether using copyrighted works to train an AI model qualifies as fair use.
 
The three-judge panel consists of Judges L. Felipe Restrepo, Tamika R. Montgomery-Reeves, and Emil J. Bove, who focused questioning on the first and fourth fair-use factors — transformativeness and actual or potential market harm.


The district court posture: 
Judge Bibas granted partial summary judgment to Thomson Reuters on direct infringement for 2,243 headnotes, rejected several defenses, and granted Thomson Reuters summary judgment against ROSS's fair use defense.
 On appeal, ROSS invoked the Third Circuit's April 7, 2026 decision in *ASTM v. UpCodes* as a fresh doctrinal lever. 
ROSS argued that decision — involving access to standards incorporated into law — confirms that law-adjacent factual and functional works receive a fair use analysis sensitive to public access and thin protection.


On the market-harm question, 
ROSS argued that the statute asks about the market for the copyrighted work — here, the headnote — and resisted defining the market as "AI training" or "legal research platforms," warning that doing so would make fair use circular: any innovative use could be converted into a licensing market simply because the copyright owner, after seeing the use, says it would have licensed it.
 Thomson Reuters countered that copying was direct, systematic, and caused measurable market harm: Ross built a competing product using Thomson Reuters' own intellectual property as its training substrate. 
The outcome will set binding precedent on whether copying copyrighted works to train a non-generative AI system qualifies as fair use.
 A ruling is anticipated within three to six months of the June 11 argument — i.e., by approximately Q4 2026.

**Cross-system significance:** 
Three U.S. district judges have now ruled on AI training and fair use — Alsup (*Bartz*), Chhabria (*Kadrey*), and Bibas (*Thomson Reuters v. Ross*) — with Alsup and Chhabria finding for AI companies on training and Bibas finding against. The Third Circuit's ruling — argued June 11, 2026 — is the first appellate check on that split and remains pending.
 A decision reversing Bibas would align the only appellate ruling with the California consensus; affirmance would deepen the split and accelerate pressure on the Ninth Circuit or Supreme Court.
