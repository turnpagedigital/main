### Hut 8 / USBTC: Preliminary Approval Motion Filed S.D.N.Y. (New)


Hut 8 Corp. has agreed to pay $2.35 million to settle a securities class action alleging it misled investors about operational risks at a Texas Bitcoin mining site linked to its 2023 merger with U.S. Data Mining Group, Inc. (d/b/a US Bitcoin Corp. / USBTC), according to a preliminary settlement approval motion filed June 22 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York.
 
The settlement details were laid out in a memorandum of law filed June 22, 2026, before Judge Victor Marrero.


The class period runs 
from February 13, 2023, through January 18, 2024, covering investors who purchased or acquired Hut 8 securities in the U.S. or on a U.S.-based exchange.
 The litigation traced directly to 
a short-seller report by J Capital Research published on January 18, 2024 — the same day Hut 8 rang the Nasdaq opening bell — which challenged the company's statements about the USBTC deal and alleged problems at King Mountain.
 
Hut 8 shares fell more than 23% that day.


The case narrowed substantially before settlement. 
In September 2025, Judge Marrero dismissed investors' Exchange Act claims in their entirety and rejected their Securities Act claims arising from alleged misstatements about USBTC's financial condition before the merger.
 
He allowed Securities Act claims to proceed only on alleged omissions regarding risks at King Mountain — specifically, whether Hut 8's merger materials adequately disclosed infrastructure vulnerabilities at a facility that was material to USBTC's mining operations.
 Facing that posture, 
Hut 8 indicated it would seek judgment on the pleadings by challenging traceability, arguing that registered and unregistered shares had been commingled after the merger, making it difficult for aftermarket purchasers to trace their holdings back to the registration statement.
 That traceability argument — a live and potentially dispositive defense under Section 11 of the Securities Act — framed the settlement calculus.


The $2.35 million figure represents approximately 19.6% of the maximum estimated recoverable damages of roughly $12.08 million.
 
Plaintiff counsel said that it exceeds the 12.9% median and 14.6% average recovery for
 Securities Act-only settlements — a benchmark that matters because the Exchange Act fraud claims were already gone. 
The settlement was reached after a full-day virtual mediation session before JAMS mediator Jed D. Melnick on May 7, 2026.
 
The mediator's proposal was accepted by both parties on May 13, 2026.
 
The formal settlement agreement is dated June 18.


Recovery-posture note: 
Lead Plaintiff Abhishek Maheshwari has asked the court to grant preliminary approval, certify the settlement class, and schedule a final fairness hearing.
 The traceability problem that drove settlement here is a recurring structural feature of crypto-miner securities litigation — all-stock mergers that combine registered and unregistered share pools create exactly the commingling argument Judge Marrero allowed Hut 8 to threaten. Claimants in peer miner cases should expect defendants to press the same theory at the motion-to-dismiss or judgment-on-the-pleadings stage.

### Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal. 3:24-cv-05417): Final Approval Pending; Distribution Clock Running

The case has been in the post-fairness-hearing limbo since May 14. 
After a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, presiding judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement in the *Bartz v. Anthropic* copyright infringement lawsuit — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval for the deal relatively quickly.


The claims picture is settled and favorable. 
Class counsel filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10%).
 
Lead attorney for the plaintiffs Justin Nelson gave a brief update at the hearing, noting the opt-in rate had inched up from 91.3% to 92.77%.
 The extraordinarily high claims rate — driven in part by direct outreach from the Authors Guild and Authors Alliance — has materially compressed the per-work recovery. 
There was no indication in the hearing that if the settlement is approved, there would be any change in the agreement that each work would be eligible for a payout of approximately $3,000 to $3,100.


Judge Martínez-Olguín's post-hearing order targeted fee structure and late opt-outs: 
the order directed Anthropic to file, by May 21, 2026, a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and required plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.
 Fee exposure is significant — 
plaintiff lawyers are seeking 25% of the fund, or $375 million, with settlement administration by JND expected to cost several million dollars, and service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives).


The distribution mechanics are structured on four Anthropic payment installments. 
Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 Per the settlement timeline, 
the settlement administrator was scheduled to calculate per-work and per-claimant distributions on June 11, 2026 — with June 2026 the earliest payments if no objectors filed appeals.
 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved; payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest if appeals are filed.


The structural lesson for rights-holders tracking AI-training copyright settlements: the 92.77% claims rate in Bartz is an outlier driven by the narrow, well-defined class (works from LibGen / PiLiMi with registered U.S. copyright registrations and ISBN/ASIN numbers) and aggressive institutional mobilization. Future AI-training cases with less crisply defined classes — or where the registry-to-work matching is contested — should anticipate standard 10–15% claim rates, which dramatically improves per-claimant recovery for those who file. The opt-out posture also matters: 
Anthropic has denied any wrongdoing, and this settlement does not create binding precedent on the fair-use question — future courts may find such AI training use to be non-infringing.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration: Scale, TOS Mechanics, and the July 1 Inflection Point

This campaign, launched formally on May 11, 2026, is the largest mass arbitration ever attempted by estimated dollar value and warrants close attention from any sophisticated advertiser. The predicate is solid. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
While many companies that displayed ads purchased through Google have sued for damages since the rulings in 2024, advertiser contracts with the search giant require mandatory arbitration over legal disputes
 — which is precisely what Keller Postman is exploiting.


According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.
 
The $218 billion estimated recovery figure is an estimate, not a filed demand amount; in mass arbitration, the leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger — not from any single damages number.
 On the damages theory: 
potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law
 via Clayton Act § 4. 
In addition to representing the private claimants in the arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state AGs in a parallel case.


The July 1 TOS inflection point is material. 
Google's current terms expire on June 30, 2026, and the company sent notification emails to account holders on June 1, 2026.
 The revised terms — which take effect July 1 — introduce batch arbitration mechanics: 
the new terms allow for batch arbitration of identical or nearly identical claims, replacing the previous framework that handled each claim individually.
 Critically, 
the new terms include a 30-day window to opt out of arbitration via a web form; if an advertiser opts out, it retains the right to bring disputes in court, but if the window is missed, the advertiser is bound by the arbitration clause.
 The practical read: advertisers with ongoing ad spend who want to preserve judicial options must affirmatively opt out within 30 days of July 1. Advertisers who instead want to join the mass arbitration campaign should be aware that 
deadlines vary by state, and earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.



The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most group legal proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.
 That novelty creates real procedural uncertainty — forum selection disputes, batch-formation rules under AAA/JAMS Mass Arbitration Supplementary Rules, and fee-shifting mechanics are all untested at this scale on a corporate-advertiser docket. The remedies phase before Judge Brinkema in the underlying ad-tech antitrust case (E.D. Va.) is still pending, and 
Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling is expected in 2026; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines.
 A divestiture order (DOJ's preferred remedy) would significantly strengthen damages computations in the arbitration by locking in monopoly-period boundaries.
