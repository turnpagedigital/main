## Theme: How $218B in mass arbitration works — the fee trigger, not the merits

### LinkedIn

There's a $218 billion claim against Google that most people are analyzing the wrong way. They're asking whether the advertisers will win. That's not the question that moves it.

On June 23, Keller Postman fires the first AAA wave in its Google advertiser mass-arbitration campaign — the largest such campaign ever attempted, built on alleged ad overcharges and the antitrust findings in the FTC and DOJ cases. Here is the mechanism that makes mass arbitration different from a class action.

In a class action, one filing aggregates millions of claimants, and the defendant litigates once. In mass arbitration, the same claimants file individually — thousands, then tens of thousands of separate arbitrations. Under AAA's mass-arbitration fee schedule, each one carries its own administrative and arbitrator fees, and the company pays the lion's share before a single merits question is decided. That per-claim fee exposure, multiplied at scale, is the settlement-forcing mechanism. It operates whether or not any individual claim is strong.

This is the same dynamic that turned arbitration clauses — drafted by companies to avoid class actions — into a liability of their own. The clause that blocks aggregation forces individualization, and individualization at scale is expensive in a way aggregation never was.

The same posture is queuing up against Apple's App Store developers and Meta's advertisers. For any business that bought Google Search or Display advertising since August 2016, the practical question before June 23 is narrow: does the net recovery — 20–30% of relevant ad spend, less contingency and filing cost — justify enrolling? Run that number on the economics, not the headline.
