# $1B+ Class Actions & Mass Arbitration — Posts | Wednesday, May 20, 2026

**Single theme:** The June 17 *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee* (MDL 1720) final-approval math — 10 bps reduction vs. the $13–15B/year merchant-overcharge baseline.

## LinkedIn

Twenty-eight days. That is the runway between today and the June 17 final-approval hearing in *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL 1720, before Judge Brian M. Cogan in the Eastern District of New York. The hearing will close 21 years of litigation that began in 2005. The question that will dominate the courtroom is the one the merchant objectors have spent the briefing cycle making: whether the relief is meaningful enough to clear Rule 23(e)(2)(C)(i).

The headline term is a 10-basis-point reduction in interchange on standard consumer credit transactions over five years (2026 through 2031), and a 1.25 percent cap on interchange for standard consumer credit over eight years (2026 through 2034). Premium rewards cards are exempt from the cap. The Honour-a-Customer rule is relaxed to permit merchants to steer customers toward lower-cost payment methods, and merchant surcharging and decline rights are expanded.

The objectors' math is straightforward. The annual overcharge estimate that economic experts have built for the class — Cornerstone Research, NERA, and the National Retail Federation's economic consultants converge in the same range — sits between $13 billion and $15 billion per year, depending on transaction-mix and rate-structure assumptions. Ten basis points off the standard consumer credit base does not move the annual overcharge meaningfully against that baseline. NRF, the National Association of Convenience Stores, the National Restaurant Association, Walmart, and Hugo Boss have all preserved this objection on the record.

Plaintiffs' counsel will argue that a 10 bps reduction over five years, plus the rate cap, plus the steering and surcharging relief, together capture between $30 billion and $40 billion in projected merchant savings — and that the structural relief (steering, surcharging, HAC relaxation) is the more durable part of the deal because it does not sunset in five years. Judge Cogan inherited the case from Judge Margo K. Brodie in September 2025 after Judge Brodie's June 2024 rejection of the original settlement on the same insufficient-relief ground. The procedural posture at the hearing will turn on whether the revised structural terms cure what the original deal failed to cure.

Merchants tracking the docket should be doing two things now. First, evaluate their pro rata recovery model against the headline 10 bps reduction and the 1.25 percent cap, with the structural-relief value modeled separately. Second, prepare claim-filing infrastructure for the post-approval window — distributions, if approved, will follow within 90 to 120 days of entry of final judgment, and merchants with strong contemporaneous interchange records will file faster and clear validation cleaner.
