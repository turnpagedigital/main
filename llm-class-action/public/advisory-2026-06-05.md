The clarifying development for claimants remains timing: the settlement administrator's June 11 distribution computation locks per-work allocations, but disbursement will not begin until Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín enters the final-approval order and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved — counsel and the claims administrator indicate payments are unlikely to commence before late fall 2026 at the earliest, notwithstanding the administrator's own portal language that initial disbursement could begin "June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed" (__[Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/advocacy/artificial-intelligence/what-authors-need-to-know-about-the-anthropic-settlement/)__) (__[Authors Alliance — FAQ](https://www.authorsalliance.org/resources/generative-ai/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-faq/)__) (__[Copyright Alliance](https://copyrightalliance.org/participating-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement/)__). UpCodes* on the record (__[Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)__).

The $1.5 billion *Bartz v. Anthropic* settlement — the largest copyright class resolution in United States history — remains pending final approval twenty-two days after the May 14 fairness hearing before Judge Martínez-Olguín in Courtroom 12 of the San Francisco Federal Courthouse. The named plaintiffs are nonfiction authors Charles Graeber (*The Good Nurse*) and Kirk Wallace Johnson (*The Feather Thief*) and thriller novelist Andrea Bartz (*We Were Never Here*). The 91.3 percent claims rate — 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works claimed by approximately 120,000 authors and rightsholders — was certified in Class Counsel's April 16 updated claims filing (Dkt. 643) and is treated in the proposed final-approval order (Dkt. 646-1) as foreclosing any adequacy-of-notice objection on appeal. Class counsel revised the fee request downward from the $300 million originally contemplated (15 percent) to 12.5 percent of the gross fund, yielding approximately $187.5 million in fees plus $3 million in incurred expenses, an $18.22 million cost reserve, and $50,000 service awards to each of the three class representatives (__[BakerHostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/bartz-v-anthropic/)__). The settlement requires Anthropic to destroy its pirated-copy training datasets and pay approximately $3,000 per eligible claimed work, with a contingent additional $3,000 per work if claimed works exceed 500,000 — a threshold the 91.3 percent rate keeps the payout near the original $3,000 figure (__[Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-understanding-americas-largest-copyright-settlement/)__). At twenty-two days post-hearing, the sequencing question is concrete: the June 11 computation will be calculated against a fund whose final approval may still be pending, and disbursement is gated on both approval entry and appeal exhaustion. Authors who submitted claims should verify payment-information completeness on the settlement portal now, since the June 11 computation locks per-work allocations even though cash will not move until the fall.

## Thomson Reuters v. ROSS — Six Days to Third Circuit Oral Argument

*Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.), is six days from its June 11 oral argument — the first appellate review of a district-court fair-use ruling applied to AI model training and the highest-priority AI-copyright event on the 2026 judicial calendar. The district court initially granted ROSS partial summary judgment on fair use, then reversed, holding that ROSS's construction of a competing legal-research tool from Westlaw headnotes was not transformative because the economic purpose was direct substitution. ROSS's appellate theory is that the "purpose" prong of the four-factor fair-use test — as developed in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994) and refined by *Blanch v. Koons*, 467 F.3d 244 (2d Cir. 2006) — is an objective inquiry into the end-state utility of the use, not a market-substitution inquiry, and that model training produces a fundamentally new artifact whose relationship to the training corpus is analytically distinct from the original expression. Thomson Reuters counters that fourth-factor market substitution is the governing lens: any commercial use that enables a competing product is non-transformative where it extracts verbatim or near-verbatim expressive content. Both parties filed supplemental briefs on May 11 addressing *ASTM v. UpCodes, Inc.*, the Third Circuit's April 2026 decision holding that copying legal standards incorporated by legislative reference into enforceable law is fair use under an access-to-law rationale. ROSS argues *UpCodes* signals that the first-factor purpose inquiry is objective utility; Thomson Reuters distinguishes on the basis that the access-to-law rationale is narrowly grounded and does not extend to commercial AI training of private editorial work product (__[Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)__). The Third Circuit's framing of "transformativeness" will propagate into the *OpenAI* MDL fair-use defense, the *Elsevier v. Meta* Rule 12 briefing, and the *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic* cross-MSJ, setting the standard those matters will litigate against. The June 25, 2025 *Kadrey v. Meta* ruling — in which Judge Vince Chhabria found training fair use on the specific record but warned that unlicensed training "in most cases" will infringe absent a market-harm failure of proof — is the district-level counterweight the panel will be asked to reconcile (__[Jackson Walker](https://www.jw.com/news/insights-kadrey-meta-bartz-anthropic-ai-copyright/)__). Appellate practitioners and amicus coordinators with positions in those cases should finalize their June 11 positioning this week.

## Elsevier v. Meta — 31 Days Without Responsive Pleading; Executive-Authorization Narrative

*Elsevier v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (S.D.N.Y., filed May 5, 2026) — the complaint by Elsevier, Cengage Learning, Hachette Book Group, Macmillan Publishing Group, McGraw Hill, and author Scott Turow against Meta for systematic copyright infringement in training the Llama models — is now thirty-one days on the docket without a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, extension request, or answer. The complaint alleges that between April and July 2024, Meta downloaded 134.6 TB via torrenting while uploading 40.42 TB from Library Genesis and Sci-Hub — a combined 267 TB corpus (__[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01481-0)__). The distinguishing feature of the *Elsevier* complaint is the executive-authorization narrative: it alleges that in April 2023, Meta's business-development team received verbal instructions, escalated to and approved by Mark Zuckerberg, to cease licensing efforts and shift to shadow-library acquisition. That allegation strengthens the willful-infringement case both as a statutory-damages multiplier (17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2)) and as a discovery anchor for depositions of the decision-making chain above the procurement team. At thirty-one days, a responsive pleading of some kind — a motion to dismiss, a 30-day extension request, or an answer asserting fair use — is the structural next event; its absence past the conventional response window is itself worth tracking. The seeding theory (that downloading and storing pirated material is independently actionable regardless of the training fair-use defense) survived *Bartz* and is pressed here alongside the training-infringement claim (__[Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/05/05/surprise-elsevier-is-suing-meta-for-you/)__).

## Kadrey v. Meta — Discovery Closes in June; Seeding Theory for Trial

*Kadrey v. Meta* continues toward the June close of the fact-discovery window, which will trigger summary-judgment briefing on the seeding theory in Q3 2026. Judge Chhabria's denial of summary judgment on the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy issue preserved the seeding claim — the allegation that Meta's act of uploading torrented copyrighted material is independently actionable separate from the training fair-use defense. Mark Zuckerberg's December 17, 2024 deposition is on the record. The *Elsevier* complaint's corroborating executive-authorization narrative on essentially the same April 2023 factual timeline creates a direct intersection between the *Kadrey* discovery record and the *Elsevier* complaint. Meta's litigation teams across both matters must now coordinate deposition-alignment strategy for members of the business-development chain and the Zuckerberg approval chain at the April 2023 decision point, with the discovery window's June close the immediate forcing event.

## OpenAI MDL — Log Analysis Ongoing; Scheduling Order Mid-July

The plaintiff-side pattern analysis of the full 20 million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs — produced pursuant to Judge Sidney Stein's January 2026 affirmance of the magistrate's production order — continues in *In re OpenAI Copyright Litigation* MDL (S.D.N.Y.). An additional 88 million supplemental logs (78 million plus 10 million additional reservoirs), ordered produced on March 9, 2026, are in the production queue. The analytical frame is the fourth-factor market-substitution inquiry: do ChatGPT outputs compete with copyrighted news content in a way that reduces market or value? The parties are working through statistical-summary logistics while preserving individual-user privacy, with a scheduling order anticipated mid-July before Judge Stein. Settlement is not on the horizon for this matter; discovery and expert phases are likely to run through 2027 (__[Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-must-turn-over-20-million-chatgpt-logs-judge-affirms)__). The $3,000-per-book *Bartz* benchmark continues to be invoked by both sides in fee and quantum modeling, including for the news-content and academic-content categories of the *OpenAI* MDL claimants.

## AI Copyright Legislation — TRAIN, NO FAKES, CLEAR vs. TRUMP AMERICA AI

The competing legislative frameworks continue their committee tracks. The TRAIN Act (subpoena procedures for copyright holders), NO FAKES Act (digital-replica rights), and CLEAR Act (notice-filing requirements for AI training datasets) remain in House Judiciary and Senate Commerce markup. The administration's TRUMP AMERICA AI Act — which would preempt state AI laws and limit class-action remedies for training-data infringement — is the competing vehicle, and its pre-emption provisions would, if enacted, retroactively eliminate liability for training uses that form the basis of the *OpenAI* MDL, *Kadrey*, *Elsevier*, and *Concord* complaints. The *Bartz* final-approval order, when it enters, will land as a concrete legislative data point: a $1.5 billion settlement ratified by a federal court is the strongest possible evidence against the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act's premise that training-data infringement claims are without merit or adequately addressed by existing law.
