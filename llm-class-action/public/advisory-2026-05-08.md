# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | May 8, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Six days before the Bartz v. Anthropic fairness hearing, five of the world's largest academic and trade publishers — Elsevier, Cengage, Hachette Book Group, Macmillan Publishing Group, and McGraw Hill — joined bestselling author Scott Turow in filing a class action in the Southern District of New York against Meta Platforms, Inc. and CEO Mark Zuckerberg personally, alleging willful copyright infringement on an extraordinary scale to train Meta's Llama large language models. ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/05/nx-s1-5812623/scott-turow-meta-lawsuit)) The May 5 complaint contends that Meta torrented approximately 267 terabytes of pirated material — equivalent to hundreds of millions of publications and many times the size of the entire print collection of the Library of Congress — from LibGen and Anna's Archive, and that Zuckerberg "personally authorized and actively encouraged" the piracy after Meta's business development team received verbal instructions in early April 2023 to abandon a licensing strategy that had contemplated budgets of up to $200 million. ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/05/05/james-patterson-mark-zuckerberg-authorized-copyright-infringement-meta-book-lawsuit/)) The timing is procedurally significant: the complaint arrives just as Judge Martínez-Olguín prepares to evaluate the adequacy of the $1.5 billion Bartz settlement under Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(e)(2), and the publishers' evidentiary showing — internal Meta communications, the scale of the alleged copying, and the deliberate rejection of market-rate licensing — will inevitably be cited as a comparator for settlement valuation, both by proponents arguing the Bartz figure is reasonable given the industry's willingness to sue and by objectors arguing the per-title payout undercompensates relative to the statutory damages exposure of 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) ($150,000 per willfully infringed work). The Association of American Publishers issued a statement supporting the publishers' action on the day of filing. ([AAP](https://publishers.org/news/publishers-and-authors-file-class-action-lawsuit-against-meta-and-zuckerberg-for-willful-copyright-infringement-to-develop-llama-ai-models/))

The Meta publisher complaint also distinguishes itself from the dismissed Kadrey v. Meta action in a manner that has direct doctrinal implications. Judge Chhabria's June 2025 summary judgment for Meta in Kadrey turned on the absence of substantially similar outputs — the court found that the plaintiffs had not demonstrated that Llama produced text sufficiently similar to the ingested works to satisfy the extrinsic/intrinsic similarity test. ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)) The publisher plaintiffs appear to have structured their complaint to avoid that deficiency: by foregrounding the unauthorized reproduction and distribution of the works themselves (the torrenting, storage, and processing of 267 TB of pirated copies) rather than relying solely on output-side similarity claims, the complaint tracks the theory that proved successful in Bartz — where Judge Alsup held that training on lawfully obtained works constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107, but that downloading pirated copies from shadow libraries constitutes per se infringement independent of any fair-use defense. ([Variety](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/meta-ai-mark-zuckerberg-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-publishers-scott-turow-1236738383/)) Meta has vowed to "fight this lawsuit aggressively," citing Google v. Oracle and contending that training AI on copyrighted material qualifies as fair use, but the Bartz precedent on pirated-copy liability and the internal evidence of abandoned licensing undercut the strongest version of that defense.

On the Bartz settlement administration front, the claim statistics remain unchanged from the prior advisory — 440,490 of 482,474 eligible works claimed (91.3% claim rate) — and the distribution computation remains scheduled for June 11, with the settlement administrator applying the weighted methodology challenged by Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's objection. ([All About Lawyer](https://allaboutlawyer.com/bartz-v-anthropic-1-5-billion-copyright-settlement-authors-claim/)) The Authors Alliance continues to maintain its updated FAQ and unsealed-objections catalogue, which identifies five principal objection themes: foreign-work exclusion, publisher-author allocation, notice deficiencies, inadequate per-work compensation relative to § 504(c) statutory damages, and class-counsel conflict concerns arising from simultaneous representation in the Concord and UMG music-publisher dockets. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) The hearing will be held May 14 at 2:00 p.m. PT at the San Francisco Federal Courthouse with Zoom participation available, and Judge Martínez-Olguín's ruling will be the first judicial determination under Rule 23(e) of whether a billion-dollar AI copyright settlement meets the adequacy, reasonableness, and fairness standard — a determination that will set the benchmark for every subsequent AI-training settlement negotiation.

The broader docket continues to accelerate toward a summer of dispositive rulings. The Third Circuit's oral argument in Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence on June 11 remains the most consequential near-term appellate event — the first federal appeals court to address whether AI training on copyrighted works constitutes fair use — and ROSS Intelligence's Rule 28(j) letter citing the Third Circuit's recent ASTM v. UpCodes decision continues to frame the panel's analysis. ([Chat GPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/04/14/third-circuit-sets-oral-argument-on-june-11-for-1st-appeal-of-decision-on-fair-use-in-ai-training-case-follows-another-recent-third-circuit-decision-on-fair-use-in-american-society-for-testing-v-upc/)) In the Concord Music v. Anthropic docket, cross-motions for partial summary judgment remain fully briefed with a ruling expected this summer on whether Anthropic's acknowledged copying of 100+ compositions constitutes infringement or falls within the transformative-use framework. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/infringement-on-a-massive-scale-umg-concord-and-abkco-ask-court-to-rule-against-ai-company-anthropic-ahead-of-trial/)) Internationally, Getty Images' appeal of the UK High Court's November 2025 ruling — that AI model weights are not "infringing copies" under CDPA — remains pending before the Court of Appeal, with the novel question of whether an "article" can constitute an infringing copy when it never contained the copyrighted works in a human-readable form. ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/next-steps-for-getty-v-stability-why-has-permission-to-appeal-been-granted))

## Recommended Actions

Class members in the Bartz settlement should complete final verification of their claim status and payment information through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com within the next six days, with particular attention to works filed under group Copyright Office registrations that may require individual enumeration before the June 11 distribution computation — the Bishop objection's challenge to the weighted allocation formula makes this verification especially urgent for individual authors whose works share registration numbers with publisher-submitted batches. Practitioners advising rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz class should reassess the expected value of individual claims in light of the Meta publisher complaint: the evidentiary showing of deliberate piracy with CEO authorization and abandoned licensing strengthens the willfulness theory under § 504(c) and may increase statutory damages exposure across the industry, but it also validates the Bartz settlement's core theory that pirated-copy liability (as distinguished from fair-use-protected training) is the actionable infringement theory. AI developers facing training-data litigation should conduct immediate audits of their data-provenance documentation, particularly any internal communications regarding licensing decisions — the Meta complaint's reliance on internal emails showing an affirmative decision to pirate rather than license demonstrates that discovery of licensing-strategy communications will be a priority in every pending case, and the TRAIN Act's proposed administrative subpoena mechanism would make such disclosure mandatory upon enactment.
