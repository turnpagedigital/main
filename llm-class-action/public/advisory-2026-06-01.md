## Analysis & Developments

Two clocks now dominate the AI-copyright desk as June opens. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.), remains under submission before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín eighteen days after the May 14 Rule 23(e) fairness hearing — well past the midpoint of the customary 7–21 day post-fairness-hearing window and into the band where entry of the final-approval order should be imminent (__[Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/resources/generative-ai/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-faq/)__). The operational significance of the calendar tightened over the weekend: the settlement administrator is scheduled to calculate per-work distributions by June 11, ten days out, which means the final-approval order and the distribution-computation milestone now converge inside the same week, and any appeal clock will start running against a fully computed allocation rather than an abstract fund (__[Author's Guild](https://authorsguild.org/advocacy/artificial-intelligence/what-authors-need-to-know-about-the-anthropic-settlement/)__). The second clock is the Third Circuit's June 11 oral argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153 — also ten days out — the first appellate review of a district-court fair-use ruling applied to AI model training. Below those two, *Elsevier v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (S.D.N.Y., filed May 5) now stands at twenty-seven days on the docket without a Meta responsive pleading; *Kadrey v. Meta* seeding-theory discovery continues toward its June close; and the *OpenAI* MDL plaintiff-side pattern-analysis of the full 20 million ChatGPT conversation-log dataset continues building the fourth-factor market-substitution record (__[Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-must-turn-over-20-million-chatgpt-logs-judge-affirms)__).

The *Bartz v. Anthropic* fairness hearing on May 14 closed the record on what would be the largest copyright class settlement in United States history — the $1.5 billion fund Anthropic agreed to in August 2025. The named plaintiffs are nonfiction authors Charles Graeber and Kirk Wallace Johnson and thriller novelist Andrea Bartz, and the settlement requires Anthropic to destroy the pirated datasets, pay approximately $3,000 per work, and honored a 120-day opt-out window. Author-side claims activity at the close of the bar date set the rate at 91.3 percent of identified class members — 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works claimed by approximately 120,000 authors and rightsholders — a figure that effectively forecloses any meaningful adequacy-of-notice objection on appeal (__[Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-understanding-americas-largest-copyright-settlement/)__). Class counsel revised the fee request downward from 15 percent to 12.5 percent of the gross fund, yielding a petition of approximately $187.5 million plus $3 million in incurred expenses, an $18.22 million cost reserve, and $50,000 service awards to each of the three class representatives. Payouts to class members will issue only after the court grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are resolved; with the administrator's distribution computation due June 11, disbursement could begin in late June or later depending on appeals (__[Fieldfisher](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/services/intellectual-property/intellectual-property-blog/anthropic-s-1-5-billion-copyright-settlement-with)__). The per-book pricing implicit in the settlement — approximately $3,000 per book — remains the operative comparator across the *OpenAI* MDL, *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic*, and the *Disney v. Midjourney* negotiating posture.

## Thomson Reuters v. ROSS — Third Circuit Oral Argument June 11, Ten Days Out

The Third Circuit has scheduled oral argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153, for June 11, 2026 — now ten days out — the first appellate review of a district-court fair-use ruling applied to AI model training. The district court initially granted ROSS partial summary judgment on fair use, then reversed and held that ROSS's use was not transformative because ROSS built a competing legal-research tool from Westlaw headnotes. Both parties filed supplemental briefs on May 11 addressing the Third Circuit's April 2026 decision in *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc.*, which addressed fair use of standards legally incorporated into law and made freely available for access-to-law purposes. ROSS argues that *UpCodes* clarifies that the first factor calls for an objective inquiry into purpose, and that ROSS's objective use — training an AI model on the relationships among legal words — is at least as transformative as what UpCodes did. Thomson Reuters counters that *UpCodes* simply applies the settled framework distinguishing commercial, substitutive uses from those serving a genuinely different purpose, and that the fundamental distinction is that *UpCodes* involved standards incorporated into law, whereas ROSS trained on Westlaw headnotes to build a competing research tool. The Third Circuit's framing of "transformativeness" against substitutionary use will set the standard for the rest of the docket and likely influence settlement postures across the music-publishing and news-publisher AI cases. With argument ten days out, expect amicus attention from both the developer coalition and the rightsholder bar to peak this week.

## Elsevier v. Meta — 267 TB Piracy Complaint, Twenty-Seven Days Without Answer

On May 5, 2026, Elsevier, Cengage Learning, Hachette Book Group, Macmillan Publishing Group, McGraw Hill, and author Scott Turow filed a putative class-action complaint in the Southern District of New York alleging systematic copyright infringement by Meta in the training of its Llama models (__[Publishers Association Complaint](https://publishers.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-05-05-Complaint.pdf)__). The complaint alleges that between April and July 2024, Meta downloaded 134.6 TB via torrenting while uploading 40.42 TB, predominantly copyrighted academic and trade publications sourced from Library Genesis and Sci-Hub. The critical distinction from earlier AI-copyright filings is the executive-knowledge element: the complaint alleges that in early April 2023, Meta abruptly abandoned its licensing strategy, and that the decision to pivot from licensing to piracy was "escalated" to Mark Zuckerberg (__[NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/05/nx-s1-5812623/scott-turow-meta-lawsuit)__). Following that escalation, Meta's business-development team received "verbal instructions" to cease licensing efforts. The 267 TB figure represents the total alleged pirated corpus, equivalent to many times the entire print collection of the Library of Congress (__[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01481-0)__). Meta has indicated it will defend on a fair-use theory and intends to "fight this lawsuit aggressively." At twenty-seven days on the docket without a responsive pleading, Meta's answer or initial Rule 12 motion is the next scheduled inflection. The executive-authorization narrative substantially strengthens the non-fair-use position relative to prior AI-training filings and is likely to reset settlement signaling in parallel music-publisher and academic-publisher matters (__[Hachette Book Group](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/articles/publishers-and-authors-file-class-action-lawsuit-against-meta-and-zuckerberg-for-willful-copyright-infringement-to-develop-llama-ai-models/)__).

## Kadrey v. Meta — Seeding-Theory Discovery Continues Through June

The *Kadrey v. Meta* docket continues to move on the seeding theory — the allegation that Meta's independent act of uploading (seeding) torrented copyrighted material is actionable copyright infringement separate from the fair-use defense applicable to the training itself. Judge Vince Chhabria's earlier denial of summary judgment on the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy issue kept the seeding theory alive for discovery and trial. Meta executive depositions, including the deposition of Mark Zuckerberg (completed December 17, 2024), have been completed through the fact-discovery period. In deposition snippets, Zuckerberg cited YouTube's battle to remove pirated content as a defense to Meta's own acquisition and use of pirated e-books, distinguishing between platforms that host user-generated content and platforms that directly acquire and store pirated material (Meta's posture). The seeding theory — that downloading and storing copyrighted material from shadow libraries is independently actionable — survived the *Bartz* settlement and remains the most viable plaintiff-side theory in cases where training-corpus formation involved torrenting. The discovery window closes in June, setting up trial-readiness briefing in Q3 2026.

## OpenAI MDL — 20M ChatGPT Logs in Plaintiff Hands, Pattern Analysis Underway

Judge Sidney Stein affirmed in January 2026 the magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the full 20 million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs in the multidistrict litigation (__[Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-must-turn-over-20-million-chatgpt-logs-judge-affirms)__). The publisher plaintiffs and co-plaintiffs have now received the entire dataset and are conducting discovery pattern-analysis to identify instances where ChatGPT outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted news content. The logs — anonymized but comprehensive — cover a broad range of user queries and responses across the news, entertainment, reference, and commercial domains. This dataset is critical to the fourth-factor (market-harm) inquiry: if ChatGPT outputs directly compete with or substitute for news articles, that is strong evidence against fair use. The parties are working through the logistics of producing summaries and statistical analyses of the 20 million logs while maintaining privacy protections for the individual users who submitted the queries, with a scheduling order anticipated mid-July before Judge Stein.

## AI Copyright Legislation — TRAIN, NO FAKES, CLEAR in Committee, TRUMP AMERICA AI as Counterweight

Committee activity on the TRAIN Act (training-data transparency), NO FAKES Act (digital-replica rights), and CLEAR Act (AI content labeling) carried through May, with markup activity in House Judiciary and Senate Commerce. The TRAIN Act would create a federal mechanism allowing copyright holders to use subpoena power to determine whether their work was used to train an AI model, modeled on internet-piracy discovery procedures. The NO FAKES Act would establish a federal property right protecting voice, image, and likeness from generative-AI deepfakes and clones without consent. The CLEAR Act would require companies to file notice with the Copyright Office detailing copyrighted works used in training datasets before public release, with retroactive application to existing models and a public database maintained by the Copyright Office. Simultaneously, the administration has pushed the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act as a competing framework that would preempt state AI laws and limit class-action remedies for AI training — a provision that would dramatically narrow developer liability exposure by capping damages or eliminating class actions altogether. The competing-framework posture is the through-line: a *Bartz* final-approval order entered this month would land as the most concrete data point in the legislative debate over whether class-action remedies for training-data infringement should survive.

Anthropic*, *Disney v. Midjourney*, and any newly filed cases. Appellate practitioners and amicus coordinators tracking *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS* should finalize argument-day positioning this week, since the Third Circuit's transformativeness framing on June 11 will propagate into every pending training-data fair-use posture. Music-publishing counsel watching *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic* should expect the *Elsevier v. Meta* executive-knowledge allegations to reset settlement signaling, since the Elsevier complaint's detailed executive-authorization narrative materially strengthens the non-fair-use position. Plaintiff-side firms in AI training-data cases should align discovery on the seeding theory where shadow-library acquisition is alleged, since the fair-use defense does not reach the seeding question post-*Bartz*. Companies developing generative-AI models should prepare compliance documentation for the contingent-liability scenarios presented by TRAIN Act subpoena procedures and CLEAR Act notice-filing requirements, modeling both creator-protection and developer-preemption (TRUMP AMERICA AI passage) scenarios.
