# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 23, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Today was to have been the day that the largest copyright settlement in American history faced its reckoning before the bench: Judge Martínez-Olguín's April 8 order moved the Bartz v. Anthropic fairness hearing from this afternoon to May 14, 2026 at 2:00 p.m. PT in Courtroom 12 of the San Francisco Federal Courthouse, granting all parties — and the court — three additional weeks to absorb the six-plus unsealed objections and the revised fee petition that class counsel filed in late March. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) That fee petition is itself a significant development: Susman Godfrey and Lieff Cabraser reduced their request from $300 million (approximately 20% of the $1.5 billion fund) to $187.5 million (12.5%), a 37.5% reduction that followed pointed judicial scrutiny of the original structure, which had included $75 million earmarked for three firms — Cowan DeBaets, Edelson, and Oppenheim & Zebrak — not formally appointed to represent the full class under Rule 23(g). ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/cpi-posts/anthropic-copyright-settlement-lawyers-cut-fee-request-to-187-5-million/)) ([Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2455799/authors-attys-cut-fee-bid-to-187m-in-1-5b-anthropic-ip-deal)) The practical effect is an upward revision in the net settlement fund: with fees now at $187.5 million plus administration and notice costs, Publishers Weekly's April 20 estimate of approximately $1.29 billion available for distribution — yielding a base payout of roughly $2,931 per work across the 440,490 titles claimed by the March 30 deadline — may prove conservative, and the actual per-title figure could inch closer to the originally projected $3,000 benchmark depending on how the court resolves the publisher-versus-author allocation formula and whether any cy pres distribution is ordered for residual unclaimed funds. Anthropic's second quarterly installment of $300 million is due in seven days on April 30, bringing total funded commitments to $600 million of the $1.5 billion fund — a payment milestone that will provide the court with tangible evidence of the defendant's financial capacity under Rule 23(e)(2)(D) at the May 14 hearing. ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/digital/copyright/article/100208-anthropic-settlement-hearing-comes-into-focus.html))

The Concord Music Group v. Anthropic litigation has taken a sharply personal turn with the First Amended Complaint filed on April 7, which redirects the contributory infringement claim from Anthropic as a corporate entity to CEO Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann individually — a strategic pivot driven directly by the Supreme Court's unanimous March 25 reversal in Cox Communications v. Sony Music Entertainment, which narrowed contributory copyright liability to circumstances involving either inducement or services not capable of substantial noninfringing uses. ([ChatGPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/04/07/after-supreme-courts-decision-in-cox-communications-concord-music-changes-its-claim-of-intentional-contributory-infringement-from-anthropic-to-dario-amodei-benjamin-mann-based-on-evidence-revealed/)) The FAC incorporates allegations drawn from deposition testimony and evidence unsealed in the Bartz proceedings, adds Claude 4.6 Opus and Sonnet to the accused instrumentalities, and sits alongside the parallel $3.1 billion Concord/UMG v. Anthropic II action filed in January covering 20,517 torrented songs — together creating a litigation architecture in which Anthropic's leadership faces both corporate and personal exposure on training-input, output-generation, and distribution theories. The cross-industry amicus record continues to broaden: RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, and SoundExchange filed on the publishers' side in late March, followed by a separate brief from the AAP, News/Media Alliance, STM, and Authors Guild arguing that a functioning licensing market already exists and that Anthropic's unlicensed copying undermines it at scale — a position that, if adopted by the court on the partial summary judgment motion, would effectively collapse the fourth fair-use factor for any AI developer that trained on copyrighted material without negotiating licenses. ([Reuters](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com,2026:newsml_L6N41418J:0-anthropic-seeks-pivotal-court-win-in-music-publisher-lawsuit-over-ai-training/)) ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/))

On Capitol Hill, the 25th annual GRAMMYS on the Hill concludes today with the Recording Academy championing three bills that collectively represent the most significant legislative push yet on AI and creators' rights: the TRAIN Act, which would grant creators administrative subpoena power to compel disclosure of whether their works were used in AI training datasets; the CLEAR Act (S. 3813), introduced by Senators Schiff and Curtis, requiring AI companies to file detailed notices with the Copyright Office enumerating every copyrighted work in training data, backed by $5,000 per-work civil penalties; and the NO FAKES Act, which would create a federal property right protecting individuals from unauthorized AI-generated digital replicas of their voice, image, or likeness. ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/blogs/in-the-know/5843379-lawmakers-honor-music-protections/)) ([Recording Academy](https://www.recordingacademy.com/advocacy/news/grammys-on-the-hill-2026-chris-coons-maria-salazar-congressional-honorees)) Congressional honorees Senator Chris Coons (D-DE) and Representative María Salazar (R-FL) signal bipartisan support for creator protections, and the simultaneous advocacy push on three distinct bills — disclosure, transparency, and digital-replica rights — reflects a legislative strategy that avoids the politically fraught question of whether to legislatively abrogate fair use for AI training, as Senator Blackburn's competing "Trump America AI Act" discussion draft would do, and instead builds a compliance infrastructure that would reshape the operational environment for AI developers regardless of how courts resolve the pending fair-use motions in the Concord, OpenAI MDL, and UMG v. Suno dockets. ([Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/03/ai-legislation-copyright-hollywood-trump-1236759660/))

Internationally, the Getty Images v. Stability AI appeal remains the most consequential pending matter, with the UK Court of Appeal set to consider whether AI model weights constitute "infringing copies" under CDPA § 27 — a question Mrs Justice Joanna Smith acknowledged "has a real prospect of success" when granting leave to appeal on December 16, 2025. ([Chambers](https://chambers.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-remain-in-focus-for-2026-with-getty-appeal-given-the-green-light)) ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/next-steps-for-getty-v-stability-why-has-permission-to-appeal-been-granted)) A reversal would create secondary copyright liability for any organization importing foreign-trained AI models into the UK and would interact directly with the CJEU's Like Company v. Google referral — the first occasion the Court of Justice has addressed generative AI and copyright under EU law — whose advisory opinion could reshape the DSM Directive's text and data mining exception across all 27 member states. The Kluwer Copyright Blog's newly published comparative analysis of the Bartz settlement notes that the approximately $3,000 per-work benchmark will inevitably influence negotiation dynamics in parallel dockets and voluntary licensing arrangements worldwide, establishing a floor price that rights-holders will cite in every future training-data licensing negotiation. ([Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-understanding-americas-largest-copyright-settlement/))

## Recommended Actions

Class members who filed Bartz claims by the March 30 deadline should verify their submission confirmations through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and calendar the rescheduled May 14 fairness hearing, which will be accessible via Zoom — the revised fee petition of $187.5 million (down from $300 million) means the net fund available for distribution is likely to exceed the $1.29 billion estimate, and claimants should monitor post-hearing filings for any court-ordered adjustments to the publisher-versus-author allocation formula or the per-title valuation methodology challenged by Professor Bishop and other objectors. Rights-holders monitoring the Concord v. Anthropic docket should study the April 7 First Amended Complaint carefully, as the pivot to individual liability for Amodei and Mann — driven by Cox Communications — previews the personal-liability theories that plaintiffs' counsel will deploy in future AI training cases, and music publishers with works in the 499-song catalog should prepare for accelerated settlement negotiations if the partial summary judgment ruling goes against fair use before the summer 2026 trial window. AI developers subject to the proposed TRAIN Act's administrative subpoena authority and the CLEAR Act's retroactive disclosure mandate should accelerate training-data provenance audits immediately — the GRAMMYS on the Hill advocacy push signals growing bipartisan momentum, and the $5,000 per-work penalty structure in the CLEAR Act alone creates exposure that, for companies with millions of training works, could dwarf the Bartz settlement figure. Practitioners monitoring cross-border exposure should track the Getty v. Stability AI appeal timeline and prepare contingency assessments for the scenario in which model weights are deemed "infringing copies" under UK law, as that ruling would require urgent reassessment of AI model import and deployment strategies across all jurisdictions recognizing secondary copyright liability.
