The *Bartz v. Anthropic* settlement — $1.5 billion, the largest copyright class resolution in United States history — reached fairness hearing on May 14 before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in Courtroom 12 of the San Francisco Federal Courthouse. The named plaintiffs are nonfiction authors Charles Graeber (*The Good Nurse*) and Kirk Wallace Johnson (*The Feather Thief*) and thriller novelist Andrea Bartz (*We Were Never Here*). The settlement fund was agreed in August 2025 following Anthropic's acknowledgment that its training corpus included substantial pirated-copy acquisitions from shadow libraries, and the settlement requires Anthropic to destroy the pirated datasets, pay approximately $3,000 per eligible work, and honored a 120-day opt-out window. The claims rate at bar-date close was 91.3 percent — 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works claimed by approximately 120,000 authors and rightsholders — a figure that the settlement administrator has certified in Class Counsel's April 16 updated claims filing (Dkt. 643) and that the court's proposed final-approval order (Dkt. 646-1) treats as foreclosing any adequacy-of-notice objection on appeal. Class counsel revised the fee request downward from 15 percent to 12.5 percent of the gross fund, yielding a petition of approximately $187.5 million plus $3 million in incurred expenses, an $18.22 million cost reserve, and $50,000 service awards to each of the three class representatives. The June 11 per-work distribution computation is the next scheduled milestone, with disbursement contingent on the final-approval order and the resolution of any timely appeals. The per-book pricing implicit in the settlement — approximately $3,000 per book — continues to serve as the operative comparator across the *OpenAI* MDL, *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic*, *Disney v. ## Thomson Reuters v. ROSS — Nine Days to Oral Argument, Amicus Peak Week

*Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.), is nine days from its June 11 oral argument — the first appellate review of a district-court fair-use ruling applied to AI model training. The district court initially granted ROSS partial summary judgment on fair use, then reversed and held that ROSS's use was not transformative because ROSS built a competing legal-research tool from Westlaw headnotes. ROSS's fair-use theory is that indexing the relationship among legal words in headnotes for the purpose of training a model is fundamentally different from reproducing the expressive elements of Westlaw's editorial choices — that the objective purpose is transformative even if the economic competition is direct. Thomson Reuters counters that the second-factor market substitution is the key: any commercial use that creates a competing product using verbatim or near-verbatim extracts is non-transformative under *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994). Both parties filed supplemental briefs on May 11 addressing *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc.*, the Third Circuit's April 2026 holding on fair use of standards legally incorporated into law. ROSS argues that *UpCodes* signals that the first factor's "purpose" inquiry is objective — the end-state utility of the use — not a market-substitution analysis. Thomson Reuters distinguishes on the basis that *UpCodes* involved access-to-law rationale that does not extend to commercial AI training. The Third Circuit's framing of "transformativeness" against substitutionary commercial purpose will set the standard for the balance of the training-data docket — likely including the *Elsevier v. Meta* fair-use defense and the *OpenAI* MDL — and will directly influence settlement postures in the music-publishing and news-publisher AI cases. Expect amicus attention from the developer coalition and rightsholder bar to peak this week ahead of the June 11 hearing. Norton Rose Fulbright's mid-year AI copyright update confirms that the *ROSS* argument is the single most anticipated AI fair-use event on the 2026 litigation calendar (__[Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)__).

## Elsevier v. Meta — 267 TB Piracy, Day 28 Without Answer

*Elsevier v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (S.D.N.Y., filed May 5, 2026), the complaint by Elsevier, Cengage Learning, Hachette Book Group, Macmillan Publishing Group, McGraw Hill, and author Scott Turow alleging systematic copyright infringement in the training of Meta's Llama models, is at twenty-eight days on the docket without a Meta responsive pleading. The complaint alleges that between April and July 2024, Meta downloaded 134.6 TB via torrenting while uploading 40.42 TB from Library Genesis and Sci-Hub — a total 267 TB corpus described as equivalent to many times the entire print collection of the Library of Congress (__[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01481-0)__). The critical distinguishing feature remains the executive-knowledge narrative: the complaint alleges that in April 2023, Meta's business-development team received "verbal instructions" — escalated to and approved by Mark Zuckerberg — to cease licensing efforts and shift to shadow-library acquisition. Meta has indicated it will defend on a fair-use theory. At twenty-eight days, a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, a request for an extension, or a formal answer is the next structural event. The seeding theory — that downloading and storing copyrighted material from shadow libraries is independently actionable regardless of the training fair-use defense — survived the *Bartz* settlement and will be pressed here as it is in *Kadrey v. Meta*. The executive-authorization narrative substantially strengthens the willful-infringement case and is likely to reset settlement signaling in parallel music-publisher and academic-publisher matters (__[Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/05/05/surprise-elsevier-is-suing-meta-for-you/)__) (__[Chemical & Engineering News](https://cen.acs.org/articles/104/web/2026/05/elsevier-sues-meta-over-ai.html)__).

## Kadrey v. Meta — Discovery Closes in June; Seeding Theory Intact

*Kadrey v. Meta* continues on its fact-discovery schedule with June close of the discovery window setting up trial-readiness briefing in Q3 2026. Judge Vince Chhabria's denial of summary judgment on the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy issue kept the seeding theory — the allegation that Meta's independent act of uploading (seeding) torrented copyrighted material is actionable separate from the training fair-use defense — alive for discovery and trial. Meta executive depositions, including Mark Zuckerberg's completed December 17, 2024 deposition, are on the record. The June discovery close will trigger summary-judgment briefing on the seeding theory in Q3. Given the Elsevier complaint's corroborating narrative about Zuckerberg's April 2023 decision, the trial record in *Kadrey* — which predates the Elsevier filing — now intersects with a second corporate knowledge narrative on essentially the same factual timeline. Defense counsel coordination between *Kadrey* and *Elsevier* is now the principal deposition-alignment challenge for Meta's litigation team.

## OpenAI MDL — Log Analysis Underway, Scheduling Order Mid-July

The plaintiff-side pattern analysis of the full 20 million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs — produced pursuant to Judge Stein's January 2026 affirmance of the magistrate's production order — continues. The analytical frame is the fourth-factor market-substitution inquiry: do ChatGPT outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted news content in a way that would reduce the market for or value of the original works? The logs are being analyzed for instances of verbatim or near-verbatim news text reproduction, response substitution for paid subscription access, and paraphrase-at-scale patterns that aggregate infringing uses. The parties are working through logistics of producing statistical summaries while maintaining individual-user privacy, with a scheduling order anticipated mid-July before Judge Stein. The log-analysis results will be the fourth-factor linchpin in OpenAI's fair-use defense.

## AI Copyright Legislation — TRAIN, NO FAKES, CLEAR vs. TRUMP AMERICA AI

The competing legislative frameworks continue their committee tracks. TRAIN Act subpoena procedures, NO FAKES Act digital-replica rights, and CLEAR Act notice-filing requirements remain in House Judiciary and Senate Commerce markup. The administration's TRUMP AMERICA AI Act — which would preempt state AI laws and limit class-action remedies — is the competing vehicle. The *Bartz* final-approval order, when entered, will be the most concrete datapoint in the legislative debate over whether training-data infringement should generate class-action liability at all. A final-approval order this week at $1.5 billion would land as a settled fact in committee testimony.

Midjourney* matters should treat the *Bartz* per-book pricing (approximately $3,000 per book) as the operative settlement comparator for fee and quantum modeling. Appellate practitioners and amicus coordinators tracking *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS* should finalize June 11 positioning this week — the Third Circuit's transformativeness framing will propagate into every pending training-data fair-use posture. Objector counsel who preserved appellate rights at the *Bartz* fairness hearing should pre-stage notices of appeal against the imminent final-approval order. Music-publishing and academic-publisher counsel watching *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic* and *Elsevier v. Meta* should expect the Zuckerberg executive-knowledge narrative to reset settlement signaling by establishing willful-infringement as a viable theory against major model providers — a standard that substantially increases potential statutory damage exposure above actual-damage floors. Plaintiff-side firms pursuing AI training-data cases should align discovery on the seeding theory where shadow-library acquisition is alleged, since the fair-use defense does not reach independent seeding liability under post-*Bartz* doctrine. Generative-AI developers should prepare TRAIN Act compliance frameworks now, modeling both subpoena-defense and notice-filing obligations under the CLEAR Act's retroactive-application provisions.
