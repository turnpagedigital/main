# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | May 11, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Three days before the most consequential copyright hearing in the history of artificial intelligence, the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement enters its final pre-hearing window with all five principal objection themes fully briefed, a 91.3% claim rate that has no precedent in class-action practice, and a distribution computation scheduled for exactly one month after the May 14 fairness hearing — creating a compressed timeline that leaves Judge Martínez-Olguín little margin if the Rule 23(e)(2) analysis reveals deficiencies requiring supplemental briefing or amended distribution terms. The hearing itself — set for 2:00 p.m. PT in Courtroom 12 of the San Francisco Federal Courthouse, with Zoom access available to class members — will require the court to make individualized findings on each of the five objection categories catalogued by the Authors Alliance: the exclusion of foreign works not registered with the U.S. Copyright Office, the systematic publisher-author allocation imbalance in the distribution plan, the adequacy of notice under Rule 23(c)(2)(B), the per-work compensation level relative to § 504(c) statutory damages (approximately $3,000 per title against a statutory maximum of $150,000 for willful infringement), and the class-counsel conflict-of-interest concern arising from Susman Godfrey and Lieff Cabraser's simultaneous representation of the Bartz book-author class and their involvement in parallel dockets where Anthropic's litigation posture is adversarial. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's unsealed objection — which challenges the weighted distribution methodology's adequacy under Rule 23(e)(2)(D) and highlights that group Copyright Office registrations have collapsed dozens of distinct books into single "claimable works," reducing compensation as if Anthropic infringed one book rather than forty — will be the focal point for practitioners assessing whether the court treats the per-work valuation as a reasonable compromise or an insufficient return given the scope of admitted infringement. ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/anthropic-settlement-update-91-percent-of-books-claimed/); [AllAboutLawyer](https://allaboutlawyer.com/bartz-v-anthropic-1-5-billion-copyright-settlement-authors-claim/))

The parallel docket continues to accelerate toward a summer of overlapping rulings that will define AI copyright law for a generation. The Concord Music Group, UMG, and ABKCO cross-motions for partial summary judgment against Anthropic are now set for hearing on July 15, 2026, before Judge Lee, with the publishers' motion supported by a 47-page statement of 218 undisputed facts and an amicus brief from the RIAA and NMPA arguing that Anthropic's unlicensed copying of 499 musical compositions is "inexcusable" — language designed to foreclose the fair-use defense by emphasizing the commercial nature of Anthropic's use (a company now valued at over $380 billion with a $14 billion revenue run rate), the wholesale character of the copying, and the direct competition with licensed lyrics services under the fourth fair-use factor. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/infringement-on-a-massive-scale-umg-concord-and-abkco-ask-court-to-rule-against-ai-company-anthropic-ahead-of-trial/); [RIAA/NMPA Amicus](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) A ruling against Anthropic on summary judgment in the music-publisher docket would arrive while the Bartz settlement is still in its post-approval appeal window, creating the unusual dynamic of a defendant simultaneously paying out the largest copyright settlement in history while facing an adverse infringement finding in a related docket — a scenario that could embolden Bartz objectors to argue on appeal that the settlement undervalued the class's claims.

The Meta publisher class action filed May 5 in the Southern District of New York — brought by Elsevier, Cengage, Hachette, Macmillan, McGraw Hill, and bestselling author Scott Turow — continues to generate sustained legal and media coverage as the complaint's novel personal-liability theory against CEO Mark Zuckerberg reverberates through the AI industry's governance and insurance structures. The complaint alleges that Zuckerberg personally authorized and encouraged Meta to torrent 267 terabytes of pirated material from LibGen and Anna's Archive after abandoning a $200 million licensing budget, creating an evidentiary record on willfulness under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) that strengthens the piracy-based infringement theory across all pending cases, while Meta has publicly vowed to "fight this lawsuit aggressively." ([Variety](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/meta-ai-mark-zuckerberg-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-publishers-scott-turow-1236738383/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/05/nx-s1-5812623/scott-turow-meta-lawsuit)) The licensing-market dynamics tracked by Lewis Silkin and the Publishers Association confirm that the dual-track pattern of litigation pressure accelerating licensing deals continues: nearly 40 independent publishers coordinated by the IPG sent letters of claim to AI developers in February 2026, while the Publishers Association's March 2026 report documents a maturing AI licensing market with publisher participation expected to nearly double by year-end and multi-million-pound deals already in place with predominantly U.S.-based technology firms. ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/04/15/copyright-owners-v-ai-developers-the-emerging-trends); [AI Business](https://aibusiness.com/generative-ai/ai-lawsuits-in-2026-settlements-licensing-deals-litigation))

The Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence oral argument at the Third Circuit on June 11, 2026 — coinciding with the Bartz distribution computation — will produce the first federal appellate consideration of whether AI training on copyrighted works constitutes fair use, with ROSS Intelligence having recently filed a Rule 28(j) letter drawing the panel's attention to the Third Circuit's own April 7 decision in American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc., which addressed fair use in the context of a for-profit, AI-native startup's republication of building codes adopted as law. ([Chat GPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/04/14/third-circuit-sets-oral-argument-on-june-11-for-1st-appeal-of-decision-on-fair-use-in-ai-training-case-follows-another-recent-third-circuit-decision-on-fair-use-in-american-society-for-testing-v-upc/)) The convergence of June 11 events means that class members could receive their first payments on the same day that the appellate court begins constructing the framework determining whether the Bartz settlement's per-work valuation was a prescient compromise or a significant undervaluation. Internationally, the Getty Images appeal to the UK Court of Appeal — permission granted December 16, 2025 — will produce the most significant UK judicial consideration of AI training and copyright, with potential to diverge from or align with emerging U.S. appellate authority. ([Chambers & Partners](https://chambers.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-remain-in-focus-for-2026-with-getty-appeal-given-the-green-light)) On the legislative front, the TRAIN Act's administrative-subpoena mechanism and the French Senate's April 8 rebuttable-presumption bill continue to advance the transparency agenda from opposite sides of the Atlantic, with Jones Day's May 2026 comparative analysis identifying the French burden-shifting approach under Article L. 331-4-1 of the Intellectual Property Code as a potential model for U.S. legislative proposals and noting the CJEU's pending Like Company v. Google case (AG opinion expected September 3) as the authoritative test of DSM Directive Article 4 TDM opt-outs. ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/navigating-copyright-in-the-age-of-generative-ai-eu-french-and-uk-developments-and-approaches))

## Recommended Actions

Class members in the Bartz settlement should treat the next 72 hours as a final preparation window: verify claim status and payment information through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com, confirm that works filed under group Copyright Office registrations have been individually enumerated where required by the distribution methodology — the Bishop objection highlights that failure to enumerate can collapse forty-plus books into a single claimable work — and arrange to monitor the May 14 hearing via Zoom to understand the court's treatment of the five principal objection themes, which will signal whether appellate challenges are likely and whether the June 11 distribution timeline will hold. Practitioners advising rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz class should reassess expected value of individual claims in light of the Meta publisher complaint's evidentiary showing: the willfulness theory under § 504(c) is materially strengthened by evidence of CEO-authorized piracy with abandoned licensing, but the per-work valuation established by the Bartz settlement will function as a gravitational benchmark in any individual negotiation. AI developers facing training-data litigation should conduct immediate audits of data-provenance documentation and internal licensing-decision communications — the Meta complaint demonstrates that discovery of abandoned-licensing communications and executive authorization of shadow-library downloads will be a priority in every pending case — and should monitor the Concord Music summary judgment hearing (July 15) and Thomson Reuters oral argument (June 11) as the two proceedings most likely to produce binding or persuasive authority on the scope of the fair-use defense before the Andersen v. Stability AI trial begins on September 8, 2026.
