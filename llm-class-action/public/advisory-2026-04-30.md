# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 30, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Today marks a pivotal milestone in the administration of the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement: Anthropic's second $300 million installment is due, bringing total funded amounts to $600 million of the $1.5 billion agreed payout — making this by far the largest copyright settlement fund in U.S. history by the amount actually disbursed to date. With the Rule 23(e) fairness hearing now fourteen days away on May 14, 2026 at 2:00 p.m. PT before Judge Martínez-Olguín, the settlement enters its most consequential procedural phase. Class counsel's revised fee petition of $187.5 million — 12.5 percent of the settlement fund, slashed from the original $375 million (25 percent) request after Judge Alsup's December 23 memorandum criticizing the fee-sharing arrangements with three outside firms — will be weighed alongside the Rule 23(h) lodestar cross-check at the fairness hearing, and the court must also address the six-plus unsealed objections, including Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's challenge to the distribution plan's alleged publisher favoritism and the group-registration collapse theory that could reduce per-title compensation for prolific authors. ([Anthropic Settlement Key Dates](https://www.anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com/dates)) ([JDJournal](https://www.jdjournal.com/2026/03/23/anthropic-settlement-lawyers-cut-fees-to-win-approval/)) ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/))

The parallel Concord Music Group v. Anthropic litigation has reached its own inflection point with competing summary judgment motions now teed up for the May hearing calendar. On April 20, Anthropic filed its cross-motion arguing that training Claude on song lyrics is "transformative" fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107, characterizing the ingestion of lyrics alongside trillions of other words as necessary for Claude to "understand the interrelationships between words and concepts in human language" rather than to reproduce or substitute for licensed lyrics services. UMG, Concord, and ABKCO's partial summary judgment motion, filed March 23, counters that Anthropic — a for-profit company valued at $380 billion with revenue approaching $14 billion — copied lyrics in full without ever seeking a license, and that Claude's outputs directly compete with services like LyricFind and Musixmatch. The RIAA, NMPA, and six other industry groups' amicus brief characterizing Anthropic as a "holdout" that scrapes rather than licenses adds fourth-factor market-harm pressure, while the CCIA, AI Progress, and NetChoice counter-amicus frames training as "intermediate, non-expressive copying." The court's resolution of these cross-motions will be the first judicial ruling on whether AI training on music constitutes fair use and could set precedent across the broader AI copyright docket. ([Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/04/22/anthropic-music-publishers-lawsuit-summary-judgment-motion/)) ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) ([CCIA](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/04/ccia-urges-court-to-affirm-fair-use-protections-for-generative-ai-training/))

In the OpenAI MDL (No. 3143, S.D.N.Y.), the docket was updated as recently as April 24, with Judge Stein's enforced order requiring production of the full 20-million ChatGPT log sample — the most expansive compelled disclosure in any AI copyright proceeding — continuing to shape the discovery landscape. Summary judgment briefing in the NYT v. OpenAI track is expected by mid-2026, and plaintiffs are increasingly citing Judge McMahon's denial of OpenAI's motion to dismiss in the Advance Local v. Cohere matter as output-side infringement precedent, particularly the "substitutive summaries" theory that AI-generated outputs can serve as market substitutes for the original copyrighted works. The Books1/Books2 spoliation inquiry under Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e) remains unresolved and may yield adverse-inference sanctions if OpenAI cannot demonstrate reasonable preservation measures. Bloomberg Law's April 29 analysis of AI "vibe coding" — where developers use high-level natural language prompts to generate code with minimal review — warns that this practice "does not immunize code from copyright risk and could even increase it," adding a new output-side dimension to the infringement theories percolating through Doe v. GitHub and the broader DMCA § 1202 CMI landscape. ([CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/)) ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/legal-exchange-insights-and-commentary/copyright-infringement-suits-loom-with-unchecked-ai-vibe-coding)) ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/openai-loses-privacy-gambit-20-million-chatgpt-logs-likely-headed-copyright))

On the legislative and regulatory front, the CLEAR Act (S. 3813), introduced February 10 by Senators Schiff and Curtis, continues to gather bipartisan momentum with endorsements from more than 25 organizations including SAG-AFTRA, the Authors Guild, and the RIAA. The bill would require AI companies to file a notice with the Register of Copyrights detailing copyrighted works in training datasets at least 30 days before commercial release, with civil penalties of $5,000 per failure and a public database of filings. The NO FAKES Act (H.R. 2794 / S. 1367) addresses the adjacent right-of-publicity dimension, while Taylor Swift's April 24 trademark filings to protect her voice and image from AI deepfakes — including two "sound marks" — signal that individual creators are not waiting for legislative solutions. The Supreme Court's March 2, 2026 denial of certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter has cemented that AI-generated works require human authorship for copyright protection, a principle that may constrain output-side theories in pending cases while reinforcing that the copyrighted inputs used in training retain full protection under the Copyright Act. ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/02/11/clear-act-establish-notice-requirements-copyrighted-works-ai-training-data/)) ([Gerben IP](https://www.gerbenlaw.com/blog/taylor-swift-moves-to-trademark-her-voice-and-image-as-ai-threats-grow/)) ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/us-supreme-court-declines-to-consider-whether-ai-alone-can-create-copyrighted-works))

Internationally, the CJEU's first-ever hearing on generative AI and copyright in Like Company v. Google (March 10, 2026) is awaiting the Advocate General's opinion, which will provide the first binding EU-level guidance on whether generative AI training falls within the DSM Directive Article 4 text-and-data-mining exception. Getty Images' appeal of the UK High Court ruling dismissing its secondary copyright infringement claim against Stability AI has been granted permission and is expected to be heard during the course of 2026 — the Court of Appeal's determination of whether model weights constitute "infringing copies" will be the first appellate ruling on this issue in any common-law jurisdiction. The Munich Regional Court's ruling in favor of GEMA, holding that storage of lyrics in model parameters constitutes copyright infringement, adds a continental European data point that may influence the Advocate General's analysis. ([Chambers and Partners](https://chambers.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-remain-in-focus-for-2026-with-getty-appeal-given-the-green-light)) ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/like-company-v-google-cjeu-holds-first-ever-hearing-on-generative-ai-and-copyright-on-10-march-2026)) ([Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/what-does-2026-augur-ai-and-ip-europe))

## Recommended Actions

With Anthropic's second $300 million installment due today and the fairness hearing now just two weeks away, class members who filed claims should verify their claim status through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and ensure all contact and payment information is current, as the settlement administrator's distribution computation is scheduled for June 11 — authors whose works were registered under group Copyright Office registrations should be especially vigilant, given the live objection that group-registration collapse may reduce per-title payouts. Rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement and are pursuing independent claims should be tracking the Concord v. Anthropic cross-motions closely, as the court's ruling on whether music training constitutes fair use will directly inform the viability of training-side theories in separate proceedings and may establish the first judicial precedent on AI training fair use since the Bartz summary-judgment framework. AI developers should benchmark their licensing posture against the CLEAR Act's proposed 30-day pre-release disclosure requirement and evaluate whether their training-data provenance documentation would survive the kind of discovery compulsion demonstrated by Judge Stein's 20-million-log order in the OpenAI MDL — Bloomberg Law's analysis of "vibe coding" copyright risks underscores that output-side exposure extends beyond training to the deployment context. Practitioners monitoring international developments should calendar the expected Advocate General opinion in Like Company v. Google and the Getty v. Stability AI Court of Appeal hearing, as both will shape whether the EU and UK adopt a training-as-infringement framework that diverges from the more permissive U.S. fair-use analysis.
