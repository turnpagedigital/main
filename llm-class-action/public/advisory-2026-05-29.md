The per-book pricing implicit in the settlement — approximately $3,000 per book at the high end of the claimed pool — is now the operative comparator for the *OpenAI* MDL, *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic*, and the *Disney v. Midjourney* negotiating posture.

## Elsevier v. Meta — 267 TB Piracy Complaint, Executive Knowledge Allegations

On May 5, 2026, Elsevier, Cengage Learning, Hachette Book Group, Macmillan Publishing Group, McGraw Hill, and author Scott Turow filed a putative class-action complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York alleging systematic copyright infringement by Meta in the training of its Llama models (__[Publishers Association Complaint](https://publishers.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-05-05-Complaint.pdf)__). The complaint alleges that between April and July 2024, Meta downloaded 134.6 TB via torrenting while uploading 40.42 TB, predominantly copyrighted academic and trade publications sourced from Library Genesis and Sci-Hub. The critical distinction from earlier AI-copyright filings is the executive-knowledge element: the complaint alleges that in early April 2023, Meta abruptly abandoned its licensing strategy, and that the decision to pivot from licensing to piracy was "escalated" to Mark Zuckerberg (__[NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/05/nx-s1-5812623/scott-turow-meta-lawsuit)__). Following that escalation, Meta's business-development team received "verbal instructions" to cease licensing efforts. The 267 TB figure represents the total alleged pirated corpus, equivalent to hundreds of millions of publications and many times the entire print collection of the Library of Congress (__[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01481-0)__). Meta has indicated it will defend on a fair-use theory and intends to "fight this lawsuit aggressively." At twenty-four days on the docket without a responsive pleading, Meta's answer or initial Rule 12 motion is now the next scheduled inflection. The executive-authorization narrative substantially strengthens the non-fair-use position relative to prior AI-training filings, and is likely to reset settlement signaling in parallel music-publisher and academic-publisher matters (__[Hachette Book Group](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/articles/publishers-and-authors-file-class-action-lawsuit-against-meta-and-zuckerberg-for-willful-copyright-infringement-to-develop-llama-ai-models/)__).

## Thomson Reuters v. ROSS — Third Circuit Oral Argument June 11, Thirteen Days Out

The Third Circuit Court of Appeals has scheduled oral argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153, for June 11, 2026 — now thirteen days out. The case is the first appellate review of a district-court fair-use ruling applied to AI model training. The district court initially granted ROSS partial summary judgment on fair use, then reversed and held that ROSS's use was not transformative because ROSS built a competing legal-research tool from Westlaw headnotes. Both parties filed supplemental briefs on May 11 addressing the Third Circuit's April 2026 decision in *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc.*, which addressed fair use in the context of standards legally incorporated into law and made freely available for access-to-law purposes. ROSS argues that *UpCodes* clarifies the first factor calls for an objective inquiry into purpose, and that ROSS's objective use — training an AI model on the relationships among legal words — is at least as transformative as what UpCodes did. Thomson Reuters counters that *UpCodes* simply applies the settled framework distinguishing commercial, substitutive uses from those serving a genuinely different purpose, and that the fundamental distinction is that *UpCodes* involved standards legally incorporated into law, whereas ROSS trained on Westlaw headnotes to build a competing research tool. The Third Circuit's framing of "transformativeness" against substitutionary use will set the standard for the rest of the docket and likely influence settlement postures across the music-publishing and news-publisher AI cases.

## Kadrey v. Meta — Seeding-Theory Discovery Continues Through June

The *Kadrey v. Meta* docket continues to move on the seeding theory — the allegation that Meta's independent act of uploading (seeding) torrented copyrighted material is actionable copyright infringement separate from the fair-use defense applicable to the training itself. Judge Vince Chhabria's earlier denial of summary judgment on the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy issue kept the seeding theory alive for discovery and trial. Meta executive depositions, including the deposition of Mark Zuckerberg (completed December 17, 2024), have been completed through the fact-discovery period. In deposition snippets, Zuckerberg cited YouTube's battle to remove pirated content as a defense to Meta's own acquisition and use of pirated e-books, distinguishing between platforms that host user-generated content and platforms that directly acquire and store pirated material (Meta's posture). The seeding theory — that downloading and storing copyrighted material from shadow libraries is independently actionable — survived the *Bartz* settlement and remains the most viable plaintiff-side theory in cases where training-corpus formation involved torrenting. The discovery window closes in June, setting up trial-readiness briefing in Q3 2026.

## OpenAI MDL — 20M ChatGPT Logs in Plaintiff Hands, Pattern Analysis Underway

Judge Sidney Stein affirmed in January 2026 the magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the full 20 million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs in the multidistrict litigation (__[Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-must-turn-over-20-million-chatgpt-logs-judge-affirms)__). The publisher plaintiffs and co-plaintiffs have now received the entire dataset and are conducting discovery pattern-analysis to identify instances where ChatGPT outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted news content. The logs — anonymized but comprehensive — cover a broad range of user queries and ChatGPT responses, providing a granular record of what the model produces across the news, entertainment, reference, and commercial domains. This dataset is critical to the fourth-factor (market-harm) inquiry: if ChatGPT outputs directly compete with or substitute for news articles, that is strong evidence against fair use. The parties are working through the logistics of producing summaries and statistical analyses of the 20 million logs while maintaining privacy protections for the individual users who submitted the queries.

## AI Copyright Legislation — TRAIN, NO FAKES, CLEAR in Committee, TRUMP AMERICA AI as Counterweight

Committee activity on the TRAIN Act (training-data transparency), NO FAKES Act (digital-replica rights), and CLEAR Act (AI content labeling) has accelerated through May, with markup activity in House Judiciary and Senate Commerce. The TRAIN Act would create a federal mechanism allowing copyright holders to use subpoena power to determine if their work was used to train an AI model, modeled on internet-piracy discovery procedures. The NO FAKES Act would establish a federal property right protecting voice, image, and likeness from generative-AI deepfakes and clones without consent. The CLEAR Act would require companies to file notice with the Copyright Office detailing copyrighted works used in training datasets before public release, with retroactive application to existing models and a public database maintained by the Copyright Office. Simultaneously, the administration has pushed the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act as a competing framework that would preempt state AI laws and limit class-action remedies for AI training — a provision that would dramatically narrow developer liability exposure by capping damages or eliminating class actions altogether. The May markup activity is expected to clarify which statutory framework will prevail.

## Recommended Actions

Author-side and AI-developer counsel should treat the *Bartz* final-approval order as imminent at fifteen days post-hearing and pre-stage their respective communications now: claims administrators should confirm distribution-readiness and rightsholder-notice channels, while objector counsel should have any Rule 23(e) appellate-preservation papers prepared against the order's entry. Publisher and rightsholder counsel should treat the *Bartz* per-book pricing ($3,000 per book at the high end) as the operative settlement comparator and model damages calculations using that baseline across the *OpenAI* MDL, *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic*, *Disney v. Midjourney*, and any newly filed cases. Music-publishing counsel watching *Concord/UMG v. Anthropic* should expect the *Elsevier v. Meta* executive-knowledge allegations to reset settlement signaling in the music context, since the Elsevier complaint's detailed executive-authorization narrative materially strengthens the non-fair-use position. Plaintiff-side firms in AI training-data cases should align discovery on the seeding theory where shadow-library acquisition is alleged, since the fair-use defense does not reach the seeding question post-*Bartz* and the Elsevier complaint provides a granular template for executive-knowledge discovery. Companies developing generative-AI models should prepare compliance documentation for the contingent-liability scenarios presented by TRAIN Act subpoena procedures and CLEAR Act notice-filing requirements, modeling both creator-protection (current trajectory) and developer-preemption (TRUMP AMERICA AI passage) scenarios. News and academic publishers should analyze the 20 million ChatGPT logs through a market-substitution lens, identifying specific query-response patterns where ChatGPT competes with or substitutes for publisher content, as those patterns will anchor fourth-factor analysis in the *OpenAI* MDL and feed TRAIN Act discovery requests.
