## Theme: Thomson Reuters v. ROSS — the first appellate AI fair-use argument

### LinkedIn

Six days from now, a federal appeals court will do something no court has done before: review a fair-use ruling about training an AI model. Everyone watching the AI-copyright docket should have June 11 circled.

The case is Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence, No. 25-2153, in the Third Circuit. The district court first granted ROSS partial summary judgment on fair use, then reversed itself — holding that building a competing legal-research tool out of Westlaw headnotes was not "transformative" because its economic purpose was straightforward substitution.

The appeal turns on a single, consequential question: what does "transformative" mean when the use is machine training? ROSS argues the first fair-use factor asks an objective question about what the end product actually does — and that a trained model is a fundamentally new artifact, analytically distinct from the works it learned from. Thomson Reuters argues the controlling lens is market substitution: if your commercial use enables a competing product by extracting expressive content, it isn't transformative, full stop.

Both sides filed supplemental briefs last month on the Third Circuit's own UpCodes decision, which found fair use in copying legal standards that had been incorporated into law. ROSS reads it as confirming an objective purpose inquiry; Thomson Reuters reads it as a narrow access-to-law holding that doesn't reach commercial training on private editorial work.

Whatever the panel says about transformativeness won't stay in this case. It will propagate straight into the OpenAI MDL, the Elsevier v. Meta pleadings, and the Concord/UMG v. Anthropic cross-motions — each of which is litigating the same first-factor question. June 11 is the day the appellate standard starts to take shape.
