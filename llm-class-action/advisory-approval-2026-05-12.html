May 12, 2026
Analysis & Developments

With forty-eight hours remaining before the most consequential copyright hearing in the history of artificial intelligence, the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement has entered its final pre-hearing window under conditions that will require Judge Martínez-Olguín to navigate a uniquely complex set of Rule 23(e)(2) fairness factors: a 91.3% claim rate across 482,474 eligible works that has no precedent in class-action practice, five fully briefed objection categories that cut to the structural adequacy of the distribution plan, and an accelerating legislative campaign to declare by statute that AI training on copyrighted works does not constitute fair use — a determination that, if enacted, would retrospectively validate the settlement’s per-work valuation framework but simultaneously suggest that the $3,000 per-title compensation was a fraction of the liability exposure Anthropic would face under a regime where fair use is categorically unavailable. The hearing is set for 2:00 p.m. PT on May 14 in Courtroom 12 of the San Francisco Federal Courthouse, with Zoom access available to class members, and the court must make individualized findings on each of Professor Lea Victoria Bishop’s objections — most critically, the contention that group Copyright Office registrations have been collapsed into single “claimable works,” reducing per-author compensation by orders of magnitude where a single registration encompasses dozens of distinct titles, in apparent tension with Rule 23(e)(2)(D)’s requirement that the relief be adequate given the costs, risks, and delay of trial and appeal. (Authors Alliance; Authors Guild)

The legislative landscape surrounding AI copyright has shifted materially since the settlement was negotiated in September 2025, and that shift bears directly on the fairness-hearing calculus. Senator Blackburn’s TRUMP AMERICA AI Act — released as a discussion draft in March 2026 and the subject of an April roundtable with over twenty artists and Senators Blackburn and Welch — would amend the Copyright Act to declare that unauthorized reproduction or processing of copyrighted works for AI training does not constitute fair use, effectively overriding the pending judicial determinations in Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, the OpenAI MDL, and the Concord Music publisher action. The bill has generated growing bipartisan momentum, with formal support from the Recording Academy, SAG-AFTRA, and the Copyright Alliance, though it stands in direct tension with the Trump Administration’s own National AI Policy Framework, which recommended that Congress not take any actions that would impact the judiciary’s resolution of the fair-use question. (Blackburn Senate; Deadline; Latham & Watkins) The TRAIN Act — introduced by Representatives Dean and Moran — would separately empower copyright holders to subpoena AI developers for training-data records, with non-compliance triggering a rebuttable presumption that the developer copied the work, and the NO FAKES Act continues its bipartisan advance toward creating a federal property right in voice, image, and likeness. Taken together, the three bills would construct a legislative architecture that collapses Anthropic’s core defense — that training on lawfully obtained books is transformative and therefore protected — and any one of them reaching the President’s desk before the Bartz appeal window closes would materially alter the appellate posture for both objectors and class counsel.

The parallel docket continues to present the court with cross-cutting developments that will inevitably inform its fairness analysis. The Elsevier v. Meta publisher class action, now one week old, has produced the most damaging evidentiary showing of willfulness in any pending AI copyright case: the complaint alleges that CEO Mark Zuckerberg personally authorized and actively encouraged Meta to torrent 267 terabytes of pirated material from LibGen and Anna’s Archive after abandoning a $200 million licensing budget, with Meta publicly vowing to “fight this lawsuit aggressively.” (Variety; NPR) The willfulness evidence in Meta — CEO-level authorization of shadow-library downloads — strengthens the § 504(c) statutory-damages theory across all pending cases and provides Bartz objectors with a powerful comparative argument: if Meta’s exposure under the willfulness standard is potentially billions in statutory damages for conduct arguably more egregious than Anthropic’s, the $3,000 per-work Bartz settlement may appear even more conservative. Meanwhile, the Concord Music Group, UMG, and ABKCO cross-motions for partial summary judgment against Anthropic remain set for hearing on July 15 before Judge Lee, with the publishers’ 47-page statement of 218 undisputed facts and the RIAA/NMPA amicus brief arguing that Anthropic’s unlicensed copying of 499 musical compositions is “inexcusable” — a ruling against Anthropic on summary judgment would arrive while the Bartz settlement is still within its post-approval appeal window. (Music Business Worldwide; RIAA/NMPA Amicus)

The Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence oral argument at the Third Circuit on June 11 — coinciding with the Bartz distribution computation — will produce the first federal appellate consideration of whether AI training on copyrighted works constitutes fair use, with ROSS Intelligence’s Rule 28(j) letter drawing the panel’s attention to the Third Circuit’s own April 7 ASTM v. UpCodes decision on fair use in the context of AI-native legal technology. Norton Rose Fulbright’s comprehensive 2026 litigation survey confirms that over 70 federal copyright cases are now active against AI companies, more than doubling the approximately 30 cases at the end of 2024, with settlement negotiations in the OpenAI MDL reported as ongoing and the court-ordered production of 20 million ChatGPT output logs proceeding under Judge Stein’s January 5 order. (Chat GPT Is Eating the World; Norton Rose Fulbright) Internationally, the Getty Images appeal to the UK Court of Appeal — permission granted December 16, 2025 — will produce the most significant UK judicial consideration of AI training and copyright, potentially diverging from or reinforcing the emerging U.S. appellate framework on whether model weights constitute infringing copies, while the CJEU’s pending Like Company v. Google case (AG opinion expected September 3) will authoritatively test DSM Directive Article 4 TDM opt-outs across the European Union. (Chambers & Partners; Taylor Wessing)

Recommended Actions

Class members in the Bartz settlement should treat the next 48 hours as a final preparation window: verify claim status and payment information through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com, confirm that works filed under group Copyright Office registrations have been individually enumerated where required by the distribution methodology — the Bishop objection highlights that failure to enumerate can collapse forty-plus books into a single claimable work — and arrange to monitor the May 14 hearing via Zoom (log-in information available on page 2 of the court’s April 8 order) to assess the court’s treatment of the five principal objection themes, which will signal whether appellate challenges are likely and whether the June 11 distribution timeline will hold. Practitioners advising rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz class should reassess expected value of individual claims in light of both the Meta publisher complaint’s willfulness evidence and the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act’s proposed statutory elimination of fair use for AI training — if the bill advances, the litigation value of opt-out claims rises substantially because the primary defense available to AI developers would be foreclosed by statute. AI developers facing training-data litigation should conduct immediate audits of data-provenance documentation and internal licensing-decision communications — the Meta complaint demonstrates that discovery of abandoned-licensing communications and CEO-level authorization of shadow-library downloads will be a priority in every pending case — and should closely monitor the Concord Music summary judgment hearing (July 15), the Thomson Reuters oral argument (June 11), and the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act’s markup schedule as the three proceedings most likely to alter the legal landscape before the Andersen v. Stability AI trial begins on September 8, 2026.
