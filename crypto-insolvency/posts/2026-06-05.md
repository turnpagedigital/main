## Theme: A § 363 sale moves the assets, not the customer-property claims

### LinkedIn

A buyer is acquiring a bankrupt crypto lender's whole operation for $3.25 million. If you were a customer of that lender, here's the part that matters: the sale does not erase your claim.

On June 16, Chief Judge Colm Connolly will hear the § 363 sale-approval motion in the BlockFills Chapter 11 (In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings, D. Del.). Keyrock SA, a Brussels market maker, is the winning bidder for the client network, the trading technology, and the IP. The headline reads like an exit. For customers, it's a checkpoint.

Section 363 is a transfer of designated assets. It is not a discharge of trust-property or customer-property priority claims against the estate's residual cash and crypto. If your deposited assets were segregated and traceable — and the § 341 examination record from the May 27 Wilmington meeting is where that gets established — your claim runs against what's left in the estate, regardless of what Keyrock buys.

Two things decide your recovery, and neither is the purchase price. First, the assumption-of-liabilities schedule in the sale motion: does Keyrock take on any customer-deposit obligations, or are they left behind? Second, the Dominion Capital adversary proceeding — the one that froze 70.6 BTC under a TRO — which will set the segregation-versus-estate boundary for the affected sub-pool.

If you're a BlockFills customer, do one thing before June 16: file a proof of claim and characterize it as a customer-property priority claim, not a general unsecured claim. That single classification choice governs where you stand in the residual distribution.
