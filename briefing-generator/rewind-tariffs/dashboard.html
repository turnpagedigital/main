2. CAPE Pipeline — $86.3B Repaid as of July 10; Phase 3 Deployed on Schedule

CBP's July 13 court filing shows $86.3 billion in IEEPA refunds actually repaid to importers, with $121.75 billion total accepted for processing as of July 10, 2026.

An analysis by the Cato Institute found that the $104.29 billion approved as of June 29 covered only approximately 30% of all IEEPA-affected import entries, meaning the dollars processed so far are concentrated in a small number of large, high-value entries while many smaller importers remain in the queue.

Phase sequencing: CAPE Phase 1 launched April 20, 2026, covering refunds for unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation. Phase 2 launched June 29, 2026, covering entries flagged for reconciliation where the reconciliation entry is not yet on file.

CAPE Phase 3 addresses finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs. Programming for Phase 3 was confirmed ready in late July 2026 — meaning Phase 3 infrastructure should now be live as of this writing, though disbursement on finally liquidated entries for non-litigants remains legally blocked pending the Federal Circuit appeal.

Effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries are no longer accepted on CAPE Declarations; warehouse withdrawals continue to be accepted.

Effective June 29, 2026, CAPE accepts certain reconciliation-flagged entries (types 01, 02, and 06) where no reconciliation entry has been filed.

Per CBP court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid an estimated $166 billion across more than 53 million entries. At the current pace, the gap between accepted claims ($121.75B) and the full pool ($166B) — roughly $44 billion — reflects the contested finally-liquidated category, the government estimates at more than $30 billion, plus entries not yet submitted. Refunds include 6–7% statutory interest compounded daily; validate CBP's math against your own entry data before accepting a figure.

3. Amazon Q2 Earnings: $600M IEEPA Refund — Large-Importer Throughput Confirmed

On its Q2 earnings call on July 30, 2026, Amazon disclosed approximately $600 million in tariff refunds during the quarter, recorded in its North America segment. CFO Brian Olsavsky said that figure represents the significant majority of the refunds Amazon expects to receive.

The disclosure illuminates two structural points relevant to every importer's recovery posture. First, Olsavsky noted that Amazon received a "limited" refund amount because it worked to order and pre-position inventory in anticipation of the tariffs, and that Amazon is not the importer of record for the large majority of items sold in its store. Second, on pass-through: Amazon identified "a limited set of circumstances" where it can trace specific import charges passed to customers, and will proactively contact and automatically refund those customers — but for everything else, the money goes into pricing: "we'll utilize refunds to continue to invest in low prices for customers."

The broader corporate comparison is telling. Top logistics competitors UPS and FedEx have flagged nearly $6 billion in refunds they expect to pass along to customers. Retail giants including Walmart, The Home Depot, and Costco have all applied for refunds through CBP.

Apple reported separately that tariff refunds added 11 cents to quarterly earnings per share, a boost of approximately 5%.

The Amazon disclosure also reinforces the importer-of-record limitation: companies that were not the importer of record will likely have no direct recourse to seek refunds from CBP, but may have contractual or other claims against the importer of record.

4. Section 301 Forced-Labor Tariffs Live as of July 24 — Section 122 Expired on Schedule

The post-IEEPA tariff architecture completed its second transition. The new tariffs were announced around 5 p.m. ET on July 23, 2026, and took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24 — precisely when the Section 122 global surcharge under the Trade Act of 1974 expired, having served as the Trump Administration's temporary replacement for the IEEPA tariffs struck down by the Supreme Court.

The Section 301 forced-labor tariffs are either 10% for countries that USTR has determined have prohibitions on imports made with forced labor, or 12.5% for countries that have no such prohibitions.

Exemptions include products already subject to Section 232 tariffs, agricultural goods not produced domestically in sufficient quantities, and inputs used in U.S. manufacturing.

Section 122 expired and USTR's Section 301 replacement took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET July 24 — a two-tier 10%/12.5% tariff on roughly 60 economies, wider than the 46-country proposal. There was no gap between the two regimes.

The practical implications for refund planning: only tariffs paid under the invalidated IEEPA authority are eligible for refunds — duties paid under Section 122, Section 232, Section 301, or other authorities are not subject to the CIT's refund order. If the Section 122 surcharge litigation ultimately succeeds on appeal, a separate refund pool for February 24–July 23 Section 122 payments could open, but that remains speculative. Unlike IEEPA or Section 122, Section 301 tariffs carry no expiration date, and if they materialize at the scale the administration intends, they could approximate the breadth of the original IEEPA tariffs — on far more durable legal ground.
