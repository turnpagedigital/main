II. Lululemon — New Consumer Suit Filed July 1 (E.D. Mich. + New Action)

On July 1, 2026, Lululemon was accused of boosting prices in response to the Trump administration's global tariffs and then failing to refund customers when the U.S. Supreme Court invalidated the duties, becoming one of the latest household brand names to face such claims. This July 1 filing supplements the earlier Michigan action. For context: the initial Lululemon suit was filed March 27 in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, Neuman v. Lululemon USA Inc., No. 2:26-cv-11029, before Judge Stephen J. Murphy III, alleging unjust enrichment on the grounds that the company unfairly received a benefit at consumers' expense.

The "double recovery" theory at the heart of these suits is pointed: consumers were left to foot the bill when Lululemon raised prices in response to the tariffs, and now the company stands to obtain a significant refund through a lawsuit it filed against the federal government in the CIT.

The lawsuit against Lululemon includes four named plaintiffs and claims the class contains more than 100 people, with total damages exceeding $5 million; it is estimated that Lululemon paid approximately $240 million in tariffs, but those costs were passed down to consumers — the company announced it would raise prices in response to the tariffs.

Lululemon "has made no commitment to return any portion of its anticipated tariff refunds to the consumers who ultimately bore those costs."

The evidentiary record here is strong for plaintiffs at the pleading stage: the lawsuit pointed to Lululemon CFO Meghan Frank saying there would be "modest" price increases to mitigate the tariff impact, and CEO Calvin McDonald saying the company was being "strategic" on upping prices for some items because of the tariffs. That kind of on-the-record executive commentary reduces the pleading risk and could complicate any early motion to dismiss.

III. CAPE Pipeline — Phase 2 Live June 29; $40B+ Disbursed, $36B Gap Remains

The most consequential update for importers-of-record this week is operational: effective June 29, 2026, CAPE began accepting entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, 06) for which the reconciliation entry (entry type 09) has not been filed. This is Phase 2. CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas estimated Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.

As of the June 9, 2026 CIT hearing, the macro trajectory was: Phase 1 of CAPE had accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, and approximately $23 billion had been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund. The government's own projection was more aggressive: the government represented that more than $95 billion had been queued for refund through CAPE and, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.

The $36 billion gap — the difference between $166 billion collected and $130 billion addressable through Phases 1 and 2 — is the critical open question. Phase 3, targeted for late July 2026, will cover finally liquidated entries, and the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.

Under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE, dividing the importer universe into two groups — those who filed at the CIT and those who did not — and for importers in the latter category, the risk is no longer theoretical, as the government's current position is that such importers may wait indefinitely — and perhaps permanently — for refunds of finally liquidated entries.

One operational trap to flag: Framework Agreements with the EU, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland/Liechtenstein required that Column 1/General Duty be paid alongside IEEPA duties such that the combined rate equaled 15%; importers who reported the total 15% on a single line will not receive a refund of the full 15%, because a portion accounts for the Column 1/General Duty rate that remains in effect. Importers with entries in these frameworks need to re-examine their CAPE declarations line by line before the Phase 2 filing window closes.

Process timing: CBP advises that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review. At 60–90 days from a June 29 Phase 2 acceptance, that puts first Phase 2 payments in the late-August to late-September 2026 window — assuming clean submissions.

IV. Section 122 — 22 Days to Expiration; Federal Circuit Stay Holds

The Section 122 litigation posture has not materially shifted since June 11, but the proximity of the July 24 expiration makes this the most time-sensitive structural issue in the tariff landscape.

To restate where things stand: on May 7, 2026, the U.S. Court of International Trade held that Proclamation 11012, which imposed a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, was invalid because the proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficits" required by the statute.

The CIT majority — comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly — held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted. But: on June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motion to stay the lower court's injunction, allowing the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 import surcharges.

The CAFC's stay applies to the named plaintiffs — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — meaning the 10% surcharge continues to be collected from all importers, including those who were party to the original CIT ruling, throughout the appeals process.

The permanent injunction from the CIT extends only to the three importer plaintiffs who were found to have standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.; all other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.

Critically, the CAPE refund mechanism covers only IEEPA tariffs; there is currently no administrative refund channel for the Section 122 surcharge. An importer seeking a refund of Section 122 duties paid since February 24, 2026 must file its own CIT action under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) — and must do so before liquidation of its entries becomes final. For lower-volume importers and those unburdened with cash-flow concerns for the duration, the best course of action may be to pay the Section 122 duties where no exemption is available, to monitor appellate proceedings, and to maintain records for use in a future claims system.

One important planning note: the administration reportedly intends for its wide-ranging Section 301 investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs. That creates a potential seamless handoff — the 10% Section 122 surcharge lapses July 24; a new § 301-based tariff regime could activate in the same window.

V. USTR Section 301 / Germany — Post-Section 122 Successor Framework Takes Shape

On June 18, 2026, USTR Jamieson Greer self-initiated a Section 301 investigation under the Trade Act of 1974 targeting Germany's "persistent underpayment" for innovative pharmaceutical products, citing a 3.9x price disparity between U.S. and German consumers for brand-name drugs. The docket numbers are USTR-2026-0463 and USTR-2026-0464, adding a second trade law instrument to the pharmaceutical tariffs proclaimed on April 2, 2026 under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 — signaling a multistatute strategy to pressure trading partners to raise reimbursement prices.

The Section 301 investigation targets two specific German policies: confidentiality-conditioned discounts requiring a mandatory 9% price reduction, and a 2026 draft law proposing variable rebates on patented medicines that industry estimates could reach 20% by 2030.

The Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs — 100% on patented pharmaceutical products and ingredients — are scheduled to take effect July 31 for a list of 17 major pharmaceutical companies, including Eli Lilly and Co., Pfizer, and Novo Nordisk, with other companies facing the tariff starting September 29.

Why does this matter for the IEEPA/Section 122 refund ecosystem? Because together, the Section 232 tariffs of up to 100% on imported pharmaceuticals and the new Section 301 investigation form a coordinated two-statute strategy — and the administration's apparent intention is that successor § 301 determinations conclude before July 24 to fill the Section 122 void. Importers who have been paying 10% Section 122 duties on pharmaceutical goods may find themselves simultaneously processing an IEEPA refund, running out the Section 122 clock, and facing a new tariff regime at an entirely different rate. Written comments and requests to appear at the September 22, 2026 hearing are due August 10, 2026.

Recommended Actions

Importers of record with IEEPA exposure should confirm Phase 2 CAPE declarations are filed — or in final preparation — now: CAPE Phase 2, live as of June 29, accepts reconciliation-flagged entries (types 01, 02, 06) for which the reconciliation (Type 09) entry has not been filed, limited to unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation. Entries approaching the 80-day liquidation boundary are time-critical; a declaration filed after that window closes is ineligible for Phase 2 and will require CIT litigation for Phase 3 recovery. Any importer with Framework Agreement entries (EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland/Liechtenstein) must audit CAPE declarations at the line level — the Column 1/General Duty rate is not refundable and overstated CAPE claims carry false-certification exposure under 28 U.S.C. § 1746. On Section 122: entries that have paid the 10% surcharge since February 24 and face imminent liquidation should be reviewed for preservation of protest rights or CIT standing — there is no administrative CAPE channel for those duties, and the surcharge expires July 24 by statute without any guaranteed refund mechanism for non-plaintiffs. Pharmaceutical importers should note that the Section 232 100% tariff on patented drugs triggers July 31 for the named 17 companies; that rate change resets the IEEPA refund math for those product lines and will require separate duty-drawback analysis under 19 U.S.C. § 1313 where applicable. On the consumer litigation front, any retailer that (a) publicly attributed price increases to IEEPA tariffs and (b) is an importer of record eligible for CAPE refunds should engage counsel now on the "double recovery" litigation exposure before class certification briefing advances in any of the pending W.D. Wash. or E.D. Mich. actions.
