# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Thursday, July 30, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's principal delta:** The Section 122 surcharge hard-expired at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24, 2026, exactly as the statute required, and the administration moved simultaneously — without a single day's gap — to replace it with a new global tariff regime under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. 
The new tariffs range from 10% to 12.5% on imports from 60 countries accounting for roughly 99.4% of U.S. imports, taking effect at 12:01 a.m. ET Friday and replacing the temporary 10% levy imposed under § 122, which expired the same day.
 Two lawsuits challenging the Section 301 tariffs were filed at the CIT within hours of the new rates going live. The IEEPA refund pipeline, meanwhile, reached a significant interim milestone: as of the July 10 court declaration, CBP has paid back more than half the $166 billion pool, and CAPE Phase 3 — covering finally liquidated entries — was targeted for launch this week. The net result is a three-front litigation landscape: (1) the Section 301 challenges now opening at the CIT; (2) the Section 122 appeal pending before the Federal Circuit; and (3) the IEEPA refund administration before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton at the CIT, with the government still contesting the scope of Phase 3 recovery.

### I. Section 301 Tariffs: Two CIT Challenges Filed July 24


On July 24, 2026, the Liberty Justice Center filed suit at the U.S. Court of International Trade on behalf of Burlap & Barrel and Collective Horology, challenging the administration's Section 301 tariffs and arguing that USTR failed to satisfy the statutory requirements Congress established before tariffs may be imposed under that authority.
 The case caption is *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. et al. v. Greer et al.*, 
CIT No. 26-03345.



The complaint presses three grounds: (1) the action exceeds § 301's actual statutory scope; (2) it reflects arbitrary and predetermined decision-making rather than reasoned analysis; and (3) if allowed to stand, it would hand the executive a degree of unchecked tariff power the Constitution reserves for Congress.
 The Liberty Justice Center's specific statutory argument is concrete: 
Section 301 tariffs have historically been targeted to address specific nations and industries, and Trump's broad-brush approach has no historical precedent; LJC attorney Jeffrey Schwab stated that § 301 does not include authority "to tax substantially all imports from substantially all countries at preestablished rates."



The complaint asks the court to declare the tariffs unlawful, prevent their enforcement, and preserve complete relief for plaintiffs and the proposed class for affected entries, including refunds of tariffs already paid, with interest.


A parallel action was filed the same day: 
*Learning Resources v. United States*, CIT No. 26-03347, filed July 24, 2026, advancing similar arguments — that the tariffs are arbitrary and capricious, fail to meet the requirement for individualized findings, and violate the nondelegation doctrine — though it is not structured as a class action.


The critical threshold question in both cases is whether § 301's country-specific, practice-specific structure can bear a near-universal tariff regime. 
Unlike IEEPA or the temporary global tariff authority, Section 301 has been used regularly by past presidents
 — the administration's core durability argument — but that prior usage involved targeted, bilateral actions, not 60-country simultaneous levies. 
The practical distinction is that Section 301 actually says "duties," so the government isn't fighting on the ground it lost on in February
 when the Supreme Court invalidated the IEEPA tariffs. 
As one trade attorney has already noted by analogy to Section 122: "Look at Section 122: the trade court struck it down May 7, the government appealed May 8, and the Federal Circuit stayed the ruling within days."
 The same emergency-stay playbook is nearly certain to deploy here, meaning collection of Section 301 duties will continue for months regardless of initial trial-court rulings.


The administration imposed the new duties under Section 301 following a probe into the alleged failure of around 60 economies to prevent forced labor in their supply chains to the detriment of American workers.
 
The complaint alleges that USTR imposed near-uniform tariffs across 60 economies without adequately establishing, for each, the specific governmental act or policy being challenged, how it burdens U.S. commerce, or how the tariffs would eliminate the identified practice.

### II. Section 122 Appeal: Federal Circuit Merits Briefing Underway

The Section 122 litigation — *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805 (Fed. Cir.) — is now in merits briefing following the Federal Circuit's June 11, 2026 stay.


On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805), consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of Proclamation No. 11012 and § 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132). While the appeal proceeds, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 balance-of-payments tariffs as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — pending further order of the Federal Circuit.


The stay turned on three of the four *Nken v. Holder* factors favoring the government. 
The panel found that the government made a sufficient showing of likely success on the merits but expressly declined to adopt any final interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" at this stage; the court found the legislative history cited by the government and by Judge Stanceu's dissent sufficient to call into question the CIT majority's three-measure framework.


The underlying CIT ruling, entered May 7, 2026 (Slip Op. 26-47), 
held that Proclamation 11012, which imposed a 10% import surcharge under § 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, was invalid because the proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficits" required by the statute.
 
The CIT issued a permanent injunction limited to the three direct-importer plaintiffs — Burlap and Barrel, Basic Fun, and the State of Washington — and declined to enter a universal injunction; the judgment declared Proclamation 11012 "invalid as contrary to law" and ordered Section 122 surcharges paid by those plaintiffs to be "refunded with interest as provided by law."


The practical effect of the July 24 expiration of the Section 122 surcharge is significant: the levy is now dead by its own 150-day statutory clock regardless of how the Federal Circuit rules on the merits. 
Although the Federal Circuit explained that it did not "state a final conclusion as to the correctness of the CIT's judgment," the court did find that "the federal government has made a sufficient showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits," leaving the opportunity for refunds uncertain pending affirmance; if the CAFC sustains the CIT's initial opinion, however, importers should be prepared to consider filing a complaint at the CIT to seek refunds.
 Any importer that paid Section 122 duties during the February 24–July 24, 2026 window and has not yet filed a protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or a summons at the CIT should treat the Federal Circuit's merits schedule as a hard planning deadline.

### III. IEEPA CAPE Pipeline: $86.3B Paid, Phase 3 Arriving

The IEEPA refund administration before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton at the CIT (*V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066) has reached a material inflection point.


As of CBP's July 10, 2026 court declaration, $86.3 billion has been repaid to importers (including statutory interest), out of roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties collected; $121.75 billion in claims have been accepted for processing, and June 2026 was the largest single month at $49.1 billion paid.
 That pace is remarkable by any measure: 
in all of the last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


Phase 3 — the tranche covering finally liquidated entries — was confirmed to be ready by end of July 2026 per CBP testimony. 
Phase 3 of the CAPE tariff refund tool was targeted to be ready by the end of July; CBP confirmed that timeline at a June 9, 2026 hearing before Senior Judge Eaton, with Susan Thomas, executive assistant commissioner for CBP's trade office, delivering the update.
 Separately, 
on July 15, 2026, the CIT ordered that CBP will be directed to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries ahead of CAPE Phase 3, with the order to be entered across roughly 3,700 IEEPA cases before the court.


The government's contest of Phase 3 scope, however, is the critical constraint: 
the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 This position, if sustained, carves out the overwhelming majority of the ~330,000-importer universe — most of whom participated in Phase 1 via CAPE Declarations rather than through litigation. That dispute is live before Judge Eaton and is the single most consequential pending issue in the refund pipeline for non-plaintiff importers. The government's fiscal rationale is evident: 
June's $49.1 billion in refund payments was more than double the $23.6 billion collected in new tariff revenue the same month, making the tariff program a net cash outflow and driving the first widening of the FY2026 deficit — $1.37 trillion through nine months, approximately 2% wider than 2025.

### IV. The Refund Regime in Context: IEEPA Closed, § 122 Contested, § 301 Opening


On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed the Federal Circuit's en banc ruling that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs.
 
The decision was 6-3, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts.
 The mandate issued from the Federal Circuit on March 2, 2026. That ruling is now final. The IEEPA refund window is fixed: duties paid from the first IEEPA tariff order through February 20, 2026 are refundable through CAPE (subject to the Phase 3/litigation contest for finally liquidated entries). Section 232 tariffs — steel, aluminum, copper, autos — were not challenged under IEEPA and are unaffected.

The administration's strategy is now legible: each time a tariff authority falls, it deploys the next available statute. IEEPA fell in February; 
Trump responded by imposing a new temporary 10% global tariff under different legal authority; those tariffs, like the IEEPA tariffs, were also imposed under a section of the law that no previous president had used to impose tariffs, and were also ruled illegal by the U.S. trade court; the Trump administration appealed that ruling.
 Now Section 122 is expired by its own terms and Section 301 is the replacement vehicle. The legal durability of § 301 is genuinely different — it expressly authorizes "duties" — but the scope question (whether it can cover 99.4% of all U.S. imports simultaneously) has never been litigated in this posture, and the Liberty Justice Center's track record in this cycle gives the challenge more institutional weight than a typical first-filing.

For importers: the Section 301 tariffs are being collected now and will continue to be collected while any challenge moves through the CIT and the Federal Circuit. The same administrative-stay mechanism that protected the § 122 collection posture will almost certainly be invoked here. Budget planning and contract force-majeure analysis should proceed on the assumption that 10–12.5% Section 301 duties are the operative rate for 2026–2027.

## Recommended Actions

GCs and CFOs with Section 301 exposure should file or engage counsel to file protective CIT summonses and begin documenting per-country, per-entry harm now — both cases (*Burlap & Barrel*, CIT No. 26-03345; *Learning Resources*, CIT No. 26-03347) are styled as class actions or parallel proceedings, but protective filings under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) preserve independent refund rights if the class structure does not ultimately cover your entry profile. For IEEPA Phase 3: if your company paid IEEPA duties on finally liquidated entries and has not filed a CIT complaint, that is the most urgent operational item — the government's stated position is that Phase 3 refunds flow only to CIT litigants; confirm with customs counsel whether you are inside an existing proceeding or need to file independently ahead of any Phase 3 deadline set by Judge Eaton's July 15 reliquidation order. For Section 122 exposure on entries from February 24–July 24, 2026: file § 1514 protests with CBP to preserve refund rights pending the Federal Circuit's merits decision in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805); the Federal Circuit stay confirmed refund-with-interest is an adequate remedy if the CIT ruling is eventually affirmed, so the protest preserves that optionality at low cost. Drawback under 19 U.S.C. § 1313 remains available for qualifying exported goods — review whether any IEEPA duties paid during the refund-eligible window are also recoverable through this parallel mechanism.
