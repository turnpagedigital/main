# ⚖️ Tariff Refunds | Tuesday, August 04, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Two developments broke in the last 24 hours that carry direct implications for the refund pipeline and the durability of the Section 301 replacement tariff regime: (1) a 25-state coalition filed suit at the CIT Monday challenging the July 23 Section 301 forced-labor tariffs as an unlawful attempt to reconstruct the IEEPA tariff program the Supreme Court struck down in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (Feb. 20, 2026); and (2) today — August 4 — is the CBP-court deadline for CBP's Phase 3 CAPE progress report, a filing that will define whether finally-liquidated-entry refunds are flowing to the ~4,000 plaintiff importers who sued at the CIT. Both events land on the same day, making this the busiest refund-calendar date since the CIT's July 17 Phase 3 reliquidation order.

---

### 1. 25-State Section 301 Challenge — CIT (Filed August 4, 2026)

**New action, direct refund relevance.** 
A coalition of 25 states filed a lawsuit Monday against the Trump administration over sweeping new tariffs imposed on 60 trading partners, arguing the duties are an illegal attempt to revive a tariff regime the Supreme Court struck down.
 
New York Attorney General Letitia James and Governor Kathy Hochul announced the filing in the U.S. Court of International Trade, where the states are asking a judge to declare the tariffs unlawful, halt their collection, and **order refunds of duties already paid**.


The statutory theory differs from the IEEPA litigation but the refund ask is structurally identical. 
The administration imposed the tariffs on July 23 under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, citing the failure of 59 countries and the European Union to prevent goods produced with forced labor from entering their supply chains.
 
The tariffs of 10% or 12.5% cover countries that together account for 99.4% of U.S. imports.


The complaint raises three interlocking vulnerabilities. First, procedural APA defects: 
the lawsuit argues the administration violated Section 301's procedural requirements by completing investigations into 60 economies in roughly two and a half months, bypassing required country-specific consultations, and setting tariff rates with no explanation of how they would reduce forced-labor practices — and also alleges violations of the Administrative Procedure Act.
 Second, pretext: 
the states' complaint argued that the new tariffs used "forced labor" as a pretext to re-impose the tariffs that had already been ruled illegal in court.
 Third, internal contradiction: 
the states point to internal contradictions in the tariffs themselves — the administration's investigation cited frozen beef from Brazil as one of only three products linked to forced labor, yet exempted it from the new duties.


The Section 301 APA angle is procedurally distinct from the IEEPA constitutional challenge. 
Section 301 grants authority not to the President but to an agency — USTR — and the Federal Circuit has held that Section 301 actions are subject to judicial review under the standard prescribed by the Administrative Procedure Act, in contrast to presidential actions, which are not subject to APA requirements.
 That distinction cuts both ways: USTR gets Chevron-style deference on findings of fact, but *is* constrained by APA notice-and-comment and reasoned-explanation requirements — precisely the ground the states are contesting.

**Timeline context.** 
On June 2, 2026, USTR proposed tariffs of 10% or 12.5% on imports from all 60 trading partners in the Forced Labor Investigation.
 
USTR received and reviewed over 1,600 written comments on the proposed responsive action, and from July 7 to July 9 held public hearings at which over 100 witnesses provided testimony.
 The states' "two and a half months" procedural objection maps directly onto that compressed March-to-July timeline.

**Refund posture.** If the CIT enjoins these tariffs — consistent with its prior IEEPA and Section 122 holdings — the refund demand swells beyond the $166 billion IEEPA universe. Importers paying these new 10%/12.5% duties on entries dated July 23 forward would acquire a fresh protest right under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 and, if a CIT injunction issues, a direct reliquidation claim. Given the CIT's speed in the IEEPA pipeline, the litigation timetable for a preliminary injunction motion could compress to 60–90 days. ### 2. CAPE Phase 3 — CBP Progress Report Due Today (August 4, 2026)

**Scheduled milestone, real-time significance.** 
On July 17, 2026, CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued an order directing CBP to "reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, any and all of Plaintiffs' entries that have been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which Plaintiffs made estimated deposits pursuant to IEEPA."
 That order followed the transfer to Judge Eaton's docket of more than 3,700 cases seeking IEEPA duty refunds. 
CBP is required to file a report on the progress of the CAPE for these "Phase 3" entries on August 4, 2026.


Today's filing is the first public accounting of whether Phase 3 reliquidations are actually processing for finally-liquidated entries — the category most at risk of permanent refund loss without litigation. As of the most recent data point published before today: 
a July 15, 2026 CIT order recorded that, as of July 13, roughly $121.75 billion in potential and certified refunds had been accepted into CAPE processing, and approximately $86.3 billion of that total — including duties and interest — had already been transmitted to Treasury.


Phase 3 is structurally the most contested segment of the refund pipeline. 
The government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT; approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who filed refund lawsuits are expected to have all IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined; but under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE.


That position has been contested. 
CBP stated that, unless an importer had obtained its own CIT judgment, the agency lacked the ability to issue IEEPA duty refunds even though the Supreme Court had ruled them unlawful; on May 26, 2026, CBP provided an update to the CIT on current progress; Judge Eaton promptly denied CBP's motion, and the government filed an appeal on June 2, 2026.
 Whether today's progress report reflects compliance with Judge Eaton's July 17 order — or signals further government resistance — is the operative question. ### Standing Context (No New Movement)

The broader refund infrastructure remains as last reported: 
approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 
CBP has indicated that valid IEEPA refunds will generally be issued within 60 to 90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.
 No new CIT orders in *Atmus Filtration*, *Euro-Notions*, *Burlap and Barrel*, or *State of Oregon v. Trump* (the Section 122 docket) have been confirmed in the last 24 hours; those dockets carry no delta today.
