## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: The 28-Day Clock Is Now the Governing Fact

The single most consequential number on the board today is **28 days**. 
Presidential Proclamation No. 11012 — the 10% universal import surcharge imposed under 19 U.S.C. § 2132 (Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974) — is set to expire at 12:01 a.m. EST on July 24, 2026, 150 days after it took effect on February 24, 2026.
 That statutory hard-stop has now become the organizing deadline for every competing interest in this landscape: the USTR's Section 301 investigation race, the Federal Circuit's appeal of the Section 122 invalidation, the CBP CAPE refund pipeline for invalidated IEEPA duties, and the broader question of what tariff architecture governs American imports on July 25. The three-track dynamic — IEEPA refunds processing, Section 122 on judicial life support, Section 301 approaching from the rear — is now in its final compression phase.

---

### Track 1: Section 122 — Federal Circuit Stay in Place, Merits Brief Due; Surcharge Expires Before a Decision Is Likely


On June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805 — consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs imposed under Proclamation No. 11012 and Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. While the appeal proceeds, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 tariffs as to the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — the three parties covered by the underlying injunctions.


The backstory: 
On May 7, 2026, the CIT ruled, in a 2–1 opinion in *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* and *State of Oregon v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606, that Proclamation 11012 — which imposed a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122 — was invalid because the proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficits" required by the statute.
 The majority consisted of 
Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly, who held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974.
 
The Court held that Section 122's reference to a "balance-of-payments deficit" is limited to three recognized measures: (1) liquidity, (2) official settlements, and (3) basic balance.
 
Judge Timothy Stanceu dissented, arguing that BEA statistics on related measures could reasonably support a balance-of-payments finding, and that the majority erred procedurally by granting summary judgment without giving the government an opportunity to contest the plaintiffs' factual assertions.


The injunction itself was narrow. 
The permanent injunction issued against the government's collection of Section 122 tariffs extends only to three importer plaintiffs found to have standing — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.
 
On May 20, 2026, the CIT denied the government's separate motion for a stay of enforcement of its judgment pending appeal; nevertheless, the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remains in place.



In granting the stay on June 11, the Federal Circuit found that the government showed a sufficient likelihood of success on appeal and that the CIT's interpretation of Section 122 may be incorrect. The court also found that the government would face irreparable harm without a stay, including trade disruption concerns and difficulties in administering tariff refunds if the injunction remained in place.



The consolidated appeals (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) will proceed before a merits panel, which will address the proper interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" under Section 122, the nondelegation arguments raised by the private plaintiffs, and whether Proclamation No. 11012 was validly issued.
 The practical reality is that the Section 122 surcharge will almost certainly expire under its own 150-day statutory clock before the Federal Circuit issues a merits ruling. 
The Federal Circuit will review the CIT's decision, potentially en banc, with possible Supreme Court review thereafter.
 That timeline points well past July 24. For non-plaintiff importers, this means: 
entries must continue to include Section 122 duties unless and until further court action changes enforcement. For non-plaintiff importers, there is no automatic refund mechanism at this stage. Any potential refunds depend on the final outcome of the appeal and whether relief is extended beyond the named plaintiffs.


One structural point worth tracking: 
importers have paid an estimated $25 billion in Section 122 duties since February 24.
 That figure creates a significant refund contingent liability for the government if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT — a proceeding that, unlike the Section 122 expiration, has no hard deadline.

### Track 2: Section 301 — The Replacement Architecture Closing on July 24

The administration's legislative answer to both the IEEPA invalidation and the Section 122 vulnerability is a pair of Section 301 investigations launched in March 2026. 
On March 11, 2026, USTR initiated investigations under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 into "structural excess capacity or production in certain manufacturing sectors" of China, the EU, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan, and India.
 
The Trump Administration also initiated, on approximately March 12, a second substantial Section 301 investigation targeting 60 countries for forced labor enforcement failures.



July 24, 2026 is the unofficial target date for completion of the investigations and remedy determinations, according to USTR and President Trump. USTR has stated that it intends to conduct these investigations on an expedited basis, aiming to complete the process and be prepared to impose tariffs by around July 24, 2026.
 Ambassador Greer has confirmed this timeline publicly. 
The administration reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs. It is therefore possible that the Section 122 tariffs will be effectively replaced by Section 301 tariffs that approximate the scale and scope of the previous IEEPA tariffs, with no limit on their duration.


The legal distinction matters enormously for refund math. 
Unlike Section 122, Section 301 does not impose comparable limits on the duration or magnitude of tariffs. Stakeholders therefore should prepare for the possibility of higher tariffs that may remain in place for an extended period.
 
Section 301 tariffs, once imposed following a completed investigation, have neither expiry dates nor rate caps. The first Trump administration imposed Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods in 2018 — those tariffs are still in force in 2026.
 The strategic logic is clear: Section 301 is litigation-resistant in a way that IEEPA was not, courts having largely rejected prior attempts to limit its scope.


Given the intense judicial scrutiny applied to both the IEEPA tariffs and Section 122 tariffs, USTR is expected to proceed carefully to ensure that all substantive and procedural requirements are satisfied before imposing new Section 301 tariffs.
 The comment and hearing record closed in May; tariff action could follow within the next several weeks. Importers should treat July 24 not as a relief date but as a potential reset to higher, permanent, country-specific duties.

### Track 3: IEEPA Refunds — CAPE Pipeline and the $175 Billion Reckoning

The IEEPA refund process remains the most immediate cash-recovery opportunity and the most administratively underdeveloped one. 
The scale is unprecedented. Penn-Wharton Budget Model economists estimate that IEEPA-based tariff collections total approximately $175 billion to $179 billion.
 
The Supreme Court on February 20, 2026, held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's August 2025 ruling.
 
Following the ruling, the government ceased assessing and collecting importer deposits of IEEPA tariffs as of February 24, 2026.



Since the Court issued its ruling, the CIT has ordered CBP to commence the process of refunding IEEPA tariffs to importers. In response to the CIT's order, CBP has stated that it is developing a new automated system called the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) to process refund requests.
 The CAPE system represents the primary administrative channel for mass refund claims. The critical distinction — already well-established — is between unliquidated entries (where CBP can adjust without further protest) and liquidated entries (which require a formal protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or a civil action under 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i) before the CIT). 
Secretary of the Treasury Bessent previously testified to Congress in September 2025 that invalidation of tariffs would require refunds, though in the wake of the decision, implied that if tariffs were to be refunded, it would be up to the lower courts on remand — not Treasury — to decide how they would be paid.
 That posture has not materially changed.


Disputes over the allocation of IEEPA refunds are already emerging among importers of record, downstream purchasers, and end-users, including class actions seeking to recover pass-through tariffs. Similar disputes are conceivable with respect to Section 122 refunds.
 Supply-chain participants who absorbed rather than invoiced duty costs should be reviewing contractual allocation language now.


Bloomberg News reported that by the end of February 2026, over 2,000 lawsuits had been filed by many companies — before and after the decision — to recover the tariffs that were collected.
 Each of those dockets is now a potential CAPE touchpoint or a Section 1581(i) action tracking through the CIT.

### Background: The Governing Legal Architecture at a Glance


In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, argued November 5, 2025, and decided February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's August 2025 ruling.
 The Court's holding was textual: 
the Court concluded that IEEPA's grant of authority to "regulate … importation" does not include the power to impose tariffs, emphasizing that the power to impose tariffs is "very clearly … a branch of the taxing power" reserved for Congress under Article I of the U.S. Constitution.
 
The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump Administration that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.



Section 122 has never before been invoked, and it caps tariffs at 15% for a maximum of 150 days unless Congress votes to extend them.
 The administration's pivot to Section 122 on the same day as the Supreme Court ruling was immediate; 
within hours of the decision, President Trump signed a proclamation imposing a new 10% global tariff under Section 122, effective February 24, 2026.
 
Secretary of the Treasury Bessent stated that combining Section 122, Section 232, and Section 301 tariffs "will result in virtually unchanged tariff revenue in 2026," signaling the administration's resolve to find alternative avenues to reimpose equivalent duty levels.

## Recommended Actions

For importers, claimants, and counsel: the next 28 days are asymmetric. July 24 terminates the Section 122 surcharge under its statutory clock — but the Federal Circuit stay means named plaintiffs in *Burlap and Barrel* and *Oregon v. United States* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) will not receive refunds before that date, and non-plaintiff importers remain fully exposed in the interim; any importer that has not yet filed its own CIT action to secure injunctive relief and refund rights under *Slip Op. 26-47* should assess whether the cost of that litigation is justified by Section 122 duty exposure before the surcharge's natural expiration moots the injunction question. On the IEEPA side, every importer with unliquidated IEEPA entries should confirm their status in the CBP CAPE system immediately — the distinction between unliquidated entries (administrative refund) and liquidated entries (requiring a timely § 1514 protest or § 1581(i) CIT action) determines whether recovery is administrative or litigated; the protest clock runs from liquidation and will not be tolled by goodwill. On the Section 301 front, importers sourcing from any of the 16 excess-capacity economies or 60 forced-labor-risk countries named in the March 11–12 USTR investigations should model their landed-cost exposure under scenario-specific Section 301 rate structures — the rates will be country- and sector-specific, not the uniform 10% floor currently in place under Section 122, and unlike IEEPA or Section 122, Section 301 tariffs carry no statutory expiration and no rate ceiling, making them potentially more durable and more costly than anything that preceded them.
