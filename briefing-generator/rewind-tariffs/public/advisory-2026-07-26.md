## Analysis & Developments

**The Section 122 surcharge is now dead; the Section 301 regime is live; and the IEEPA refund pipeline has entered Phase 3 territory — all within the last 72 hours. Three separate tracks, each with distinct consequences for importer recovery and compliance exposure, moved simultaneously this week.**

### I. Section 122 Expiration + Section 301 Succession: The Rate Transition That Happened Friday


The 10 percent global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) expired by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — exactly 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 
Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation was pending. Absent congressional action, the surcharge terminated on its own terms.


The administration closed the gap in real time. 
The Trump administration announced a new round of tariffs up to 12.5% on 60 trading partners on Thursday, preventing a lapse in import taxes as the temporary tariff program expired. The move shows the White House's determination to preserve its tariff agenda by turning to new trade laws as earlier authorities expire or face legal challenges. The new tariffs took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET on Friday, precisely when the temporary duties ended.


The legal authority underlying the replacement is materially different from Section 122. 
The tariffs stem from an investigation launched by U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer in March under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 into whether trading partners had failed to effectively block goods made with forced labor.
 
The finding of wrongdoing followed an investigation initiated by the Trump administration under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which permits levies imposed in response to an adverse trade policy taken up by another country.


The rate architecture matters. 
As the temporary 10 percent tariff expired at midnight, the United States replaced it with new duties under Section 301 ranging from 10 percent to 12.5 percent, according to USTR. China, Taiwan, Australia, and the European Union now face the higher 12.5 percent tariff, while Canada, Mexico, the United Kingdom, and India face the 10 percent tariff.
 
The tariffs apply to 60 trade partners and cover 99.4% of U.S. trade, the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative said in a fact sheet.
 Critically, 
the new tariffs would not "stack" on top of existing import taxes on steel and aluminum, known as Section 232 duties, that Trump imposed last year on national security grounds.


The structural shift has lasting consequences for refund claimants and importers modeling future duty exposure. 
Two structural differences matter more than the 2.5-point rate differential. Section 301 has no statutory rate cap and no time limit — it does not sunset in 150 days, it does not need Congress, and rates can be raised by product list later. Importers who treated the 10% as temporary pain should plan for the replacement as the durable baseline.
 
In March, the Trump administration announced a separate Section 301 investigation centered on concerns about excess manufacturing capacity by 16 economies. That probe has yet to be finalized.
 Higher rates may follow.

### II. The Section 122 Federal Circuit Appeal: *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805)

The Section 122 sunset did not moot the pending appellate litigation — and that distinction is consequential for any importer preserving a refund claim.


On May 7, 2026, the U.S. Court of International Trade held that Proclamation 11012, which imposed a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, was invalid because the proclamation did not identify the type of balance-of-payments deficit required by the statute.
 
The immediate relief was limited to the successful plaintiffs: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, and Basic Fun.
 
The government appealed on May 8, 2026, to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit; on May 12, the Federal Circuit granted an administrative stay suspending enforcement of the permanent injunction and consolidated the appeals from both the State of Oregon and Burlap and Barrel, Inc. cases.



On June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805), consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs imposed under Proclamation No. 11012 and Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. While the appeal proceeds on the merits, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 "balance-of-payments" tariffs as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions, including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc., pending further order of the Federal Circuit.



The consolidated appeals (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) will now proceed before a merits panel, which will address the proper interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" under Section 122, the nondelegation arguments raised by the private plaintiffs, and whether Proclamation No. 11012 was validly issued.



The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality. The Court of International Trade held the surcharge unlawful, and the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling pending the government's appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, so CBP continued to collect the duty on entries through July 23. The July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period; the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the appeal.


This is the IEEPA pattern repeating. If the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's ruling that Section 122 was unlawfully invoked, the refund question for entries made between February 24 and July 23, 2026 — five months of 10-to-15 percent surcharge on the near-totality of U.S. imports — will be live and large. Importers should be preserving protest rights under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 and filing post-summary corrections on unliquidated entries now. The 180-day protest clock is running on February and March entries.

On July 24, 2026 — the day the surcharge expired — Burlap and Barrel filed a new complaint at the CIT, 
challenging the Section 122 tariff under the Trade Act of 1974, which the court has also held unlawful, with that decision currently on appeal at the Federal Circuit.
 The filing signals that private plaintiffs intend to preserve and extend their refund claims beyond the original three-plaintiff universe, even as the statutory surcharge has lapsed.

### III. IEEPA Refund Pipeline: CAPE Phase 3 Launch + Lead Case Transition

The IEEPA refund program entered its most consequential phase this week as CIT Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton issued the Phase 3 reliquidation order and the lead case changed hands for the second time in four months.

**Phase 3 Reliquidation Order — July 17, 2026.** 
On July 17, 2026, CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued an order directing U.S. Customs and Border Protection to "reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, any and all of Plaintiffs' entries that have been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which Plaintiffs made estimated deposits pursuant to IEEPA." This order follows the recent transfer to Judge Eaton's docket of more than 3,700 cases filed with the CIT seeking refunds of amounts deposited as IEEPA duties ruled unconstitutional by the U.S. Supreme Court.
 
CBP is next required to file a report on the progress of CAPE for these "Phase 3" entries on August 4, 2026.


**Lead Case Transition: Euro-Notions Out, Freestyle World In.** 
The lead IEEPA refund case at the CIT is changing hands. On July 16, 2026, Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. filed a voluntary notice of dismissal in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00595 — the case Judge Richard K. Eaton has used to supervise CBP's CAPE refund program since April.
 
Judge Eaton announced that the new lead case is *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States of America* (1:26-cv-01088), where a motion for class certification is now pending.
 
Judge Eaton said the court will lift the stay in *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 26-01088 — a separate IEEPA refund case in which a motion for class certification is currently pending.
 Class certification, if granted, would structurally transform the refund litigation from individual protest proceedings into a proceeding binding on the government with respect to the full class of IEEPA duty payers.

**CAPE Pipeline Scale.** 
According to declarations filed with the CIT, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries. CBP has processed more than 16 million entries under Phase 1. CBP has accepted more than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA refunds for processing. Of that total, roughly $22 billion has moved to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for disbursement.


**Government's Contested Posture on Finally Liquidated Entries.** The refund pipeline's fundamental legal dispute remains unresolved. 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion with significant implications for all importers seeking IEEPA refunds. Most notably, for the first time since the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, CBP asserted that it lacks the legal authority to issue IEEPA refunds on finally liquidated entries (i.e., on entries 180 days or more past liquidation) without an importer-specific court judgment.
 The July 17 Phase 3 order from Judge Eaton directly responds to this posture — he is manufacturing the importer-specific court-order predicate at scale, across 3,700 cases, rather than waiting for the government to concede the point administratively.


Phase 3 will cover finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period. Critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who
 have obtained an applicable court order. Judge Eaton's July 17 order appears designed to satisfy that condition for the 3,700 cases now before him. Importers not yet in a CIT proceeding should evaluate whether filing now or joining a class, if certified, provides better coverage for their finally liquidated entries.

### IV. Appellate Posture: *Learning Resources v. Trump* / *V.O.S. Selections* — SCOTUS Holding and Its Downstream Effects

The foundational ruling has not changed, but its downstream consequences continue to compound. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court issued its decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250), holding 6-3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs.
 
The Federal Circuit, sitting en banc, had affirmed, holding that IEEPA's authority to "regulate…importation" did not authorize such tariffs, as their scope, amount, and duration were unbounded.



On May 29, 2026, the DOJ filed a notice of appeal and a motion to amend the CIT's order, with filings going to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The petition for a writ of mandamus argues that universal injunctions are unlawful under the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in *Trump v. CASA*. The CIT had previously distinguished CASA from its own authority. However, the government's June 4 filing pointed to the Federal Circuit's en banc decision in V.O.S. Selections, which vacated an earlier universal injunction based on CASA. The DOJ filing also argued that the CIT failed to apply the equitable factors for injunctive relief required by the Federal Circuit's en banc IEEPA decision.


The government's mandamus track is a second-order litigation strategy: if the Federal Circuit accepts the argument that *Trump v. CASA* limits CIT's authority to issue universal reliquidation orders, the Phase 3 framework could be disrupted even after the July 17 order. Monitor that docket closely.

### V. Section 301 Succession: Refund Math Reset and Structural Implications

The succession from Section 122 to Section 301 resets the tariff baseline for refund modeling without extinguishing existing claims. Three points for GCs and CFOs:

First, unlike Section 122 — which carried a 150-day hard statutory ceiling under 19 U.S.C. § 2132 — Section 301 authority under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 carries no fixed expiration and no statutory rate ceiling above the determination level. 
Unlike Section 122, these authorities carry no statutory rate ceiling and no fixed expiration.
 Rates can be modified upward by supplemental Federal Register action; the pending excess-manufacturing-capacity probe could do exactly that for countries already at 12.5%.

Second, 
trading partners affected by the new round of tariffs account for about 99% of all U.S. imports, but a series of exemptions would significantly ease the measure's impact.
 Product-exclusion petitions filed at USTR under Section 301 procedures are the primary relief mechanism available to importers going forward. Unlike IEEPA, where no exclusion process was ever operationalized, Section 301 has established exclusion practice. File early; USTR backlogs grow quickly once a determination becomes final.

Third, 
USTR's June 2, 2026 Section 301 determination concerning the forced-labor enforcement practices of 60 trading partners proposes additional duties of 10 to 12.5 percent, with a public hearing on July 7. Section 232 actions are proceeding in parallel, including 100 percent duties on patented pharmaceuticals and active pharmaceutical ingredients effective July 31 for larger companies and September 29 for others.
 The pharmaceutical Section 232 action is a distinct and additional exposure layer not subsumed by the forced-labor Section 301 determination.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and refund claimants should move on three tracks in parallel this week. On the IEEPA refund track: verify that all entries are correctly enrolled in CAPE and that ACH enrollment is current — CBP will not issue paper refunds; confirm that any entries that liquidated more than 180 days ago have either a pending protest filed under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or an active CIT proceeding, given the government's position that finally liquidated entries require importer-specific court orders before Phase 3 disbursement; and monitor the *Freestyle World* class certification motion (Court No. 1:26-cv-01088 before Judge Eaton), which, if granted, could provide class-wide judicial cover for the full universe of finally liquidated entries without individual filings. On the Section 122 track: any importer that paid the February 24–July 23, 2026 surcharge and does not already have a protest on file should calculate the 180-day protest deadline for their earliest entries (entries from February 24, 2026 hit the 180-day clock on August 23, 2026) and file under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 before that date; follow *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) at the Federal Circuit for the merits briefing schedule, which will determine whether a broader Section 122 refund universe opens. On the Section 301 succession track: map your product lines against the forced-labor determination's 60-country list and model the net rate impact accounting for Section 232 stacking exclusions; for pharmaceutical-adjacent supply chains, the July 31 and September 29 Section 232 effective dates are immediate compliance obligations, not planning items.
