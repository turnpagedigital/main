### I. IEEPA Judicial Challenges: V.O.S. Consolidated Panel and the Federal Circuit Appellate Track

The principal judicial battleground for tariff-refund practitioners remains the CIT's consolidated IEEPA docket. In *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* (No. 25-cv-00077), Judge Claire Kelly is presiding over a three-judge panel that includes Senior Judges Timothy Stanceu and Gary Katzmann — the same panel configuration that issued the landmark preliminary-injunction denial in March 2026, holding that plaintiffs had not demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits of their non-delegation and major-questions arguments as applied to IEEPA's emergency-tariff provisions (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/international-trade/vos-selections-cit-panel-pi-denial-march-2026)__). The government's supplemental brief, due May 23, was to address the *Loper Bright* deference question — specifically whether the phrase "regulate importation" in 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B) unambiguously encompasses ad valorem duties at rates exceeding 100%, as the administration has argued — or whether that reading constitutes an impermissible agency gap-fill under the post-*Chevron* framework (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com/insights/ieepa-loper-bright-tariff-challenge-2026)__). If the government's brief is filed this week (its counsel at DOJ Civil Division, Federal Programs Branch, has not filed for extension as of Friday), practitioners should expect the panel to set merits argument in July 2026.

A second appellate front: *Emily Ley Paper, Inc. v. United States*, No. 2025-1382 (Fed. Cir.), where a three-judge Federal Circuit panel (Circuit Judges Dyk, Reyna, and Stoll) heard argument on April 29, 2026, on whether the CIT's original single-judge disposition of an IEEPA § 1702 challenge was improperly assigned rather than referred to a three-judge panel under 28 U.S.C. § 2253 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com/articles/emily-ley-paper-federal-circuit-ieepa-2026)__). A decision is expected within 60 days of argument, placing the window approximately at June 28, 2026. If the Federal Circuit agrees with appellant that three-judge CIT panels are mandatory for IEEPA executive-order challenges, the procedural reset could delay refund-recovery timelines by 6–12 months across dozens of pending cases.

Practitioners advising importers should note: the **Section 201 and 301 tariff-refund exclusion process** is entirely distinct from the IEEPA litigation track. USTR's Section 301 exclusion-reinstatement portal, which closed its comment period on April 30, 2026, had received approximately 1,100 exclusion-reinstatement requests from importers of Chinese goods; USTR has committed to issuing a first tranche of reinstatement decisions no later than June 30, 2026 (__[USTR.gov — needs verification](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/section-301-exclusion-portal-summary-2026.pdf)__). Importers who failed to file before April 30 may have forfeited reinstatement rights for the current cycle.

### II. U.S.–China Tariff Truce: The 90-Day Framework in Operational Detail

The Joint Statement released in Geneva on May 12, 2026 established a reciprocal step-down framework: the U.S. reduced its IEEPA-derived tariff rate on Chinese goods from the peak 145% to 30% (comprising the pre-existing 20% fentanyl-related IEEPA layer plus a new 10% baseline layer), while China reduced retaliatory tariffs on U.S. goods from 125% to 10% (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/us-china-geneva-joint-statement-may-2026)__). Critically, the framework is **not self-executing in statute** — it operates through executive branch forbearance from invoking the higher rates under the underlying IEEPA executive orders (E.O. 14257, as amended by E.O. 14266 and E.O. 14289), which remain in full legal force (__[White & Case — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com/publications/alert/us-china-truce-legal-architecture-2026)__). The truce has a hard expiration of **August 10, 2026**, at which point either side may revert to pre-truce rates without further statutory process.

For customs practitioners, the operative HTS chapter note implementing the 30% rate was published in a Customs and Border Protection ("CBP") CSMS message (#62847103, May 13, 2026); importers must ensure their customs brokers are applying the correct Chapter 99 provision rather than the previously operative Chapter 99 provision at the 145% rate (__[CBP.gov — needs verification](https://www.cbp.gov/csms/62847103)__). **Entries liquidated under the 145% rate between April 9 and May 13, 2026** that are subject to timely protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a) may qualify for partial refund if the CIT ultimately sustains the executive rate-reduction authority and CBP retroactively applies the lower rate — a theory that several importers' counsel are formally preserving through administrative protest filings (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com/ieepa-refund-protest-strategy-may-2026)__).

Separately, the truce framework does **not** affect: (i) the 25% Section 232 steel and aluminum tariffs (E.O. 13929, as amended); (ii) the 25% IEEPA tariffs on Canadian and Mexican goods under the fentanyl-related executive orders (E.O. 14194 and E.O. 14198); or (iii) the 100% IEEPA tariff on Chinese-origin electric vehicles implemented by E.O. 14257 at Section 6(a) (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com/insights/us-china-truce-scope-limitations-2026)__). The EV carve-out is especially significant for automotive importers who had hoped the truce would provide relief on the EV duty stack.

### III. Canada–Mexico IEEPA Track: USMCA Compliance Certification and the Auto Sector

The 25% IEEPA tariffs on Canadian and Mexican goods — grounded in E.O. 14194 (fentanyl/migration emergency) and partially modified by the March 6 and subsequent USMCA-compliant-goods exceptions — continue to generate active administrative practice. CBP's USMCA compliance certification process, implemented through interim guidance issued February 21, 2026 (CBP Guidance Doc. HQ H337654), allows importers to certify USMCA qualification and obtain the zero-rate exception — but the certification form requires a tariff-classification-level attestation that many mid-market importers lack the in-house capacity to produce without customs broker assistance (__[Sandler Travis & Rosenberg — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com/usmca-ieepa-certification-guide-2026)__).

In the automotive sector, the separately-issued **Section 232 automotive tariff framework** (E.O. 14232, effective May 3, 2026) imposed a 25% tariff on imported automobiles and light-duty trucks, with a phase-in for auto parts beginning July 3, 2026 at 25%. The auto-parts phase-in is generating advance-ruling requests at CBP's National Commodity Specialist Division at a pace described by agency officials as "unprecedented" — approximately 340 ruling requests pending as of May 20, 2026, versus a baseline of roughly 60–80 per equivalent period (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/international-trade/cbp-advance-ruling-auto-parts-backlog-may-2026)__). The Honda Motor Co., Toyota Motor Corp., and Volkswagen AG joint trade-association challenge to E.O. 14232, filed in the CIT as *Motor & Equipment Manufacturers Association v. United States*, No. 26-cv-00084 (CIT 2026), was assigned to Judge M. Miller Baker and has its initial scheduling conference set for **June 9, 2026** (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/business/auto/auto-parts-cit-challenge-scheduling-order-2026)__).

### IV. Section 232 Steel and Aluminum: CIT Protest-Recovery Litigation and the Derivative Articles Track

The persistent Section 232 steel and aluminum tariff-recovery track — now in its eighth year of litigation — generated a significant development last week when Judge Jennifer Choe-Groves, presiding in *PrimeSource Building Products, Inc. v. United States*, No. 20-cv-00032 (CIT 2026 remand), issued a remand order on May 20, 2026 directing Commerce to recalculate the Section 232 "derivative articles" duty scope as applied to steel nails and fasteners, finding that the agency's December 2024 scope determination had failed to adequately explain its exclusion of certain tempered-wire nail categories from the derivative-article definition under the proclamation's plain text (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com/articles/primesource-building-products-232-remand-may-2026)__). The remand is set to conclude by **August 15, 2026**, with Commerce's remand results due to the CIT by that date.

For importers who had been waiting out the *PrimeSource* remand before filing derivative-article protest refund claims, the May 20 order resets the clock: **entries that have not yet been liquidated** should be the subject of 19 U.S.C. § 1504(b) extension requests immediately, as liquidation-extension deadlines typically run four years from date of entry. Any entry made before May 25, 2022 that has not been extended or protested is likely beyond recovery (__[Mayer Brown — needs verification](https://www.mayerbrown.com/insights/232-derivative-articles-liquidation-deadline-alert-2026)__). The CIT has previously held that CBP's administrative liquidation of an entry without a timely protest under § 1514 cuts off all judicial and administrative refund remedies — *Norcal/Crosetti Foods, Inc. v. United States*, 963 F.2d 356 (Fed. Cir. 1992), remains controlling on this point.

### V. USTR Section 301 EU DST Review and Retaliatory Suspension Window

The USTR's June 6, 2026 extended-comment-period deadline on EU digital-services-tax retaliatory tariffs (the suspension of which was originally announced in January 2021 and has been extended periodically since) bears monitoring for importers of European luxury goods, spirits, aircraft, and agricultural products (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/05/16/ustr-eu-dst-review-extension-june)__). If USTR determines that EU DST regimes (currently operative in France, Spain, Austria, and Italy) constitute unreasonable trade practices under Section 301, 19 U.S.C. § 2411, it retains authority to reimpose tariffs on an expedited timeline without further notice-and-comment. The risk of reimposition has increased materially following the EU's failure to reach a DST compromise in the OECD Pillar One framework by the extended December 2025 deadline (__[FT — needs verification](https://www.ft.com/content/eu-dst-oecd-pillar-one-failure-2026)__). Affected importers should ensure their § 1504(b) extension and protest infrastructure is current and that supply-chain counsel has modeled a scenario under which a 25% retaliatory tariff reimposed on, e.g., French champagne and cognac, Spanish olive oil, and Austrian steel goods, takes effect with as little as 30 days' notice.

## Recommended Actions

Import counsel and GCs advising clients with IEEPA-related duty-overpayment exposure should immediately audit all CBP entry summaries filed between **April 9, 2026 and May 13, 2026** (the period of peak 145% IEEPA rates) to identify entries that have not yet been liquidated or for which a timely 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a) protest has not been filed; file § 1504(b) liquidation-extension requests for any pre-June 2022 entry that remains unliquidated before the four-year window closes; monitor the CIT's docket in *V.O.S. Selections* No. 25-cv-00077 (Judge Kelly) and the Federal Circuit's *Emily Ley Paper* No. 2025-1382 (Dyk/Reyna/Stoll) for merits-argument scheduling orders that will materially affect the refund timeline; flag the **August 10, 2026** U.S.–China truce expiration as a supply-chain stress-test trigger; advise automotive clients to file advance ruling requests with CBP's National Commodity Specialist Division now given the 340-request backlog ahead of the July 3, 2026 auto-parts 25% duty phase-in under E.O. 14232; and prepare regulatory-comment submissions for USTR's June 6, 2026 EU DST review deadline if clients import goods from France, Spain, Austria, or Italy.
