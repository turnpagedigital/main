# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Sunday, July 5, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The nineteen-day countdown begins today.** The Section 122 surcharge imposed by Proclamation 11012 — the 10% flat duty on virtually all U.S. imports effective February 24, 2026 under 19 U.S.C. § 2132 — expires by statute on July 24, 2026, nineteen days from this briefing. 
The proclamation ran from February 24 to July 24, 2026.
 
On June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motion to stay the CIT's injunction, allowing the government to continue collecting the 10% surcharge.
 That stay means every importer — including the three named plaintiffs who won at the CIT — is still paying 10% on covered entries today. The operational and legal picture for the week is therefore split across three simultaneous tracks: (1) the Section 122 expiration and its Section 301 successor, (2) the Federal Circuit appeal of the CIT's universal IEEPA refund orders, and (3) the CAPE pipeline, which added new functionality as recently as July 7.

---

### Track One: Section 122 Expires July 24 — Section 301 Successor Hearings Run This Week

The Section 122 surcharge has no mechanism for unilateral presidential extension. 
The 150-day limit is statutory; only Congress can extend Section 122 tariffs beyond that date.
 Congressional action is not expected. The administration's stated strategy is to replace the surcharge with Section 301 authority — which carries no statutory rate cap and no expiration date.


On June 2, 2026, USTR issued its Section 301 forced-labor determination and proposed tariffs following an investigation into the acts, policies, and practices of 59 countries and the European Union — proposed at 10% for most goods of 15 trading partners, and 12.5% for most goods of 45 trading partners.
 A parallel overcapacity investigation covers 16 additional economies. 
On March 12, 2026, USTR self-initiated Section 301 investigations into 60 of the largest U.S. trading partners; the public comment docket received over 450 written submissions, with two days of public hearings and confidential government-to-government consultations with 46 of the 60 economies under investigation.


This week is the decisive procedural moment: 
the docket for public comments was open from June 2 to July 6, 2026, and the first day of public hearings at the USITC was scheduled for July 7, 2026, continuing over subsequent days if needed.
 
USTR likely intends to be ready to impose the tariffs by the time the Section 122 global surcharge expires on July 24, 2026; the notice does not discuss whether the tariffs would be implemented immediately or whether there would be a grace period for goods in transit.


The refund-calculation consequence for importers is material: 
Section 122 is temporary by statute and capped at 15% by law; Section 301 tariffs have no expiry date and no statutory rate cap.
 
Unlike Section 122, Section 301 tariffs focus on specific countries and industries and could result in significantly higher rates.
 Importers who shifted sourcing geography or renegotiated supply contracts around the 10% flat rate need to reprice their exposure before July 24 — they may face a higher, country- and sector-specific rate the morning the surcharge drops off. 
These tariffs, if finalized, apply on top of any other applicable duties.


On the Section 122 litigation itself: the CIT's May 7, 2026 ruling in *State of Oregon et al. v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Ct. Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606, remains stayed pending Federal Circuit appeal. 
A two-judge majority — Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly — granted summary judgment for the importer plaintiffs, holding that the duties imposed through Proclamation 11012 exceeded the president's statutory authority under Section 122, reasoning that the statute requires the president to identify "balance-of-payments deficits" measured according to specific recognized metrics Congress contemplated when it enacted Section 122 in 1974.
 
The permanent injunction extends only to Burlap and Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington (as importer of record); all other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.


The practical upshot: because the Section 122 surcharge expires in nineteen days regardless of how the Federal Circuit rules, the appellate stay is largely mooting the equitable relief. Importers whose entries fall in the February 24–July 24, 2026 window who paid the 10% surcharge on HTSUS heading 9903.03.01 entries should begin documenting their duty payments now. 
Section 122 was imposed under entirely different statutory authority from IEEPA and is not part of the IEEPA refund program; there is no announced refund mechanism for Section 122 duties.
 If the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's ruling, a separate recovery mechanism — likely modeled on CAPE but requiring fresh legislation or a new CIT order — would need to be built. That process could take months.

### Track Two: Federal Circuit Appeal of the Universal IEEPA Refund Order


On June 3, 2026, the DOJ filed notices of appeal in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.
 
The government challenges the CIT's orders requiring universal refunds of IEEPA tariff payments, arguing that the CIT's refund orders constitute impermissible universal injunctions that extend relief to importers who did not file protective actions at the CIT.
 The government's core theory rests on *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, 606 U.S. 831 (2025), which limited district courts' authority to issue nationwide injunctions — a precedent the CIT has distinguished on the ground that its statutory authority differs from Article III district courts created under the Judiciary Act of 1789.


The government's mandamus petition argues that universal injunctions are unlawful under *Trump v. CASA*; the CIT had previously distinguished CASA from its own authority.
 
It remains unclear whether the Federal Circuit will accept the CIT's position that it is not subject to the restrictions on universal injunctions discussed in CASA; the Federal Circuit has previously expressed skepticism about the CIT's authority to grant such broad relief.


At the June 9, 2026 CIT show cause hearing — triggered by the CIT's May 27, 2026 order directing the government to show cause why the stay of refund orders should not be lifted — 
DOJ reiterated its position that the CIT erred and the court cannot order the government to refund money to non-plaintiffs.
 
The hearing ended without the CIT lifting the stay but produced important disclosures about CAPE's scope and limits.


The critical fracture line remains the "finally liquidated" entry category. 
The government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 
Under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE; the government's appeal has divided the importer universe into two groups — those who filed at the CIT and those who have not.
 For the second group, 
the risk is no longer theoretical: the government's current position is that importers who did not file at the CIT may wait indefinitely — and perhaps permanently — for refunds of finally liquidated entries.


On June 4, 2026, 
plaintiffs in *V.O.S. Solutions, Inc. v. Trump* filed a motion for class certification covering all importers that paid IEEPA tariffs whose entries are not eligible for processing in CAPE Phase 1, along with a request for an injunction directing the government to expand CAPE to process all refund claims with interest on the basis that the class is entitled to indivisible injunctive relief.
 That motion remains pending.

### Track Three: CAPE Pipeline — Phase 2 Live, Phase 3 Imminent, Warehouse-Entry Rule Change Effective July 7


As of June 29, 2026, CBP's CAPE system reached Phase II — enhanced processing for reconciliation-flagged entries, making the large majority of IEEPA duties eligible for refund.
 
Consistent with CBP's June 4, 2026 CIT filing, Phase 2 covers entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, and 06) where a corresponding reconciliation entry (entry type 09) has not yet been filed.
 
The scope of Phase 2 is further limited to unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation, consistent with Phase 1.


The aggregate refund numbers are striking. 
EAC Thomas reported that Phase 1 of CAPE has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, and that approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.
 
The government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and that by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.


A significant operational change took effect two days ago. 
Effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) will no longer be accepted on a CAPE Declaration; warehouse entries accepted between April 20, 2026, and July 6, 2026, without a corresponding warehouse withdrawal submission will not be (re)liquidated with an IEEPA duty refund; warehouse withdrawals (Entry Types 31, 32, 34, 38) will continue to be accepted, with refunds processed upon (re)liquidation of the associated warehouse entry.
 Importers who filed CAPE Declarations for warehouse entries before July 6 without pairing them with withdrawal submissions need to file corrective declarations now.

Phase 3 — covering finally liquidated entries — remains the most consequential and legally contested phase. 
Technical readiness does not guarantee that refunds will follow; the DOJ continues to argue that CBP lacks authority to reliquidate finally liquidated entries without an individual lawsuit, and that position is now before both the CIT and the Federal Circuit.
 
According to the June 29, 2026 CBP CAPE page, certain reconciliation entries with the reconciliation entry already on file are not included in the June 29, 2026 CAPE deployment and will be included in a future phase.

### Drawback, Protests, and the 19 U.S.C. § 1514 Clock

For importers who did not file at the CIT and whose entries are or will become finally liquidated, the 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest clock is the last administrative lever. Under § 1514, protests must be filed within 180 days of the date of mailing of notice of the CBP decision — which, for liquidated entries, typically runs from the liquidation notice. 
CBP's CAPE refund program remains operational and continues to process refunds for many unliquidated and recently liquidated entries, but important categories of entries remain outside its current scope; importers should closely monitor liquidation and protest deadlines, as preserving administrative remedies may become increasingly important if the government's position prevails.
 
Categories such as drawback entries, certain reconciliation entries, entries under administrative protest, and entries not filed in ACE are anticipated in still-later phases.
 Importantly, 
CAPE covers only IEEPA tariffs — duties collected under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act using codes 9903.01.xx and 9903.02.xx; Section 122 was imposed under entirely different statutory authority and is not part of the IEEPA refund program.

## Recommended Actions

For the next ten business days, prioritize three things in parallel. First, audit every entry carrying HTSUS codes 9903.03.xx (Section 122) from February 24, 2026 forward: document duty amounts, entry status, and liquidation dates so that if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's *Burlap and Barrel* ruling — and a refund mechanism is subsequently ordered — your records are structured for rapid CAPE-style filing; no refund mechanism exists today, but the litigation posture favors preservation now. Second, review all warehouse entries (Types 21/22) filed in CAPE between April 20 and July 6 without a paired warehouse withdrawal: the July 7 rule change cuts off eligibility retroactively for unpaired entries, requiring corrective declarations before those entries liquidate. Third, assess by July 14 whether any finally liquidated IEEPA entries (entries liquidated more than 80 days before your CAPE Declaration filing date) fall within the still-open § 1514 protest window; importers who have not filed at the CIT should strongly consider filing a protective action before the Phase 3 gate closes — CBP has stated explicitly that non-litigants will not receive Phase 3 refunds; this is not a theoretical risk. On the Section 301 front, the July 7 USITC hearing is the last formal procedural opportunity to submit product-exclusion or rate-objection arguments before USTR finalizes the successor tariff architecture; if any covered goods in your import mix face stacking exposure under both the forced-labor and overcapacity investigations, time-sensitive interventions must be submitted through the docket before post-hearing rebuttal comments close — five days after the last hearing day.
