# IEEPA TARIFF ADVISORY | May 14, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The first IEEPA tariff refunds reached importers' bank accounts on Tuesday, marking a tangible milestone in the federal government's effort to return approximately $166 billion in duties collected under the now-invalidated IEEPA tariff regime. As of May 11, U.S. Customs and Border Protection reported that 126,237 CAPE declarations have been submitted since the portal launched on April 20, with 86,874 passing initial file validations and 15,123,221 individual entries accepted for the removal of IEEPA duties. Of those accepted entries, 8,338,081 have been liquidated or reliquidated without IEEPA duties, generating anticipated refunds and interest totaling approximately $35.46 billion. Supply chain company Flexport reported that its clients alone had received $137 million in refunds as of Monday, and small business owners confirmed deposits covering both the original duty amounts and statutory interest. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-ieepa-tariff-refunds-businesses/))

The momentum, however, comes with significant friction that disproportionately affects smaller importers. Early data shows CAPE rejection rates approaching 40 percent, driven by a combination of user filing errors — outdated ACE credentials, incorrect banking information, entry number formatting mistakes — and apparent CBP processing issues. The 80-day post-liquidation filing window under 19 U.S.C. §1501 creates acute time pressure, as entries liquidated around the CAPE launch date will begin falling out of eligibility in late June. Legal practitioners warn that the accessibility gap between large multinational importers with in-house trade counsel and small businesses navigating the system independently is producing a two-tier refund experience, with the businesses most hurt by the original tariffs also most likely to encounter procedural obstacles to recovery. ([FoodNavigator-USA](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2026/05/14/tariff-refunds-what-brands-need-to-know/))

On the replacement tariff front, the CIT's May 7 decision invalidating Section 122 tariffs as *ultra vires* — holding that Proclamation 11012's reliance on current account and goods trade deficits did not constitute "large and serious" balance-of-payments deficits as Congress understood the term in 1974 — has been temporarily neutralized by the Federal Circuit's administrative stay issued May 13, keeping the 10 percent surcharge in effect during appeal. The CIT's permanent injunction benefits only the named plaintiffs (two small business importers and the state of Washington), meaning the remaining universe of importers continues to pay Section 122 duties with no automatic refund mechanism. Holland & Knight's analysis emphasizes that unlike the IEEPA refund pathway, there is no universal refund process for Section 122, and importers who wish to preserve future refund rights must affirmatively file protests under 19 U.S.C. §1514 or pursue individual CIT actions under 28 U.S.C. §1581(i). ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/us-court-of-international-trade-invalidates-the-administrations))

President Trump and President Xi began a high-stakes summit in Beijing today — the first visit by a sitting U.S. president to China in nearly a decade — with trade at the center of discussions. Reports indicate both sides may announce a limited trade agreement involving mutual tariff reductions on approximately $30 billion in goods, excluding strategic technologies and national security items. The proposed "Board of Trade" framework, first floated by USTR Greer in March, would create a managed-trade mechanism for ongoing tariff negotiations. Whatever emerges from the summit will directly shape the scope and severity of forthcoming Section 301 tariffs, for which USTR concluded public hearings on May 8 covering 16 economies and is accepting comments through July 6. The administration has signaled that Section 301 tariffs — which carry no statutory limits on duration or magnitude — will serve as the primary long-term replacement for both the expiring Section 122 surcharge and the invalidated IEEPA tariffs. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/14/trump-xi-beijing-summit-trade-taiwan-ai-iran-rare-earths-tariffs.html))

A parallel litigation front is emerging in downstream consumer class actions. On May 9, Nike was sued in a proposed class action in Portland federal court alleging the company raised footwear prices by $5 to $10 per pair and apparel prices by $2 to $10 per item to offset roughly $1 billion in IEEPA tariff duties — while now positioned to collect full federal refunds without returning those price increases to consumers. The complaint follows a similar March 27 filing against Lululemon alleging approximately $240 million in tariff cost pass-through. These cases signal that companies receiving IEEPA refunds should expect heightened scrutiny over their pricing decisions during the tariff period, and the litigation risk extends beyond the importer-of-record to any company in the supply chain that adjusted consumer-facing prices in response to IEEPA duties. ([FloraDress/Industry Reports](https://floradress.com/news/nike-class-action-ieepa-tariff-refunds-2026/))

## Recommended Actions

Companies with pending CAPE declarations should verify their ACE account status, confirm banking details, and audit entry number formatting immediately, given the 40 percent rejection rate and the 80-day filing window that is now actively narrowing for entries liquidated in late March and April. Importers who have not yet filed should prioritize CAPE submission over all other compliance tasks, as entries are permanently falling out of eligibility each day. For Section 122 duties, companies should file protective protests under 19 U.S.C. §1514 within 180 days of liquidation to preserve potential refund rights in the event the CAFC upholds the CIT's invalidation — there will be no automatic refund process for Section 122 regardless of the appeal outcome. Companies that adjusted consumer-facing prices during the IEEPA tariff period should conduct an internal review of pricing decisions, promotional adjustments, and margin documentation in anticipation of potential consumer class action exposure as refunds are received. Finally, importers should begin modeling exposure to forthcoming Section 301 tariffs by reviewing the USTR's investigation scope across 16 economies, preparing comments before the July 6 deadline, and monitoring summit outcomes for signals on country-specific tariff rates and product exclusion frameworks.
