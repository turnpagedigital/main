# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Friday, July 24, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

### The Regime Turns Over: Section 122 Expires at Midnight; Section 301 Takes Effect Simultaneously

Today is the most consequential single-day tariff reset since the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 6-3. 
The 10% global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) expired by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT today — exactly 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 
Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation was pending; the surcharge terminated on its own terms.


The administrative vacuum that might have followed lasted approximately zero seconds. 
USTR's final action in its forced-labor Section 301 investigations — Dockets USTR-2026-0265 and USTR-2026-0266 — took effect at 12:01 a.m. Eastern time on July 24, 2026, imposing additional duties of 10% or 12.5% on all products of 60 investigated economies, subject to exemptions set out in two annexes.
 
These Section 301 duties are additive to currently existing tariff rates. Trading partners that have made commitments to adopt and effectively enforce forced-labor import prohibitions face a 10% tariff; those that have failed to adopt any such prohibition face 12.5%.


The practical consequence for most affected supply chains: the rate on top of MFN has not dropped — it has in many cases increased, and now rests on Section 301 statutory footing rather than the time-limited § 2132 emergency authority that courts already found wanting. 
The new Section 301 tariff does not stack on top of Section 232 tariffs, unlike the existing China Section 301 regime.
 
Goods loaded onto a vessel and in transit on the final mode of transit before July 24 escape the additional duty if entered before July 28.
 Importers who moved cargo to port ahead of today's deadline have a four-day window; those who did not should treat July 28 as a hard cut-off.

### USTR Final Action — Forced-Labor Section 301 on 60 Economies: Rate Mechanics and Scope


USTR announced its findings yesterday in its forced-labor Section 301 cases on 60 countries around the globe, proposing additional duties on all products except those listed in Annex A. USTR determined that 54 of the investigated countries failed to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor, and proposes a 12.5% tariff on those countries.
 
The remaining six economies failed only to *effectively enforce* an existing prohibition; USTR proposes 10% on those countries.



The 46-country group facing 12.5% includes, among others: Algeria, Angola, Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Colombia, Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, Egypt, India, Israel, Japan, Jordan, Kazakhstan, Kuwait, Morocco, New Zealand, Nigeria, Norway, Oman, Peru, the Philippines, Qatar, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, South Africa, South Korea, Sri Lanka, Switzerland, Thailand, and Trinidad and Tobago.


Note the Brazil overlay: 
USTR's final action in a separate, standalone Brazil Section 301 investigation imposed a 25% tariff on goods of Brazil effective July 22, 2026 — a distinct action from the 46-country forced-labor tariff, with its own legal footing.
 Brazilian importers must now analyze potential stacking between a 25% country-specific rate and the 12.5% forced-labor rate — 
USTR has not clarified whether the two Section 301 tariffs stack.


One bloc is insulated from today's reset: 
the EU. As of July 1, 2026, EU-origin goods pay the trade deal's 15% all-inclusive ceiling instead of Section 122 — it includes MFN rather than stacking on it (goods with MFN at or above 15% pay MFN only), covers autos at 15% in lieu of the 25% Section 232, and leaves steel and aluminum at 50%.


The Section 301 replacement is legally distinguishable from § 2132 in two material ways. First, it does not carry a 150-day hard cap; Section 301 actions remain in force until USTR formally terminates or modifies them. Second, the statutory trigger — unfair trade practices and forced-labor enforcement failures — has no inherent time limit tied to a balance-of-payments test. The administration has effectively traded a time-limited emergency surcharge for a durable, Congressionally-grounded trade remedies framework. That durability matters for refund math: importers who sought to quantify post-Section 122 tariff exposure based on a reversion to MFN-only rates should revise their models upward.

### Section 122 Litigation — Federal Circuit Appeal Live as Surcharge Sunsets; Refund Posture for Named Plaintiffs

The § 2132 surcharge is now expired, but the litigation over its legality continues — and the outcome carries direct financial consequences for the three named plaintiffs and for the government's appeal posture in the broader IEEPA refund dockets.

Procedural record: 
On May 7, 2026, the CIT ruled 2-1 in *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* (Slip Op. 26-47) that the Section 122 surcharge exceeded the President's statutory authority, finding that the Administration had not satisfied the statutory preconditions for invoking § 2132.
 
The CIT found that the Proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficit" required by the statute, and noted that if the executive branch could select among any sub-accounts to identify a deficit, this would effectively create open-ended tariff authority under § 2132, raising constitutional nondelegation concerns.


Appellate posture: 
The government appealed and the Federal Circuit issued a temporary administrative stay pausing enforcement of the court's order while the court considered the government's motion for a longer stay pending appeal.
 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit issued a stay of the CIT's injunction, allowing CBP to continue collecting § 2132 duties until today's statutory sunset.
 
The CIT separately denied the government's own motion for a stay pending appeal on May 20, finding the government had failed to demonstrate irreparable harm or likelihood of success on the merits — the CAFC's administrative stay nonetheless remained in place.


The expiration of the surcharge does not moot the Federal Circuit appeal. 
The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality.
 Named plaintiffs — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — have standing-based refund claims for duties paid between February 24 and today. 
The CIT found the Administration exceeded its authority by citing general trade deficits rather than the specific "balance-of-payments deficits" Congress had in mind in 1974; however, the court limited its permanent injunction to those three named plaintiffs.
 Every other importer who paid § 2132 duties now faces the question of whether to pursue their own protest or litigation track under 19 U.S.C. § 1514.

### IEEPA Refund Pipeline — CAPE Phase 3 Arriving, Lead Case Transitions to *Freestyle World*

The IEEPA refund program is simultaneously advancing on the technical side and contracting on the legal side. Both developments are consequential before the weekend.

**Lead case succession.** 
On July 16, 2026, Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. filed a voluntary notice of dismissal in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00595 — the case Judge Richard K. Eaton has used to supervise CBP's CAPE refund program since April.
 
The court's July 15 order disclosed Euro-Notions' intention to dismiss and named *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 26-01088, as the successor lead case, where a motion for class certification is now pending.
 
The dismissal does not end the broader IEEPA refund litigation. Judge Eaton said the court will lift the stay in *Freestyle World*, Court No. 26-01088, where a motion for class certification is currently pending.
 If class certification is granted in *Freestyle World*, it would be the first mechanism capable of extending CAPE-equivalent refund rights beyond individually-docketed importers — a structural development that dwarfs any single-entry resolution.

**CAPE pipeline metrics.** 
In March and April 2026, the CIT in both *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States* and *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* ordered CBP to liquidate unliquidated entries without regard to IEEPA duties; in response, on April 20, 2026, CBP launched CAPE Phase 1 in the ACE portal.
 
According to declarations filed with the CIT, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries. CBP has processed more than 16 million entries under Phase 1, and has accepted more than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA refunds for processing.


**Phase 3 — finally liquidated entries.** 
CAPE Phase 3 will address finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs. CBP's executive assistant commissioner for the trade office, Susan Thomas, confirmed at a June 9, 2026 CIT hearing before Senior Judge Eaton that Phase 3 programming will be ready in late July 2026.
 
However, the federal government continues to contest the legal basis for refunds on finally liquidated entries — it remains unclear whether refunds will actually flow once the technical capability is in place.
 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion asserting, for the first time since the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs, that CBP lacks the legal authority to issue refunds on finally liquidated entries without an importer-specific court judgment.
 That position, if accepted by the Federal Circuit in 
*Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, Fed. Cir. Case No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026,
 would wall off a material subset of the $166 billion pool from CAPE processing entirely. Phase 3 readiness without legal authorization is a technical capability without commercial value.

**July 15 CIT order on finally liquidated entries.** 
On July 15, 2026, the CIT ordered that CBP would be directed to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries ahead of CAPE Phase 3, with the order to be entered across roughly 3,700 IEEPA cases before the court.
 The breadth of this order — 3,700 cases — signals that Judge Eaton is pushing the government toward compliance on finally-liquidated entries notwithstanding CBP's legal objection. The Federal Circuit's resolution of Case No. 26-1898 will determine whether that push lands.

**Federal Circuit parallel appeal on refund scope.** The government's appeal in Fed. Cir. 26-1898 is the single most consequential live docket for importers holding finally liquidated entries. The briefing schedule has not been confirmed in these search results; counsel should check the PACER docket directly. The government's argument — that 19 U.S.C. § 1514 liquidation-finality bars administrative reliquidation absent an importer-specific judgment — is not frivolous; it rests on established customs law. The counterargument is that the *Learning Resources* ruling operates as a structural injunction requiring CBP to treat IEEPA duties as void ab initio.

### Rate Environment Post-July 24 — What the Tariff Stack Looks Like Starting Today


As of May 2026, the average effective U.S. tariff rate stood at 7.2%, down from prior months following the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling that IEEPA tariffs were unconstitutional.
 That figure will move upward as Section 301 duties replace Section 122 across 60 economies. 
Effective tariff rates vary dramatically by trade partner and product; among major trading partners, China already faced the highest effective tariff rate at 23.4% as of May.


The pre-today stack for most goods: MFN rate + 10% Section 122 + applicable Section 232 (50% steel/aluminum, 25% autos, 50% copper). Starting today: MFN rate + 10% or 12.5% Section 301 (forced labor) + applicable Section 232. For most country-product combinations, the net rate is essentially flat or marginally higher. The material shifts are: (a) Brazil, which had been at 10% Section 122 and now faces a 25% Section 301 standalone rate effective July 22 — a 15-point jump on most goods; and (b) any importer who modeled a reversion to MFN-only after Section 122 expiration and did not account for the Section 301 replacement.


Penn Wharton estimates that new tariffs raised $268.8 billion in gross customs revenue between January 2025 and May 2026, before accounting for IEEPA refunds; if importers had not accelerated purchases or changed purchasing patterns, gross tariff revenue would have been $57.9 billion higher over the same period.

## Recommended Actions

Importers paying Section 122 duties between February 24 and today should immediately audit their ACE entry records to confirm entry numbers, liquidation status, and importer-of-record identity — the three most common CAPE filing-validation failures per the July 13 Lord Declaration — and determine whether their entries fall within CAPE Phase 1 (already processed) or Phase 3 (finally liquidated, legally contested, technical readiness confirmed for late July). Named plaintiffs in *Oregon / Burlap and Barrel* — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — should engage counsel immediately to ensure their injunction-backed refund claims are preserved through the Federal Circuit appeal; today's statutory expiration does not eliminate those claims, but it may sharpen the government's mootness arguments on prospective relief. All other Section 122 payors should evaluate whether to file administrative protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 to preserve their legal options in the event the Federal Circuit rules against the government in the § 2132 appeal — the protest deadline under § 1514 runs 180 days from liquidation, not from today, so calendar-review against each entry's liquidation date is essential. On Section 301: model Brazil-origin goods on the 25% standalone rate immediately; for all other affected economies, the 10% or 12.5% forced-labor rate is now in effect and stacks on Section 232 where applicable; goods in-transit before July 24 and entered before July 28 qualify for duty-free passage under the final action's in-transit carve-out. Track *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 26-01088, for class certification developments — a grant there would represent the broadest possible expansion of CAPE-equivalent relief.
