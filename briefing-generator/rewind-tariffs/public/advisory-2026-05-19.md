# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Monday, May 19, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Three developments frame the tariff landscape on May 19: the Federal Circuit's briefing schedule on Section 122 closes today with plaintiff responses due, marking the inflection point toward a substantive stay ruling within weeks; the CAPE Phase 1 refund processing has achieved 82% entry coverage ($35.46 billion anticipated refund and interest pool) with 1,880 consolidated refunds stalled on missing ACH registration; and consumer-side class action litigation targeting retailers including Amazon, Nike, Shein, Temu, Costco, and Lululemon has accelerated, with state attorneys general simultaneously pressing Congress to mandate tariff-refund pass-through to consumers. The June 7 DOJ appeal deadline on the scope of the CIT's March 4 reissued refund order remains 11 business days away with no appellate signal from the government.

The U.S. Court of International Trade issued a 2–1 divided opinion on May 7 (Slip Op. 26-47) holding that the President's invocation of Section 122(a) of the Trade Act of 1974 to impose a 10% temporary tariff violated the statute's "fundamental international payments problem" predicate. Judge Kamieniecki's majority distinguished between "balance-of-payments deficits" and "fundamental international payments problems," applying historical Section 122 precedent and legislative history; Judge Marsden's dissent argued the statutory language granted the President broader discretion. Critically, the CIT's relief was expressly limited to the three named plaintiffs (Burlap & Barrel Inc., Basic Fun Inc., and the State of Washington). On May 12, the Federal Circuit entered an administrative stay, freezing the CIT's order pending the government's motion for a full stay pending appeal. The expedited briefing schedule—plaintiff response due May 19 (today), DOJ reply due May 22—closes this week, after which a substantive stay order is expected on a compressed timeline. CBP continues assessing the 10% duty on all other importers pending appellate resolution.

## CBP CAPE Refund Processing — $35.46B Pool, Phase-1 Coverage at 82%

CBP's controlling operational snapshot continues to be the second progress report to the Court of International Trade, which confirmed 8,338,081 entries liquidated or reliquidated without IEEPA duties against an anticipated refund-and-interest pool of approximately $35.46 billion. The CAPE module is capturing roughly 82% of IEEPA entries; the remaining 18%—entries with non-standard liquidation postures, suspended-entry status, or compliance flags—continues to be routed to manual processing. A total of 75,306 CAPE Declarations have been submitted covering approximately 11.2 million entries; 47,315 declarations passed validation and are routed for refund processing within the 60–90 day standard timeline. The CAPE Phase 1 window covers unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation (roughly April 22–June 9, 2026). However, 1,880 consolidated refunds remain stalled for missing ACH registration. Sandler, Travis & Rosenberg has reiterated that importers should not rely on CAPE alone: protest filings under 19 U.S.C. §1514 remain the durable preservation mechanism, particularly for entries that have not yet liquidated or that encounter downstream validation issues. CBP anticipates valid refunds for unliquidated entries will generally be issued within 60 to 90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a Declaration warrants further CBP review for compliance concerns.

## Section 122 Federal Circuit Briefing Closes May 22

The Federal Circuit's May 12 temporary administrative stay of the CIT's May 7 ruling continues to keep the 10% Section 122 global tariff in force for all importers. The expedited briefing schedule closes this week—plaintiffs' response due May 19 (today), DOJ reply due May 22 (Friday)—after which a substantive stay order is expected on a compressed timeline. The CIT's underlying May 7 decision held by 2–1 that the statutory predicate of a "large and serious United States balance-of-payments deficit" was not satisfied; relief was named-plaintiff-limited (Burlap & Barrel, Basic Fun, State of Washington). Regardless of the appeal outcome, the Section 122 surcharge expires by operation of statute on July 24, 2026 under the Trade Act's 150-day limit absent affirmative congressional extension. Importers not yet named plaintiffs in the CIT action should be filing their own Section 122 challenges before the Federal Circuit issues its stay ruling, since the May 7 relief does not extend to other importers. Each filing creates a separate record and multiplies the plaintiff pressure on the appellate court.

## DOJ Appeal Window—June 7 Pivot Point

With the reissuance of Judge Eaton's refund order on March 4, the 60-day DOJ appeal window has been reset to approximately June 7. As of May 19, no notice of appeal, motion for extension, or public administration signaling has surfaced. The lengthening silence is itself a market-moving fact: trade-law commentators are increasingly modeling a non-appeal scenario, in which the refund architecture stabilizes through the summer with the only residual litigation risk centered on the small subset of high-compliance-flag entries. Refund-recovery counsel should treat June 7 as the operative pivot—a missed appeal date converts the refund obligation from contingent to settled. The government would need to file a notice of appeal by June 7 to preserve its right to challenge the scope of the refund order on appeal to the Federal Circuit. A silent June 7 effectively locks in the nationwide refund mandate across all importers, settling the boundary question that has animated the litigation since March.

## Consumer Class Action Wave and State AG Coordination

Consumer-side class action litigation has accelerated significantly in May 2026, with putative nationwide actions naming Amazon, Nike, Shein, Temu, Costco, and Lululemon as defendants. These complaints allege unjust enrichment, breach of contract, and violation of state consumer-protection statutes on the theory that the defendants retained refunds or failed to pass them through to customers who ultimately bore the tariff surcharge. The discovery posture in the early filings is being built around whether companies retained the IEEPA-tariff surcharge in their pricing after the refund became foreseeable (roughly late March 2026 onward). State attorneys general, including Connecticut Comptroller Sean Scanlon and representatives of New York, Arizona, Oregon, and Vermont, have simultaneously demanded that Congress mandate automatic pass-through of tariff refunds to consumers. The coalition narrative frames the refund retention issue as consumer protection and pricing disclosure failure, applying pressure on both the companies and Congress to enact mandatory rebate mechanisms. Connecticut estimates that tariffs cost the state's citizens $1.7 billion, with a per-capita refund of approximately $1,200.

## Section 301 Investigations—Structural Excess Capacity Cycle and July 24 Target

USTR's 76 Section 301 investigations into structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors, launched in March 2026 and subjected to public hearings May 5–8, 2026, continue their compressed timeline toward remedy determination. USTR is targeting 16 economies (China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan, and India) on allegations including excess steel production in China, low chemical-facility utilization in Germany, below-60% manufacturing capacity utilization in Thailand, and non-market support propping up unprofitable firms in Japan. The hearing transcript will be posted on ustr.gov post-May 8. The informal target completion date and remedy determination is July 24, 2026—the same date the Section 122 surcharge expires by statute. This temporal overlap creates a potential trade calendar squeeze: any new Section 301-based tariffs imposed near July 24 would immediately fill the Section 122 void, providing the administration with a continuity vehicle for broad-based import protection absent congressional action or appellate intervention.

## Recommended Actions

Importers holding IEEPA exposure should (1) verify ACH registration on the CBP electronic refund portal and clear stalled consolidated refunds if ACH account information is outstanding—this is a mechanical prerequisite that does not depend on litigation outcome; (2) continue filing §1514 protests in parallel with CAPE declarations to preserve protest remedies if CAPE processing encounters downstream validation friction; and (3) treat June 7 as the operative deadline for refund-finality modeling—a lapsed DOJ appeal window de facto converts contingent refund rights to settled refund obligations. Section 122-exposed importers should (1) continue paying the 10% duty into a segregated reserve pending the Federal Circuit's stay ruling (expected within weeks); (2) file their own named-plaintiff CIT actions before the Federal Circuit issues its decision on the stay if not yet named plaintiffs, since the May 7 CIT relief was expressly limited to Burlap & Barrel, Basic Fun, and State of Washington; and (3) model continuity planning for July 24 in light of the Section 301 remedy timeline, since any new tariffs announced near the Section 122 expiration would reset the tariff landscape for Q3 2026. Retail and wholesale companies that retained IEEPA pass-through surcharges in customer pricing should (1) prepare detailed pricing documentation analyzing the date-range during which the refund became foreseeable (roughly late March onward); (2) engage pricing counsel on disclosure obligations under state AG settlement frameworks and the emerging consumer class-action discovery standards; (3) model claimant exposure under unjust-enrichment and state consumer-protection statutes before class-action discovery posture hardens; and (4) consider whether voluntary disclosure or early-settlement engagement with state AGs or consumer counsel would reduce ultimate exposure relative to multi-year class-action defense costs.
