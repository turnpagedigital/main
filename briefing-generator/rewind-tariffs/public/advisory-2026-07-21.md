## Analysis & Developments

### Lead: Three Deadline Collisions This Week — Section 122 Sunsets in 72 Hours, Brazil 25% Tariff Active Wednesday, and the CAPE Test Case Rotates Again

The week of July 21 compresses more rate-change and refund-pipeline risk into a single calendar than any prior week since the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling. Three developments demand attention now.

**First: Section 122 expires at 12:01 a.m. ET this Friday, July 24.** 
The 10% global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) is scheduled to expire by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24. Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending. Absent congressional action, the surcharge terminates on its own terms.
 
The President cannot extend it unilaterally.
 The statutory ceiling is hard. That said, expiration does not translate to tariff-free imports: 
the July 24 sunset does not mean a general reduction in U.S. tariffs. Section 232 duties on steel (25%), aluminum (25%), copper (25%), lumber (25%), and semiconductors remain firmly in place, as does Section 301 on Chinese-origin goods.


**Second: USTR's Section 301 replacement regime reached its July 20 completion deadline yesterday.** 
USTR faced a completion deadline of Monday, July 20, on two parallel Section 301 investigations launched in March: one targeting structural excess manufacturing capacity across 16 major economies, and a second covering forced-labor practices across 60-plus countries. The proposed replacement rate is 12.5% on 46 countries including China, Vietnam, India, Thailand, Japan, and South Korea.
 The architecture matters: 
unlike Section 122, Section 301 has no rate cap and no time limit — so 12.5% is a starting proposal, not a statutory ceiling.
 For CAPE-eligible importers who recovered IEEPA duties at the 10% Section 122 level, a Section 301 successor at 12.5% on their origin countries means ongoing landed-cost exposure that exceeds the regime they were refunded from. Model the net position now.

**Third: the CAPE lead case rotated again on July 20, and the pipeline numbers are significant.** 
On July 20, 2026, the U.S. Court of International Trade selected a new underlying case as the one to test the federal government's updates on its system for refunding duties charged under Trump's struck-down global tariff regime, though it otherwise kept in place certain reporting and hearing deadlines.
 The new lead case is 
*Freestyle World Inc. v. United States*, selected after the plaintiff in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States* indicated its intention to voluntarily dismiss.
 This is the third lead-case rotation since Learning Resources — from Atmus Filtration (dismissed April 6) to Euro-Notions (now exiting) to Freestyle World. Each rotation resets the government's 60-day appellate clock, though the substantive refund order travels with the reassignment.

### CAPE Pipeline: $121.75 Billion Accepted, $86.3 Billion Transmitted — and 9,837 Importers Frozen Out

The pipeline figures as of July 13 are the clearest signal yet of both the program's scale and its execution gap. 
On July 13, 2026, the government filed the Declaration of Brandon Lord, Executive Director, Trade Programs Directorate, Office of Trade, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (ECF No. 45). Among the items reported is that approximately $121.75 billion in both potential and certified refunds have been accepted for processing in CAPE, and approximately $86.3 billion in refunds (duties plus interest) have been sent to the U.S. Department of Treasury for disbursement.
 That leaves roughly $35.45 billion accepted but not yet transmitted — a processing queue that matters for timing expectations.

More concretely for individual claimants: 
9,837 refunds have not been transmitted to Treasury because Automated Clearing House ("ACH") account information has not been provided by the importer of record or its authorized designee.
 
As of 3 p.m. ET on Friday, July 10, 2026, those 9,837 refunds remain stuck for this reason.
 If your ACH banking information is not on file with CBP via Form 4811 designee, your disbursement is not processing — the fix is administrative and urgent.

Phase 3 — the tranche covering finally liquidated entries — is the next critical unlock. 
On July 15, 2026, the CIT issued an order that is the clearest indication of next steps for addressing the status of finally liquidated entries. The CIT expects CBP to launch Phase 3 of the CAPE platform. The order states that the court will issue case-specific orders in each of the approximately 3,700 individual IEEPA cases directing CBP to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries in accordance with a procedure to be outlined in that anticipated order.
 The stakes for Phase 3 access are significant: 
the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT. Approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected, if CBP proceeds as planned, to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined. Under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE.

### The Consumer Windfall Theory: *Hastings v. Microsoft Corporation* and the Downstream Refund Wave

A new liability theory reached a federal court on Friday. 
Microsoft Corp. stands to make an "unjustified windfall profit" through refunds of Trump's now-invalidated global tariff regime, according to a proposed consumer class action removed to Washington federal court Friday, July 20, 2026, reported by Law360 in *Hastings v. Microsoft Corporation*.
 
Named defendants associated with the case include Amazon.com Inc., Costco Wholesale Corp., Microsoft Corp., and Sony Interactive Entertainment Inc.


The theory mirrors earlier actions in the gaming sector. 
The plaintiffs allege that Xbox had sued the federal government for return of the illegal tariffs but has not committed to return those tariffs to consumers, arguing that the costs of the tariffs were included in all product purchases and should therefore be returned.
 
The tariffs were struck down by the U.S. Supreme Court in February 2026, and a parallel PlayStation action, *Walker et al. v. Sony Interactive Entertainment LLC*, similarly claims a "double recovery windfall" that Sony is set to receive should be passed on to the buyer who purchased hardware post-price-hike.


The downstream windfall theory is not frivolous and deserves tracking. Its resolution could affect how importers receiving CAPE refunds need to account for consumer-side pass-through claims — particularly retailers and consumer-electronics brands that imposed publicly announced price increases citing tariff costs and may now face discovery requests about their refund receipts and pricing decisions. The U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington is the current venue, unverified docket number pending.

### Section 122 Appellate Posture: Federal Circuit Stay Intact Through Sunset; Refund Rights Undecided


On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the CIT, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47 (Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606), invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 
A two-judge majority — Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly — granted summary judgment for the importer plaintiffs, holding that the duties imposed through Proclamation 11012 exceeded the President's statutory authority under Section 122. The court reasoned that Section 122 requires the president to identify "balance-of-payments deficits" measured according to specific recognized metrics that Congress contemplated when it enacted Section 122 in 1974.



On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motion to stay the lower court's injunction. This ruling allows the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 import surcharges from the plaintiffs who had previously won relief — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. The CAFC's decision effectively pauses the relief previously granted to the named plaintiffs, meaning the 10% surcharge will continue to be collected from all importers through the duration of the appeals process.


The practical consequence of the Section 122 sunset is narrower than it appears: the surcharge was going to stop collecting on July 24 regardless of the litigation outcome. 
The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality. The Federal Circuit stayed the CIT's ruling pending the government's appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, so CBP has continued to collect the duty on entries through July 23. The July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period; the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the appeal.


For any importer who paid the Section 122 10% surcharge on entries from February 24 through July 23, 2026: 
Section 122 duties are explicitly excluded from the IEEPA refund mechanism — the CIT's injunction in *Oregon v. United States* applies only to named plaintiffs, not to importers generally. A broader refund pathway exists only if the Federal Circuit or the Supreme Court ultimately upholds the CIT's ruling and issues a remedy broad enough to cover all importers.
 That appellate track is live; preserving entry records and protest rights under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 is the only hedge available today.

### Brazil 25% Section 301 Tariff: Effective Tomorrow

Separate from and concurrent with the Section 122 sunset: 
following the required comment period and a public hearing, USTR's final action imposes a 25% tariff on goods of Brazil, effective July 22, 2026. This is a distinct action from the 46-country, 12.5% Section 301 tariff (the forced-labor investigation). Brazil's 25% rate is specific to Brazil and stands on its own legal footing.
 
USTR's own materials describe exemptions for beef, orange juice, aircraft and aircraft parts, and energy products.


For importers of Brazilian-origin goods, the math as of Wednesday: 
the 25% tariff takes effect July 22, 2026, arriving four days before the unrelated Section 122 sunset and USTR's separate 46-country forced-labor Section 301 deadline — importers sourcing from Brazil now have three moving parts to track instead of one.
 Verify your specific HTS code against the official Federal Register annex; the exemption list is more granular than headline reporting reflects.

### Section 301 Successor: USTR's July 20 Completion Deadline Has Passed — Effective Date Pending


On June 2, 2026, USTR issued the notice of determinations and proposed responsive actions in the Section 301 investigations of "Acts, Policies, and Practices of Various Economies Related to the Failure to Impose and Effectively Enforce a Prohibition on the Importation of Goods Produced with Forced Labor," covering 60 economies.
 
For economies that impose a forced labor import prohibition or have committed to impose and enforce such a prohibition through an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade, USTR proposes 10% as the rate of additional duties. For all other economies, USTR proposes 12.5% as the rate of additional duty.



A new Section 301 regime covering 46 countries is expected to activate on or near the same date the Section 122 surcharge disappears. USTR faced a completion deadline of Monday, July 20 — yesterday — on two parallel Section 301 investigations.
 As of this morning, no Federal Register notice announcing the effective date of the successor Section 301 action has been confirmed in search results. That gap matters: 
if Section 301 tariffs are not finalized by July 24, imports would revert to pre-IEEPA duty rates plus any existing Section 232 and prior Section 301 tariffs on China.
 Monitor USTR's press office and the Federal Register for the activation notice today and tomorrow.

## Recommended Actions

Importers with CAPE-eligible IEEPA refund claims should verify immediately that ACH banking information is on file with CBP via Form 4811 — 9,837 disbursements are frozen for this reason alone, and the fix is administrative, not legal. For Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries), the CIT's July 15 order confirms that case-specific reliquidation orders are coming for the approximately 3,700 active plaintiff cases, but only for importers who filed at the CIT; those who have not filed are at material risk of permanent exclusion from Phase 3 refunds under the government's current litigation posture in *Freestyle World Inc. v. United States* before Judge Eaton. On Section 122 (19 U.S.C. § 2132): the surcharge stops collecting at 12:01 a.m. ET Friday — document every entry and duty payment from February 24 through July 23, 2026, now, because the Federal Circuit appeal of *State of Oregon v. United States* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) is live and a successful outcome could open a second round of refund eligibility requiring reconstructed entry records across 150 days. Brazilian-origin importers face a 25% Section 301 tariff effective tomorrow, July 22; confirm your HTS code against the Federal Register annex and do not rely on headline exemption lists. Finally, monitor USTR's Federal Register filings today for the activation notice on the 46-country, 12.5% forced-labor Section 301 regime — the July 20 completion deadline has passed, and the gap between that deadline and the Section 122 sunset on July 24 is the window in which landed-cost modeling is most unstable.
