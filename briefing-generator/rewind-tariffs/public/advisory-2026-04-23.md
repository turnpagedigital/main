# IEEPA TARIFF ADVISORY | April 23, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Three days into the CAPE portal's operation, the contours of the $166 billion IEEPA tariff refund process are becoming clearer — and so are its complications. CBP's Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries system went live on April 20, accepting Phase 1 declarations covering approximately 82% of eligible IEEPA duty payments, or roughly $127 billion across entries that remain unliquidated or were liquidated within the past 80 days. The system's first hours were marked by technical difficulties including "Duplicate Tax ID" errors that locked some importers out of their accounts, portal timeouts during CSV uploads, and hold times exceeding four hours on CBP's support line. CBP has acknowledged the reports and indicated it is investigating, but has not yet issued a formal technical bulletin or revised its submission guidance. For importers whose entries fall within Phase 1 parameters, the 60-to-90-day processing timeline announced by CBP begins upon acceptance of the CAPE Declaration — not upon submission — meaning that rejected or error-flagged filings will see their refund clocks reset. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/tariff-refund-portal-system-trump-cape-cbp/)) ([Forvis Mazars](https://www.forvismazars.us/forsights/2026/04/ieepa-tariff-refund-update-cape-system-and-processing-guidance))

The most significant operational development this week is the entry of major logistics carriers into the CAPE filing process. UPS confirmed on April 21 that it has begun submitting declarations for shipments where it served as importer of record, processing refund claims on behalf of its customers. FedEx similarly announced it is filing declarations for eligible entries where it acted as customs broker and will issue refunds to customers "expeditiously" once CBP processes its claims. DHL has indicated a comparable approach. This is consequential not merely for the volume of entries these carriers represent, but because it establishes an operational precedent for broker-intermediated filing that will shape how the tens of thousands of smaller importers — many of whom lack direct ACE access or internal customs compliance teams — navigate the process. The carriers' early engagement also signals institutional confidence that the refund mechanism will function as designed, notwithstanding the initial technical friction. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/21/ups-fedex-tariff-refunds-ieepa.html)) ([Supply Chain Dive](https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/fedex-ups-and-dhl-detail-tariff-refund-approach-for-customers/818057/))

The appeal question continues to loom over the entire process. The Trump administration's window to seek rehearing of the Supreme Court's February 20 decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* extends through approximately late May, and as of today there has been no filing, no motion for extension of time, and no public signaling of intent to challenge the ruling. Judge Eaton's subsequent orders directing CBP to build and deploy CAPE have proceeded without objection from the government. The absence of appeal activity is increasingly being read by market participants and litigation funders as a strong signal that the administration has accepted the outcome — but the window remains open, and any filing would introduce substantial uncertainty into both the CAPE process and the secondary market for tariff refund claims, where deal pricing continues to discount for appeal risk and the potential application of tariff setoffs that could materially compromise recovery. ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/04/insights-april-2026/tariff-refund-mechanism-takes-shape)) ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-10/trump-tariff-refund-tool-will-go-live-on-april-20-us-customs))

Beyond the IEEPA refund process, the trade policy landscape is shifting rapidly in ways that affect the same importer population. The USTR's Section 301 public hearings on forced labor enforcement begin April 28 at the International Trade Commission, followed by hearings on manufacturing overcapacity targeting 16 economies starting May 5. USTR Greer has indicated these investigations will proceed on an "accelerated timeline," with action — potentially new tariffs — expected by late July. Simultaneously, the CIT heard oral arguments on April 10 in two challenges to the administration's Section 122 replacement tariffs, where a three-judge panel questioned whether the government had demonstrated the "large and serious" balance-of-payments deficit required by statute to justify the surcharge. A ruling could come within weeks, and an adverse outcome for the government would create a second major refund obligation while further constraining the administration's available tariff authorities. ([Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/new_section_301_investigations_ieepa_tariff_refund_developments_legal_challenges_section_0426.html)) ([Reason/Volokh](https://reason.com/volokh/2026/04/10/thoughts-on-todays-oral-argument-in-the-section-122-tariff-cases/))

The downstream litigation environment is also intensifying. Plaintiffs' firms have begun filing consumer class actions against importers who passed IEEPA tariff costs through to retail customers, alleging unjust enrichment and violations of state unfair and deceptive practices statutes. Costco, FedEx, and other large retailers and logistics providers have been named in early filings. The theory is straightforward: if an importer recovers duties from CBP but retains the price increases it imposed on consumers to offset those duties, consumers are entitled to restitution. For importers navigating the CAPE process, this creates a strategic tension — the refund itself may generate exposure to pass-through claims, and companies should be coordinating with litigation counsel on record-retention, pricing documentation, and disclosure strategies before filing their declarations. ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/03/consumer-class-actions-arising-from-ieepa-tariff-refund-efforts)) ([Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutman.com/insights/ieepa-tariff-refunds-may-come-with-an-unforeseen-cost-exposure-to-consumer-class-actions/))

## Recommended Actions

Companies with IEEPA duty exposure should prioritize three immediate workstreams. First, importers who have not yet filed CAPE declarations should verify their ACE account status, confirm their importer of record number is not flagged with duplicate-ID errors, and prepare CSV files conforming to CBP's published template — those encountering technical issues should document each attempted submission and CBP response for potential future claims of constructive filing. Second, for entries not covered by Phase 1 — including those subject to antidumping or countervailing duties, entries with suspended or extended liquidation, and certain informal entries — importers should calendar the Phase 2 announcement and ensure they have preserved all entry documentation, commercial invoices, and proof of payment under 19 U.S.C. §1514 in anticipation of filing. Third, companies that raised consumer-facing prices in response to IEEPA tariffs should immediately engage litigation counsel to assess pass-through exposure, implement litigation holds on pricing communications and tariff surcharge records, and consider whether voluntary consumer credits or adjustments may mitigate class-action risk before claims mature. Importers who also face exposure under the Section 122 replacement tariffs should monitor the CIT's pending ruling in *Oregon v. Trump* and *Burlap and Barrel v. Trump* and be prepared to file parallel protests under 19 U.S.C. §1514 if those tariffs are struck down.
