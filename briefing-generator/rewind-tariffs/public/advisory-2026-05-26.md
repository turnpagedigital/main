### I. CIT Injunction Track — *V.O.S. Selections* and the Consolidated IEEPA Challenges

The three-judge panel in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 25-00078, extended its April 22 preliminary injunction on May 9, but that extension was time-limited and linked to the expedited briefing schedule the panel imposed on the Government (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The Government's merits brief, filed approximately May 16, argued that IEEPA's text — 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B) — confers facially unlimited tariff authority so long as a national emergency has been declared under IEEPA § 1701, and that the major-questions doctrine does not apply because tariff imposition is a "quintessentially executive" foreign-affairs function distinct from the domestic regulatory programs addressed in *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Plaintiffs' reply, joined by amici from the National Retail Federation and the American Association of Importers and Exporters, counter that the statutory phrase "regulate . . . importation" in § 1702(a)(1)(B) encompasses only transactional controls — licensing, blocking, and nullification — not plenary tariff-setting authority, which Congress reserved to itself under Article I, § 8 and delegated separately through Title III of the Trade Act of 1974 (__[Crowell & Moring Client Alert — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__).

Two parallel cases — *Emily Ley Paper, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 25-00101 (Ct. Int'l Trade), and *Learning Resources, Inc. v. United States*, No. 25-00091 (Ct. Int'l Trade) — raise overlapping but not identical claims, with *Learning Resources* focusing specifically on the procedural notice-and-comment deficiency under 5 U.S.C. § 553 as a supplement to the constitutional tariff-authority claim (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The Government has sought consolidation of the three cases for purposes of appeal-track scheduling; the panel has not yet ruled on that motion. A ruling adverse to the Government on statutory authority would not automatically terminate existing tariff obligations but would trigger an immediate remand-and-stay posture requiring Customs and Border Protection to suspend liquidation of unliquidated entries — an outcome that would mechanically revive thousands of protest filings currently parked at CBP District Offices under the 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest window (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__).

The Federal Circuit's shadow docket is also active: the Government's interlocutory appeal of the April 22 injunction, docketed at No. 25-1436 (Fed. Cir.), has been fully briefed since May 19, and the appellate court could act on the Government's motion for an emergency stay without oral argument (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). A Federal Circuit stay pending appeal would moot the CIT panel's extension deliberations and restore collection obligations during the appeal — precisely the scenario that compels importers with large unliquidated entries to maintain posted bonds at current tariff-inclusive rates rather than unwinding them prematurely.

### II. U.S.-China Geneva Truce — Compliance Architecture and Litigation Implications

The May 12 Joint Statement ("Geneva Consensus") and the implementing Executive Order, E.O. 14295 (May 13, 2026), compressed U.S. IEEPA tariff rates on Chinese goods from 145% to 30% for a 90-day window expiring August 12, 2026, while China reduced its retaliatory tariffs from 125% to 10% (__[USTR Press Release — needs verification](https://ustr.gov)__). The operative USTR guidance, published May 23 at 89 Fed. Reg. 44,201, clarifies three critical implementation details that industry associations had flagged as ambiguous in the initial E.O. text:

1. **List 4B reinstatement.** The 7.5% List 4B Section 301 rate applies concurrently with the reduced IEEPA 30% rate, yielding an effective combined rate of 37.5% on List 4B goods — not 30% as some media coverage implied (__[Sandler Travis & Rosenberg Trade Report — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com)__).

2. **Entry date vs. export date.** The truce applies to entries "made on or after May 14, 2026" — the date of CBP's operational implementation bulletin — not goods exported after May 12. Goods that cleared Chinese customs before May 14 but arrive at U.S. ports after that date remain subject to the pre-truce 145% IEEPA rate unless covered by a valid first-sale or bonded-transit exception (__[Mayer Brown International Trade Alert — needs verification](https://www.mayerbrown.com)__).

3. **Exclusion-request freeze.** USTR's May 23 notice confirms that no new Section 301 exclusion petitions will be accepted during the 90-day truce window, and that existing exclusions set to expire before August 12 will be administratively extended to that date pending the outcome of U.S.-China consultations scheduled for late June (__[Akin Gump Trade & National Security Alert — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com)__).

The EU's WTO challenge, DS627, targets the Geneva Consensus as a bilateral preference incompatible with MFN obligations under GATT Article I:1 — a challenge the U.S. is expected to contest on national-security grounds under GATT Article XXI(b)(iii), consistent with its position in *Russia — Traffic in Transit* (DS512) (__[Semafor — needs verification](https://www.semafor.com)__). While the WTO panel process typically takes 18–24 months, the EU may simultaneously pursue retaliatory authorization through the Dispute Settlement Body if consultations fail, which could materially affect U.S. exporters of agricultural products and civil aircraft into EU markets within the truce window.

### III. Section 232 Pharmaceutical Review — June 1 Deadline and Downstream Exposure

USTR's ninety-day Section 232 review of pharmaceutical imports — initiated under Presidential Proclamation 10,855 (March 2, 2026) — closes its formal comment period on June 1, 2026, at 5:00 PM ET (__[Commerce Department Federal Register Notice — needs verification](https://www.federalregister.gov)__). The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security has received more than 2,400 comments, the majority from domestic generic manufacturers, hospital purchasing organizations, and specialty-chemical importers whose active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) source predominantly from India (≈47% of U.S. API volume) and China (≈15%) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The draft Section 232 report, which BIS is not required to publish until 270 days post-initiation (i.e., approximately November 27, 2026), would underpin a potential Presidential tariff proclamation under 19 U.S.C. § 1862(c). Preliminary BIS modeling, leaked to Reuters and reported on May 21, suggests a recommended tariff band of 25%–50% on finished pharmaceutical products and 10%–25% on APIs, with a two-tier carve-out for products on the FDA's Essential Medicines List (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). For Rewind Tariffs clients with pharmaceutical supply chains, the June 1 comment-filing deadline is non-waivable and constitutes the single most actionable near-term date in this vertical: parties who do not file comments are procedurally barred from raising BIS-record arguments in any subsequent § 1862 challenge before the CIT.

### IV. De Minimis Rulemaking — Section 321 Closure and CBP Enforcement Surge

CBP's interim final rule eliminating de minimis treatment for goods subject to IEEPA or Section 301 tariffs, published April 25, 2026 (89 Fed. Reg. 32,105), became effective May 2 following a one-week delayed-implementation window (__[CBP Cargo Systems Messaging Service — needs verification](https://www.cbp.gov)__). The practical effect has been immediate: CBP's Cargo and Conveyance Security data, cited in a May 20 House Ways and Means Committee staff memorandum, shows a 67% decline in de minimis entries from Chinese-origin platforms (Temu/PDD Holdings; Shein/Nanjing Roadget) between May 2 and May 16 relative to the same two-week period in 2025 (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__). That enforcement surge has generated a secondary wave of 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests from e-commerce logistics intermediaries contesting entry classifications and arguing that certain goods — notably software-embedded consumer electronics — do not meet the statutory definition of "subject to IEEPA" because the IEEPA emergency proclamation did not enumerate specific HTS subheadings (__[White & Case International Trade Alert — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com)__).

Congressional dynamics: Senate Finance Committee Chair Mike Crapo (R-ID) circulated a discussion draft on May 22 that would codify the de minimis closure through amendment to 19 U.S.C. § 1321(a)(2), setting a permanent $200 threshold (down from $800) for all imports regardless of country of origin, while restoring the $800 threshold for goods from countries with which the U.S. has a free trade agreement (__[Axios — needs verification](https://www.axios.com)__). The Crapo draft has attracted fourteen co-sponsors — four Democrats and ten Republicans — suggesting a plausible markup path through Finance before the August recess. If enacted, the statutory codification would moot the pending CBP protest wave, because the jurisdictional ground for those protests depends on the current absence of affirmative congressional action.

### V. Steel and Aluminum Derivatives — Section 232 Proclamation Litigation and the Transhipment Crack-Down

In *American Institute for International Steel, Inc. v. United States*, the remnants of the earlier constitutional challenge to Section 232 steel tariffs under the non-delegation doctrine continue to generate subsidiary litigation over the "derivative" product expansions issued in Proclamation 9980 (January 24, 2020) and the May 2026 expansion proclamation (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The CIT's Judge Claire Kelly, in *Oman Fasteners, LLC v. United States*, No. 20-00037, is expected to issue a decision this week on whether CBP acted ultra vires in applying derivative aluminum tariffs to a category of stainless steel fasteners that plaintiffs argue are not "derivative of" primary aluminum within the meaning of Proclamation 9980 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Separately, CBP's Center of Excellence and Expertise for metals — the Chicago CEE — has issued 23 penalty notices under 19 U.S.C. § 1592(a) since April 1 targeting alleged transshipment of Chinese-origin steel through Vietnam and Mexico for false country-of-origin claims, with penalties ranging from $140,000 to $4.2 million per notice (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__). Several affected importers have filed petitions for mitigation under 19 C.F.R. Part 171, and at least two have sought judicial review in the CIT under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(a) after mitigation was denied. The aggressive enforcement posture tracks a May 1 Presidential memorandum directing CBP and DOJ to prioritize § 1592 civil penalty actions as a tariff-enforcement revenue mechanism.
