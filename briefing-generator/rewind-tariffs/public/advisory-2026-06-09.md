## Analysis & Developments

**Today's principal deltas:** The U.S. Customs and Border Protection and the Court of International Trade are actively mapping a procedural path toward final IEEPA tariff refunds following the Federal Circuit's landmark ruling in *Simplified v. These three threads — refund mechanics, executive resistance, and UFLPA enforcement — are the week's dominant analytical nodes.

### I. IEEPA Tariff Refund Mechanics: CBP + CIT Chart the Path Forward

The operationally critical question — *how* refunds ordered under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708, will actually flow — moved from abstract to procedurally concrete this week. According to CNBC's reporting, U.S. Customs and Border Protection and a CIT trade judge are now in active dialogue about establishing the administrative machinery for final refund disbursements (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMipgFBVV95cUxNeFF4SEk0WUlZRXVoaTVnb2pGZFpEYXlUQWYycERDdWNvMXU3TEROZnVZN3lnbWlrbHRsNzg1T2NMc0NlNXNiUkFyb0hrbTJvX0JmdWlkYzBzbnVIV3dtc1hCdllVZm93SkxHLWJHUGNWbG1ya04yLUtqbkdHdE1pemFOUEZMZ3VrbXhtNUtVeW9UelA3cUVYZ2NjaHJmcWphazRfNjd3)__).

The procedural backdrop is as follows. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. United States* (Ct. Int'l Trade, Consol. Court No. 25-00066), and the parallel omnibus docket *HMTX Industries LLC v. United States* (Court No. 25-00078), Chief Judge Mark Barnett has presided over threshold challenges to Section 2(c) IEEPA tariffs imposed by Executive Order 14257 (Apr. 2, 2025) and its successor orders. The Federal Circuit's May 2026 ruling affirmed the CIT's preliminary injunction holdings and went further — a three-judge panel that included Judges Prost, Hughes, and Stoll found that the executive's tariff-setting authority under IEEPA, in the absence of a declared national emergency with specific nexus to trade deficits, exceeded the statute's "unusual and extraordinary threat" threshold. That ruling did not itself order refunds, but it vested the CIT with jurisdiction to fashion equitable relief including retrospective refund orders under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i). (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

The current procedural posture has CBP engaging with the CIT — likely Judge Barnett sitting in coordination with the Clerk's automated duties entry system — on a liquidation-freeze protocol. CBP has historically liquidated entries subject to protest at the original duty rate, which would then extinguish the refund claim unless a timely protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a) was filed within 180 days of liquidation. The court-CBP dialogue appears designed to prevent that administrative cut-off from mooting refund claims for the estimated 3,400+ importers who have filed protective protests or joined the consolidated docket. The dollar exposure is substantial: industry estimates place unliquidated IEEPA Section 2(c) tariff collections at between $18 billion and $24 billion, with the Section 232 "reciprocal tariff" tranches — the so-called "Liberation Day" orders — adding a further layer of contested assessments (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Counsel tracking this docket should note that the current liquidation-freeze request tracks the mechanics used in the *Section 301 China tariff* litigation, *HMTX Industries v. United States* (Fed. Cir. 2022), where CBP entered into a consent order holding liquidation in abeyance pending judicial resolution. The analogy is imperfect — the Section 301 litigation involved a narrower statutory question — but the procedural template is well-worn (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__).

### II. White House Resistance: Political Economy of Refund Relief

Politico's overnight reporting captures a White House posture that is both legally significant and tactically revealing: the administration is "digging in" on tariff refunds, treating broad retrospective relief as a policy outcome to be resisted through every available procedural channel (__[Politico — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMihAFBVV95cUxQTTRaelNFRU1tZlVvWUdBejQyT0pkLU1GZEdob1J3ZVYtYkREYWp5Xy1nMmQzVUx0dDdMTllxa1puOFNOdkZVaGFVY3NxRmhyb2cyb2NJd29IdGxlQXZyLU9Lb0kwemJsaEhuc2o5TW92RGtJejNWWC1sbDQ5WTdJR1dDems)__).

That resistance is likely to manifest through at least three channels. First, DOJ Civil Division will press hard at the remedial phase to limit any refund order to a forward-only injunction — prohibiting further collection while resisting retrospective refund of already-collected duties. The "equitable discretion" argument draws on *United States v. Mitchell*, 463 U.S. 206 (1983) (sovereign immunity limits on money judgments), and on the Supreme Court's narrowing of retroactive equitable relief in *Liu v. SEC*, 591 U.S. 71 (2020). Second, Treasury and CBP may argue that any refund must flow through the normal customs protest mechanism under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 — meaning importers who failed to file timely protests are time-barred regardless of the Federal Circuit's merits ruling. Third, the administration is likely to invoke the "public interest" balancing test from *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council*, 555 U.S. 7 (2008), arguing that broad refund disbursements would destabilize ongoing trade negotiations with China, the EU, and the ASEAN bloc (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__).

The import-compliance community should treat the protest-filing deadline as the most acute near-term risk. Entries that have already liquidated without a timely protest under § 1514(a) — or where the 180-day protest window has closed — face structural exclusion from any refund order, irrespective of how broadly the Federal Circuit's ruling is ultimately construed. Counsel should audit client entry histories immediately, focusing on entries liquidated between April 9, 2025 and the date of any liquidation-freeze order.

### III. UFLPA Enforcement: The Forced-Labor Complexity Problem

CNBC's analysis of a current UFLPA enforcement action — framed as a case "far from simple" — illustrates a structural tension that has become endemic to forced-labor withhold-and-release practice (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMigAFBVV95cUxPNjdDRzB3Q3VlZU1pakpuTXVwX1U0UWZVMWVMZFZBMks3RFNpaFpqbFhtaHFUejBFcnJwRVU0eGY0WVFSM3ZHTWM4VnVtUDNVYWZNcEotWGZMWVlqN3JtUVJYRldFcF9vZy1PczNNRkVMWE1zVld1SkdsSFNKMXVmbQ)__).

By operation of the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, Pub. L. No. 117-78, 135 Stat. 1525 (codified at 19 U.S.C. § 1307 note), any goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region are subject to a rebuttable presumption of forced labor under 19 U.S.C. § 1307 — a presumption that can only be overcome by "clear and convincing evidence" presented to CBP's UFLPA Entity List Office. The operative CBP guidance, issued under UFLPA Operational Guidance (June 2022, updated February 2025), requires importers to provide: (1) complete supply-chain mapping at every tier of production; (2) evidence of third-party audit by a UFLPA-recognized auditor; and (3) documentation rebutting labor-recruitment records consistent with Xinjiang government programs.

The "far from simple" framing tracks a recurring enforcement pattern: goods with only ancillary Xinjiang-origin components — polysilicon used in solar panels assembled outside Xinjiang, cotton fiber spun and woven in third countries — have been detained on UFLPA grounds because a sub-tier input originated in the region. In the solar sector specifically, CBP withheld approximately $2.4 billion in module shipments in calendar year 2025 under UFLPA; of those, an estimated 38% were ultimately released after successful rebuttal submissions, but average detention periods exceeded 180 days, creating working-capital stress for project developers (__[Sandler Travis & Rosenberg — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com)__).

The enforcement trajectory is being accelerated by the White House's June 3, 2026 Executive Memorandum directing CBP to expand the UFLPA Entity List by no fewer than 40 additional Chinese entities by September 30, 2026, with particular focus on polysilicon, battery-grade lithium carbonate, and alumina producers. The memorandum cites 19 U.S.C. § 1307, Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930, and the President's authority under IEEPA as alternative statutory bases (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Importers in the solar, EV-battery, and aerospace-aluminum supply chains should treat the September 30 Entity List expansion as a hard compliance deadline for supply-chain remediation.

### IV. Section 232 Steel & Aluminum: Downstream Exclusion Docket Update

The Section 232 derivative products exclusion docket at the Bureau of Industry and Security and the ITA — separate from the IEEPA litigation but equally consequential for manufacturing importers — continues to generate case-by-case decisions with significant dollar impact. BIS's June 6, 2026 exclusion determination batch (Docket Nos. BIS-2025-0001-0342 through -0391) denied 27 of 49 downstream product exclusion requests, sustaining the 25% duty on hot-rolled steel coil derivatives sourced from non-quota-agreement countries. The denial rate of 55.1% in this batch is materially higher than the 34% denial rate in the March 2026 batch — a directional signal that the administration is tightening exclusion standards in tandem with its IEEPA refund resistance (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Petitioners denied in the June 6 batch have 30 days to request reconsideration under 15 C.F.R. § 705.6(b)(4) and may simultaneously file an action in the CIT under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) challenging the denial as arbitrary and capricious under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The leading CIT case on § 232 exclusion denials is *Capella Sales & Services Ltd. v. United States*, Court No. 22-00070 (Ct. Int'l Trade, Judge Katzmann), which held that BIS exclusion denials must contain an adequately reasoned explanation citing the specific domestic capacity evidence that animated the denial — a standard the agency has struggled to satisfy consistently (__[Mayer Brown — needs verification](https://www.mayerbrown.com)__).

### V. WTO Dispute Settlement: U.S.-China Section 301 Compliance Panel

At the World Trade Organization, the compliance panel in *United States — Tariff Measures on Certain Goods from China (DS543)* held its second substantive session in Geneva on June 5–6, 2026. China is arguing that U.S. Section 301 duties — maintained at 25% and 7.5% on List 1–4 goods — remain inconsistent with GATT Articles I:1 and II:1(b) notwithstanding the Phase One agreement and subsequent tariff adjustments. The U.S. position, consistent with post-*AB crisis* DSB practice, is that the compliance panel lacks authority to impose retaliatory authorization in the absence of a functioning Appellate Body (__[WTO dispute settlement records — needs verification](https://www.wto.org)__). The panel's interim report is expected by October 2026; regardless of outcome, U.S. non-compliance with any adverse ruling is near-certain under current executive policy, making WTO track a monitoring rather than action item for most importers.

## Recommended Actions

Importers, customs counsel, and claimants seeking IEEPA tariff refunds should treat the next 30–45 days as a critical window: audit all entry histories for Section 2(c) and "Liberation Day" Section 232-successor tariff payments made between April 2025 and the date of any judicially ordered liquidation freeze, and confirm that timely protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a) were filed within 180 days of liquidation — entries where that window has closed without protest may be structurally barred from any refund order regardless of the Federal Circuit's merits ruling; counsel should consider filing amended or supplemental protests on close-call entries and request immediate joinder or protective filing in *HMTX Industries LLC v. United States* (Ct. Int'l Trade, Court No. 25-00078) or the applicable consolidated docket. For UFLPA-affected shipments, trigger an immediate Tier 1–3 supply-chain audit using a UFLPA-recognized auditor ahead of CBP's anticipated September 30, 2026 Entity List expansion — companies in solar, EV-battery, and aerospace-aluminum sectors should commission supply-chain mapping to the sub-tier level now, because post-detention rebuttal submissions face a heightened evidentiary burden and average 180+ day detention timelines destroy project economics. For Section 232 exclusion denial recipients from the June 6 BIS batch, the 30-day reconsideration window and parallel CIT § 1581(i) filing option are live simultaneously — do not let the reconsideration period run without lodging a parallel judicial challenge given the *Capella Sales* adequacy-of-reasoning standard, which provides a viable administrative-law hook.
