# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Friday, July 10, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The critical date is fourteen days away.** 
The 10% Section 122 tariff that replaced IEEPA on February 24, 2026 has a hard sunset: it expires July 24, 2026.
 
The President cannot extend it unilaterally, Congress has not acted to extend it, and bipartisan appetite is uncertain with the "Reclaim Trade Powers Act" moving in the opposite direction.
 The replacement architecture is taking shape but is not final — and for importers and refund claimants alike, the decisions made in the next two weeks will define their recovery posture for the rest of the year.

**On the IEEPA refund front**, CBP's CAPE system is processing claims at scale but faces a deepening legal dispute over finally liquidated entries that — if it produces a Federal Circuit stay — could defer hundreds of millions in payments. **On the Section 122 front**, the Federal Circuit granted the government a stay of the CIT's invalidation ruling on June 11, meaning CBP continues collecting through the statutory sunset. **On the replacement-tariff front**, USTR's Section 301 forced-labor investigation produced affirmative determinations on June 2 covering 60 economies; a July 7 public hearing just closed, and the USTR completion deadline is July 20 — four days before Section 122 lapses. **Today**, USTR's comment period for Chinese-origin product tariff-relief requests under the new US-China Board of Trade closes. Importers who missed this window cannot retroactively seek inclusion.

### I. The Section 122 Clock, the Federal Circuit Stay, and the Refund Question


On May 7, 2026, in *State of Oregon, et al. v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. Trump*, the CIT held that the Trump administration's imposition of a 10% tariff pursuant to Section 122, to address "fundamental international payment problems," was unlawful because the Government's interpretation of the statute was overly expansive.
 
The case is *The State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026).
 
In a majority opinion by Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly, the CIT held that Section 122 is not a roving license to impose tariffs whenever imports exceed exports.
 
Specifically, the CIT held that Proclamation 11012's identification of a trade deficit, current account deficit, and net negative US international investment was not contemplated by the statute. The CIT explained that the statute's legislative history indicated that a "balance-of-payments deficit" can only be measured by liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance.


The remedy is narrow. 
The CIT's remedy is party-specific, not universal. The permanent injunction issued against the government's collection of the Section 122 tariffs extends only to the three importer plaintiffs who were found to have standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc. and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.



On June 11, 2026, the US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit issued a stay of the injunction issued by the CIT, which had prevented CBP from collecting duties pursuant to Section 122 from the State of Washington and two private importers.
 The CAFC's rationale is significant for any future Section 122 refund theory: 
although the CAFC explained that it did not "state a final conclusion as to the correctness of the CIT's judgment," the appellate court did explain that "the federal government has made a sufficient showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits."
 
The CAFC explained that the CIT's interpretation — that "balance-of-payments deficit" is limited to deficits measured by liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance — "may be incorrect," and that the legislative history cited by the Government "strongly call[s] into question" whether these methods were the only methods Congress intended.


The bottom line for Section 122 refund positioning: 
CBP will continue to collect these duties until July 24, 2026 (unless extended by Congress) and the outlook for refunds remains uncertain.
 The statutory sunset is faster than the appellate calendar. Whether the CAFC ever reaches the merits depends on whether a live controversy survives July 24 — which turns on whether Section 301 creates new duties (it does, for most of the 46 affected countries), preserving standing for the appeal. Importers who have paid Section 122 on entries that will not be covered by Section 301 post-July 24 face the greatest refund uncertainty.

### II. IEEPA Refund Pipeline: CAPE Progress and the Final-Liquidation Fight

The foundational order remains Judge Richard K. Eaton's March 4, 2026 ruling in *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 26-01259 (CIT). 
Judge Eaton ordered CBP to (1) liquidate any unliquidated entries that were entered subject to the IEEPA duties without regard to the IEEPA duties and (2) reliquidate (also without regard to IEEPA duties) any liquidated entries for which liquidation is not final. This means CBP must refund all IEEPA duties that have been collected.
 
Atmus Filtration was voluntarily dismissed on April 6, 2026
, and 
Judge Eaton immediately designated *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection* as the new lead case and, on April 7, issued an order mirroring the March 27 Atmus order.


The scale of the problem CBP disclosed to the CIT is instructive: 
more than 330,000 importers paid or deposited approximately $166 billion in IEEPA duties over more than 53 million entries.
 In response, 
on April 20, 2026, CBP launched the first phase of the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) tool in CBP's Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) portal, enabling importers or customs brokers to submit IEEPA duty refund claims.


As of the most recent verified data point — CBP Executive Director Brandon Lord's declaration filed June 10, 2026, in the *Euro-Notions* case — 
125,576 CAPE declarations have passed validation, meaning have been accepted.
 
Once CBP undergoes mass processing of an IOR's CAPE declaration, CBP estimates that unliquidated entry summaries will be set to liquidate 45 days from the CAPE declaration acceptance date. CBP anticipates valid IEEPA refunds for unliquidated entries will generally be issued within 60 to 90 days following acceptance of the CAPE declaration, unless a declaration warrants further CBP review for compliance concerns.


The most consequential unresolved issue is the finally liquidated entry dispute. 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump* clarifying two critical issues: CBP does not believe it has the authority to issue refunds of IEEPA tariffs that are both liquidated and finalized without a court order; and CBP intends to appeal the universal injunction issued by the CIT with respect to the IEEPA tariffs.
 The government's position, stated in the DOJ filing, was direct: 
the CIT "entered a universal injunction ordering CBP to reliquidate final entries including entries made by importers who have not sued in this Court," but the government argued that "the universal injunction exceeds the Court's jurisdiction and equitable authority," and that defendants intend to appeal and to seek a stay of the injunction except as to the particular importer plaintiffs in each case in which the Court has entered the injunction.


The *Euro-Notions* government appeal at the Federal Circuit has been filed as Case No. 26-1898 (Fed. Cir., filed June 2, 2026). 
CBP has stated that any importer whose entries have finally liquidated and who has not filed its own claim at the CIT will NOT be eligible for a refund unless and until there is a court order in favor of such importer to receive IEEPA refunds. CBP says this issue is on appeal and so until it is settled, CBP will not expand CAPE in this context.


Importers with finalized IEEPA entries who have not yet filed at the CIT face the most immediate deadline risk. 
Judge Eaton has pointed importers to the protest remedy under 19 U.S.C. § 1514. For those entries, importers are being directed to the protest mechanism for final liquidations. A § 1514 protest must be filed with CBP within 180 days of liquidation.
 The first IEEPA tariffs — on Canada, Mexico, and China goods entered on or about February 4, 2025 — have been in the 180-day window since early August 2025. Entries liquidated around January 2026 are reaching protest-deadline expiration now.

CAPE Phase 2 is also in motion: 
entries subject to reconciliation (but for which the reconciliation entry has not yet been filed) will be eligible for CAPE refunds during Phase 2, which begins on June 29, 2026.

### III. The Section 301 Replacement and What It Means for Net Refund Math


On June 2, 2026, the Office of the United States Trade Representative issued a press release announcing affirmative determinations in all 60 of its Section 301 forced labor investigations, concluding that every major U.S. trading partner has failed to adequately prohibit or enforce bans on imports of goods produced with forced labor. The 60 economies collectively account for 99.4% of all goods imported into the United States. The USTR has proposed additional tariffs ranging from 10% to 12.5% on imports.



On March 11, 2026, USTR initiated two new Section 301 investigations. The first targets excess manufacturing capacity in 16 economies: China, the EU, Mexico, Japan, India, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, and Bangladesh. The second targets forced-labor enforcement across 60+ economies, including the UK. Public comment closed April 15, and USTR is running an accelerated ~5-month timeline with a completion deadline of July 20, 2026 — four days before Section 122 lapses.



The proposal on the table is 12.5% Section 301 duties on 46 countries — including China, Vietnam, India, Thailand, Japan, and South Korea.
 
The proposed tariff actions are not final. The President retains final decision-making authority on tariff rates and country coverage, and President Trump may depart from USTR's proposed approach.


Two structural features matter for refund claimants:

First, Section 301 is not flat. 
Section 301 rates are product and country-specific rather than universal — your rate after July 24 may be higher or lower than 15% depending entirely on your HTS code and origin country.
 Importers who currently face a 10% Section 122 surcharge on non-China goods in categories that will be covered at 12.5% Section 301 will see a net increase in landed cost post-July 24.

Second, 
the proposed Section 301 tariffs carry a similar exemption structure to Section 122 — agricultural products, aviation parts, industrial inputs, pharmaceuticals, and goods already subject to Section 232 tariffs.
 USMCA-qualifying goods from Canada and Mexico remain exempt. 
EU countries are insulated: as of July 1, 2026, they pay the trade deal's 15% all-inclusive ceiling, which insulates them from both the Section 122 expiry and the Section 301 reset.


Today, July 10, 2026, is the deadline for comment submissions to USTR on Chinese-origin products that should receive tariff relief under the newly chartered US-China Board of Trade. 
Importers have until Friday, July 10, to submit comments to the USTR about Chinese-origin products that should receive tariff relief. Up to $30 billion in "non-sensitive" imports from China could see duty reductions under the newly chartered US-China Board of Trade, which will consider the comments collected by the USTR.

### IV. IEEPA Foundational Record: What the Supreme Court Actually Held

For completeness, and because the refund right flows directly from this ruling: 
in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, argued November 5, 2025, and decided February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's August 2025 ruling.
 
By a 6–3 vote, the Court agreed on the result, though the Justices differed in reasoning. Six Justices joined the majority holding that IEEPA does not permit tariff imposition, while Justices Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Alito dissented, concluding that IEEPA authorizes tariffs and that the President acted within delegated authority.
 
Following the ruling, the government ceased assessing and collecting importer deposits of IEEPA tariffs as of February 24, 2026.
 
The decision effectively invalidates billions of dollars in tariffs collected under IEEPA authority, potentially opening the door to an estimated $175 billion in refunds for affected businesses.



The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump Administration during the past year that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 Practically, that means: 
most Chinese goods still face 25–100% in Section 301 duties. The effective rate on Chinese electronics is approximately 35% (10% Section 122 + 25% Section 301), down from the previous 45% (20% IEEPA + 25% Section 301).

### V. Forced-Labor Scrutiny: China Supply Chain and the UFLPA Angle

A parallel pressure track is developing on the forced-labor enforcement front. SCMP reported this week on intensified scrutiny of Chinese worker protections as USTR weighs its forced-labor Section 301 determination — the affirmative findings issued June 2, 2026 now move toward final rates. 
USTR proposed two rates: 10% for economies that have made forced-labor import-prohibition commitments under the Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (ART) or have a forced-labor import prohibition in place but do not enforce it effectively; and a textile mechanism that would allow a certain volume of apparel and textile imports to enter at a reduced Section 301 rate.
 For importers with China-origin supply chains, the interaction of existing China Section 301 rates (25–100%), the expiring Section 122 (10%), and any new forced-labor Section 301 layer creates a stacking analysis that cannot be deferred past the July 20 USTR completion deadline.

## Recommended Actions

The next fourteen days compress the largest tariff-related action windows in recent memory. On the **IEEPA refund track**: any importer of record with entries that liquidated between approximately August 2025 and mid-January 2026 must audit protest deadlines under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 immediately — the 180-day clock is running against finalized entries, and CBP has stated it will not extend CAPE to that category without a specific court order; the *Euro-Notions* Federal Circuit appeal, Case No. 26-1898, is the vehicle to watch. Importers who have not yet submitted a CAPE declaration for eligible unliquidated or recent-liquidation entries should do so now — 
CBP estimates that unliquidated entry summaries will set to liquidate 45 days from CAPE declaration acceptance, with refunds generally issued within 60 to 90 days thereafter.
 On the **Section 122 refund track**: the CAFC's June 11 stay signals the Federal Circuit finds the government likely to succeed on the merits; do not treat the CIT's May 7 invalidation ruling as a refund basis absent active CIT litigation — 
importers should be prepared to consider filing a complaint at the CIT to seek refunds if the CAFC eventually sustains the CIT's initial opinion.
 On the **Section 301 transition**: map every HTS code now against USTR's proposed Annex A to identify gaps and exemptions before the July 20 determination locks rates; model three post-July 24 scenarios (Section 301 replacement, Section 122 expiry with no successor, congressional extension) and identify the landed-cost break-even for any sourcing-country switches. For China-origin goods, today's US-China Board of Trade comment deadline has passed — any missed exclusion requests cannot be retroactively submitted. 
The interaction between the IEEPA refund process and Section 122 duties — including whether Section 122 duties may be offset against IEEPA refunds — is an area to monitor.
