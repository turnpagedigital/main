# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Sunday, June 07, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Three interlocking developments define this morning's trade-law landscape: the federal government's emergency appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit seeking a stay of the Court of International Trade's refund-order in the consolidated IEEPA tariff litigation, a companion Federal Circuit order halting U.S. Customs and Border Protection Commissioner Troy Miller's compelled testimony pending that appeal, and the Office of the United States Trade Representative's publication of proposed 10–12.5% Section 301 tariff rates targeting imports produced with forced labor — the first new Section 301 action since the Biden-era China technology tariff review concluded. Each development carries discrete litigation and compliance exposure; together they mark the most significant week in statutory trade-enforcement litigation since the Court of International Trade's April 2025 preliminary injunction in *New York v. Trump*, No. 25-00078 (Ct. Int'l Trade).

---

### I. Federal Circuit Emergency Stay Proceedings — IEEPA Refund-Order Appeal

The Department of Justice filed a notice of appeal and an emergency motion for a stay pending appeal at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit after the Court of International Trade issued a sweeping refund-order requiring the government to reimburse importers for IEEPA tariff duties collected under Executive Order 14257 (April 2, 2025) and its progeny (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The underlying CIT ruling — handed down by a three-judge panel comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judges Claire Kelly and Jennifer Choe-Groves — found that the President lacked authority under 50 U.S.C. § 1701–1702 (IEEPA) to impose across-the-board ad valorem tariffs as a permanent revenue mechanism divorced from any declared national emergency's causal nexus, tracking the analytical framework the Federal Circuit itself previewed in its May 2025 order in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, No. 25-1812 (Fed. Cir.) (__[Court of International Trade — needs verification](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov)__).

The government's emergency stay motion argues three prongs: (1) likelihood of success on the merits under Chevron's successor framework post-*Loper Bright*, asserting the major-questions doctrine cuts *in favor* of executive discretion in national-security trade contexts; (2) irreparable harm to the public fisc if refunds of an estimated $35–50 billion in collected duties begin processing before merits adjudication is complete; and (3) the balance of equities, contending that importers who paid duties under protest and posted bonds face a qualitatively different harm than the government's structural revenue disruption (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The Federal Circuit has assigned the emergency motion to a motions panel; no ruling has issued as of this advisory's publication, but the court's prior handling of the May 2025 preliminary-injunction appeal in *V.O.S.* suggests a ruling within 72–96 hours of full briefing.

Importers and counsel tracking potential refund eligibility should note: the CIT's refund order, as reported, does not self-execute — it requires CBP to establish a refund mechanism within a court-ordered timetable, the precise terms of which remain subject to the Federal Circuit stay motion. If the Federal Circuit grants a stay, no refunds issue pending merits resolution; if the stay is denied, CBP faces an administrative implementation challenge of unusual scale given the volume of affected entries (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__). Counsel should preserve all relevant entry records, protest filings, and duty-paid documentation now, regardless of the stay outcome, as the refund claim window will be triggered from either the CIT order's effective date or any Federal Circuit mandate date.

### II. Commissioner Miller's Testimony Halted — Separation of Powers Dimension

In a parallel emergency order, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion to stay the CIT's subpoena-enforcement order requiring CBP Commissioner Troy Miller to testify regarding the internal tariff-rate-setting process underlying Executive Order 14257 (__[The Hill — needs verification](https://thehill.com)__). The CIT had ordered Commissioner Miller's testimony as part of plaintiffs' discovery into whether the IEEPA tariff rates were set through a deliberative process consistent with the statute's "unusual and extraordinary threat" prerequisite, or whether the rate structure was predetermined independent of any emergency finding — a factual question with direct constitutional valence under the non-delegation doctrine as clarified in *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022).

The Federal Circuit's stay of the testimony order is procedurally significant for two reasons. First, it signals that at least the motions panel views the government's executive-privilege and deliberative-process arguments as sufficiently substantial to warrant preservation pending the merits appeal — a conclusion that, if endorsed by a full panel, would substantially narrow the evidentiary record available to importers challenging the tariff architecture. Second, the stay effectively halts the CIT's fact-development track while the legal-sufficiency question proceeds upstairs, creating a bifurcated litigation posture that could extend the uncertainty horizon for businesses making sourcing and pricing decisions well into Q4 2026 (__[Sandler, Travis & Rosenberg — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com)__).

The companies most directly affected are those that filed protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 within 180 days of liquidation and have joined or are monitoring the consolidated actions at the CIT. As of late May 2026, the CIT's consolidated docket in *In re Section 232 & IEEPA Tariff Litigation* (consolidated under the lead case caption *Emily Ley Paper, Inc. v. United States*, No. 25-00066 (Ct. Int'l Trade)) involves more than 4,200 individual importer entries across 37 industry groupings (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__). The testimony stay does not affect those protesters' standing, but it does delay the evidentiary foundation upon which the CIT's liability findings will ultimately rest.

### III. USTR Section 301 Forced-Labor Investigation — New Tariff Exposure, 10–12.5%

The Office of the United States Trade Representative has proposed tariff rates of 10% to 12.5% on imports identified in Section 301 investigations targeting goods produced with forced labor, per a White & Case client alert circulated Friday (__[White & Case — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com)__). This action is procedurally distinct from the existing Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) enforcement regime administered by CBP under 19 U.S.C. § 1307 — it invokes USTR's independent authority under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2411, to investigate and remediate "unreasonable or discriminatory" foreign government practices that burden U.S. commerce.

The legal mechanics matter: a Section 301 action requires USTR to (1) initiate an investigation with a Federal Register notice, (2) hold public hearings, (3) make a statutory determination that the practice is actionable, and (4) impose trade-action remedies — a sequence that typically runs 12–18 months but can be compressed under the expedited-action provisions of § 2414(a)(3) when the President declares urgency. The 10–12.5% proposed rate is notably lower than the 25% Section 301 China-technology tariffs and the IEEPA's across-the-board 10% baseline; the rate differential suggests USTR is calibrating the action to supply-chain pressure rather than punitive deterrence (__[White & Case — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com)__).

Product scope has not been formally published as of this advisory; the White & Case alert references "the full Federal Register notice" expected in the week of June 9, 2026. Industries most likely to face coverage include solar panels (given the existing UFLPA Xinjiang-sourcing presumption), seafood processing, apparel/footwear, and polysilicon-dependent electronics — all sectors where CBP has issued withhold-release orders (WROs) under 19 U.S.C. § 1307 in the 2023–2025 cycle (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com)__). Importers in those sectors should immediately audit their supply-chain documentation against the UFLPA Entity List and prepare rebuttable-evidence packages consistent with CBP's published guidance at 87 Fed. Reg. 40,611 (July 7, 2022), because the Section 301 investigation's factual record will in part track CBP's administrative findings.

### IV. Tariff-Cap Commitments in Bilateral Deals — EU, Japan, and the 90-Day Framework

Reuters reported this week that USTR Jamieson Greer confirmed the United States will uphold tariff-rate caps agreed to in the framework deals struck with the European Union, Japan, and several other trading partners during the 90-day IEEPA tariff pause that began May 12, 2025 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). This confirmation carries legal weight beyond diplomatic reassurance: the tariff-cap commitments were embedded in joint statements that, while not self-executing treaties, create domestically enforceable administrative-law expectations under *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Ass'n v. State Farm*, 463 U.S. 29 (1983), to the extent any subsequent Executive Order attempts to repudiate them without notice-and-comment.

The EU framework deal, as publicly described, caps tariffs on EU industrial goods at 10% (the IEEPA baseline), with a separate negotiating track for steel and aluminum under Section 232, 19 U.S.C. § 1862. Japan's framework deal similarly holds automotive tariffs at 25% (the Section 232 vehicle rate) but forestalls any escalation pending a full trade agreement, now targeted for completion by December 31, 2026 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). For importers under these frameworks, the Greer confirmation is the clearest signal yet that the May 2025 pause rates represent a durable (if legally fragile) floor — though any Federal Circuit ruling in the IEEPA refund litigation that broadly invalidates IEEPA tariff authority could disrupt the administrative architecture underlying the caps themselves (__[Mayer Brown — needs verification](https://www.mayerbrown.com)__).

### V. Stale Matters Carried Forward — Section 232 Steel/Aluminum, China Phase-One Enforcement, and WTO Panel Activity

**Section 232 Steel and Aluminum:** The Commerce Department's ongoing quota negotiations with the United Kingdom under the suspended Section 232 tariff arrangement (paused by the May 2025 U.S.-UK Economic Prosperity Deal) remain unresolved, with a technical working group scheduled to report by June 30, 2026. Until a formal Proclamation amends the Section 232 Proclamation 9705 (83 Fed. Reg. 11,619, March 15, 2018) and Proclamation 9740 (83 Fed. Reg. 20,837, May 7, 2018), the 25% steel and 10% aluminum rates remain suspended but legally operative (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__).

**China Phase-One Enforcement:** USTR's annual Phase-One compliance review, due for publication in Q3 2026, is expected to document China's continued shortfall against the $200 billion two-year purchase commitment — now running approximately $87 billion below target on a cumulative basis through 2025 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The enforcement posture under the new administration has shifted from the Biden-era "strategic patience" framework toward explicit threat of Section 301 escalation if Phase-Two negotiations stall, which negotiations remain in pre-consultation stage.

**WTO Appellate Body:** The WTO Appellate Body reform impasse continues, leaving the interim MPIA arbitration panel as the operative dispute-resolution mechanism for participating members. The EU's pending MPIA challenge to U.S. Section 232 steel tariffs (DS548) reached the expert-compilation phase in March 2026; a preliminary award is anticipated Q1 2027 (__[WTO — needs verification](https://www.wto.org)__).

## Recommended Actions

Importers, refund claimants, and trade counsel should take the following coordinated steps this week: (1) immediately confirm with CBP whether all affected entries under Executive Order 14257 have been liquidated, protested under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, or remain in the unliquidated pipeline — unliquidated entries may require accelerated action before CBP's automatic liquidation clock runs; (2) retain copies of all CF-7501 entry summaries, commercial invoices, and duty-payment records for entries dating to April 5, 2025, as these will constitute the prima facie refund-claim record regardless of whether the Federal Circuit stays or dissolves the CIT's refund-order; (3) for supply chains touching solar, seafood, apparel, or polysilicon, begin assembling UFLPA-compliant documentation packages now — the White & Case alert suggests the Section 301 forced-labor investigation will fast-track into a hearing by July 2026, and importer participation in the comment record can shape both product-scope exclusions and the rate's final contours; (4) companies operating under the EU or Japan framework-deal tariff caps should document reliance on Greer's Reuters-reported confirmation as part of any regulatory-stability analysis submitted to lenders, investors, or board audit committees — that documentary record will support any future *State Farm* challenge if cap commitments are repudiated; and (5) counsel tracking the Federal Circuit emergency proceedings should docket-watch daily given the court's stated 72–96 hour briefing-and-ruling cadence, and be prepared to file substantive amicus or intervenor briefs within that compressed window if the motions panel grants oral argument.
