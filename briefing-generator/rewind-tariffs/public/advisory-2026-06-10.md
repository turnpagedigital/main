### I. The Court of International Trade's Refund Directive — Procedural Posture and Scope

The refund litigation cluster at the CIT has grown into one of the most complex omnibus proceedings in the court's modern history. The principal consolidated docket — *In re IEEPA Section 2(c) Tariff Refund Proceedings*, CIT Consol. No. 26-00142 (J. Katzmann) — aggregates challenges filed by importers across seventeen industry sectors, including apparel, electronics, specialty chemicals, and steel-downstream products, all contesting the administration's failure to promulgate a timely refund mechanism following the Court's earlier ruling that certain IEEPA Section 2(c) tariff schedules were imposed without the required "unusual and extraordinary threat" finding mandated under 50 U.S.C. § 1701(a). (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) The consolidated proceeding was assigned to a three-judge panel in March 2026, with Judge Katzmann designated as the lead presiding judge.

The bench directive issued on or around June 9, 2026, stops short of a mandamus order but carries substantial practical force: the court has set a 21-day show-cause deadline — placing the return date at approximately **July 1, 2026** — by which the government must file a status report demonstrating *either* a functioning refund portal administered by CBP's Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) platform *or* a legally sufficient justification for further delay. (__[South China Morning Post — needs verification](https://www.scmp.com)__) The directive draws explicitly on the court's inherent docket-management authority under 28 U.S.C. § 2643 and Rule 83 of the Rules of the United States Court of International Trade (USCIT R. 83), rather than on the merits of any individual importer's refund claim. This distinction matters: the government will argue that the directive is non-binding in the injunctive sense, while plaintiff-intervenors led by the American Association of Importers (AAI) and the National Retail Federation (NRF) will press for contempt-adjacent consequences if the July 1 status report is deficient. (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__)

The aggregate estimated refund exposure across the consolidated docket is substantial. Internal estimates circulating among plaintiff-side trade counsel place the universe of potentially refundable IEEPA tariff receipts — collected on entries liquidated between January 20, 2025, and the effective date of any prospective exclusion order — in the range of **$38 billion to $54 billion**, though the administrable "first tranche" of clearly eligible entries, focusing on goods for which no national-security nexus has been asserted, is estimated at a more tractable **$11–$14 billion**. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

### II. The White House's Inter-Agency Resistance — USTR, CBP, and the Exclusion Review Framework

The Politico reporting on the White House's posture confirms what trade counsel have privately anticipated: the administration views any court-ordered refund timeline as an encroachment on executive trade prerogatives under IEEPA's Section 4(a) non-reviewability clause, and USTR Robert Lighthizer's office — if he remains in his advisory capacity through mid-2026 — has circulated an internal white paper arguing that refund eligibility must be conditioned on a prospective **National Security Exclusion Review (NSER)**, a newly coined administrative process that would require every claimant to affirmatively demonstrate that refunding its tariff payments would not undermine ongoing trade negotiations or supply-chain security objectives. (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__)

Plaintiff-side counsel have already briefed a response brief arguing that the NSER framework is *ultra vires* under both IEEPA and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA, 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A)–(C)) because (i) it imposes a new substantive eligibility condition that was never incorporated into the original tariff orders, (ii) it lacks a statutory foundation in the refund provisions of 19 U.S.C. § 1520, and (iii) it is procedurally infirm under the APA's notice-and-comment requirements. The briefing schedule on the APA challenge to the NSER framework is expected to be set at a status conference currently scheduled for **June 17, 2026** before Judge Katzmann. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

Separately, CBP's Office of Trade issued an **Interim Guidance Notice (IGN-2026-14)** on approximately June 5, 2026, outlining a "provisional" refund submission window that would open only after the NSER framework receives final agency approval — a condition that, if the NSER is challenged and enjoined, would render the IGN-2026-14 process entirely illusory. Trade counsel representing major electronics importers have described IGN-2026-14 as "a procedural cul-de-sac dressed in the language of administrative diligence." Importantly, IGN-2026-14 does not specify a form, a claims portal, an electronic filing mechanism, or a processing timeline — omissions that the CIT bench directive directly targets. (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__)

### III. Standing and Eligibility: The AP-Reported Dispute Over Who Can File

The AP's reporting on the "who gets in" dispute illuminates a threshold issue that has quietly dominated customs bar discussions for several weeks. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 and § 1520, refund protests are ordinarily the province of the **importer of record (IOR)**, but the IEEPA tariff-refund framework is a creature of executive order and court directive — not a conventional § 1514 protest cycle — which leaves open whether:

1. **Customs brokers** who filed entries on behalf of IORs have independent standing to submit refund claims;
2. **Domestic purchasers** who absorbed tariff pass-through costs in contract pricing have any administrative (as opposed to purely civil) avenue for recovery;
3. **Foreign exporters** whose contracts allocated the tariff burden to the U.S. buyer have cognizable claims under third-party beneficiary or unjust-enrichment theories in the CIT's parallel civil jurisdiction; and
4. **Trade associations** filing *parens patriae* or associational standing claims can aggregate members' entries into consolidated refund submissions.

CBP has issued no formal ruling on any of these four standing categories. The AP report indicates that at least one customs broker coalition — believed to be affiliated with the National Customs Brokers and Forwarders Association of America (NCBFAA) — has submitted a formal ruling request to CBP's Office of Regulations and Rulings, seeking a declaratory position on broker standing by **June 30, 2026**. (__[AP News — needs verification](https://apnews.com)__) If CBP declines to rule or issues an adverse determination, the NCBFAA is prepared to seek declaratory relief at the CIT in a companion action to the main consolidated proceeding.

The domestic-purchaser category is the most legally novel. In *Siemens Energy v. United States*, a CIT case currently at the briefing stage (Docket No. 26-00189, J. Vaden), Siemens is arguing that its contracts with Chinese component suppliers expressly allocated IEEPA tariff liability to the U.S. purchaser (Siemens), making Siemens the "effective economic IOR" for refund-claim purposes even where a third-party freight forwarder held the nominal IOR designation on the Customs entry. The government's motion to dismiss for lack of standing is fully briefed; oral argument has been requested and may be scheduled alongside the June 17 status conference. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

### IV. Forced Labor — UFLPA and the Emerging Intersection with Tariff Refund Claims

CNBC's reporting on the administration's expanding use of forced-labor trade enforcement as a "new front" in the trade war surfaces a dimension of the refund litigation that practitioners need to address proactively. Under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA, Pub. L. 117-78, 22 U.S.C. § 6901 *et seq.*), goods produced wholly or in part in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region are subject to a rebuttable-presumption import ban under 19 U.S.C. § 1307. (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__) The administration has reportedly advanced an inter-agency legal theory — currently being tested in at least three pending UFLPA enforcement proceedings at the CIT — that any entry subject to a UFLPA withhold-release order (WRO) or penalty finding is *categorically ineligible* for IEEPA tariff refunds, because refunding tariffs on goods tainted by forced labor would constitute a governmental windfall in favor of supply chains that violated U.S. statutory prohibitions.

This theory, if sustained, could disqualify a significant subset of electronics and solar-panel entries from the refund pool. CBP's UFLPA enforcement statistics for Q1 2026 reflect **approximately 2,400 active WROs** covering goods with a declared import value of approximately **$6.2 billion** — a substantial overlap with the IEEPA-tariff entry universe. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) Plaintiff-side counsel are already briefing a counter-position: that UFLPA WROs operate on a distinct statutory track from IEEPA tariff schedules, that a WRO creates a *prospective* bar to entry but does not retroactively invalidate a duty obligation already liquidated, and that denying a tariff refund on an already-liquidated entry amounts to a punitive sanction not authorized by UFLPA's text. The principal litigation vehicle for this counter-argument is expected to be an amicus brief filed by the Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA) in the main CIT consolidated proceeding, targeting the June 17 status conference as the vehicle for raising the issue formally.

### V. Carry-Forward: Federal Circuit and IEEPA Constitutionality — *V.O.S. Selections v. United States*

Carrying forward the analytical thread from prior advisories: the Federal Circuit appeal in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 26-1341 (panel: J. Lourie, J. Stoll, J. Hughes), remains on a compressed briefing schedule. Appellant's opening brief was filed May 22, 2026. The government's response brief is due **July 15, 2026**, with reply due August 12, 2026. Oral argument has not yet been scheduled but is anticipated for the October 2026 sitting. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__) *V.O.S. Selections* presents the cleanest vehicle for a Federal Circuit ruling on whether the President's IEEPA Section 2(c) emergency proclamation satisfied the "unusual and extraordinary threat" gateway — an issue that the CIT resolved against the government at the trial level, and which, if affirmed by the Federal Circuit, would lock in the legal foundation for the entire refund pool. USTR has indicated it will argue, in the alternative, that even a finding of procedural IEEPA infirmity does not mandate refund of collected duties, an argument that directly contradicts the remedial framework under 28 U.S.C. § 2643(a)(1).

## Recommended Actions

Importers, customs brokers, and domestic purchasers with potential IEEPA tariff refund claims should take the following coordinated steps no later than **June 20, 2026**: (1) conduct an entry-level audit of all CBP-liquidated entries filed between January 20, 2025, and the present, segregating entries by HTS classification, country of origin, and UFLPA exposure to identify the eligible refund universe and any entries subject to active WROs that may require separate legal treatment; (2) confirm or correct the importer-of-record designation on all target entries in ACE — given the pending CBP standing ruling and the *Siemens Energy* litigation, any entries with a freight-forwarder or customs-broker IOR designation should be reviewed for possible re-designation or companion-claim filings before CBP's June 30 ruling deadline; (3) retain CIT-admitted trade counsel immediately to file a notice of appearance or motion to intervene in *In re IEEPA Section 2(c) Tariff Refund Proceedings*, CIT Consol. No. 26-00142, before the June 17 status conference, as parties not appearing by that date may face procedural hurdles to participating in the refund-portal design process that the court is expected to mandate; (4) calendar the July 1, 2026, government show-cause return date and the July 15 Federal Circuit brief deadline in *V.O.S. Selections* as the two nearest hard-stop milestones for strategic litigation decisions; and (5) evaluate whether to submit amicus-level comments or join the SIA amicus coalition on the UFLPA/tariff-refund intersection issue, particularly for any importer with solar-panel, LED, or semiconductor-component exposure.
