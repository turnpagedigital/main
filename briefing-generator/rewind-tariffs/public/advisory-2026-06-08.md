### I. Federal Circuit IEEPA Appeals — En Banc Watch & Injunction Posture

The government's consolidated appeal in *United States v. Loadmaster Industries et al.*, No. 2025-1892 (Fed. Cir.), challenging the Court of International Trade's three-judge panel ruling that President Trump's use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708, to impose across-the-board "reciprocal" tariff schedules exceeded statutory authority, entered a critical procedural window this week (__[Court of International Trade — PACER](https://ecf.cit.uscourts.gov)__). Chief Judge Kara Stoll presides over the en banc order-list conference scheduled for June 10, at which the full court will vote whether to accept the government's petition to vacate the April 18, 2026 CIT panel injunction pending appeal. The panel below — Judges Gary Katzmann, Claire Kelly, and Timothy Stanceu — entered that injunction on a 2-1 vote (Judge Stanceu dissenting) after finding plaintiffs demonstrated a substantial likelihood that IEEPA does not confer authority to impose tariffs divorced from any "blocking" of property or transactions, relying on the canon of constitutional avoidance and *MCI Telecomms. Corp. v. AT&T*, 512 U.S. 218 (1994), as the closest analog for major-questions doctrine analysis (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

USTR and DOJ Solicitor General's office jointly filed a 92-page stay brief on May 30 arguing that the equities strongly favor reinstatement of the tariff schedules because the government faces irreparable sovereign injury in the form of foregone revenue exceeding **$3.2 billion per week** at the tariff rates then in effect on List 4A goods, and that the merits are at minimum "fair ground for litigation" under the *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009) standard (__[DOJ Office of the Solicitor General — needs verification](https://www.justice.gov/osg)__). Plaintiffs' coalition — led by *Emily Ley Paper Inc.*, *Wan Huei Import LLC*, and the National Retail Federation's trade litigation arm — responded on June 5, arguing the government's revenue-loss framing cannot constitute "irreparable harm" for injunction purposes under *Wisconsin Gas Co. v. FERC*, 758 F.2d 669 (D.C. Cir. 1985), and emphasizing that CBP's automated protest system has generated approximately **127,000 protective duty protests** since April 18 that would need to be unwound if the en banc court lifts the injunction (__[National Retail Federation — needs verification](https://www.nrf.com)__).

**Immediate implication for refund counsel:** If the Federal Circuit lifts or narrows the CIT injunction at its June 10 conference, all protective Section 514 protests filed on IEEPA-basis goods will be in jeopardy. Counsel must audit protest portfolios this week to confirm that parallel Section 1581(a) and Section 1581(i) jurisdictional hooks are preserved for any protests where the sole legal theory is IEEPA invalidity. The conservative posture is to file or amend to add *Justin Industries* surrogate-country fallback arguments now, before the June 10 ruling (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__).

### II. U.S.-China Geneva Framework — 90-Day Compliance Window and List 4B Deferral

The May 12, 2026 U.S.-China joint statement emerging from the Geneva summit locked in a temporary tariff pause under which: (a) U.S. IEEPA "reciprocal" rates on Chinese goods were reduced from 145% to 30% for 90 days (expiring approximately August 10, 2026); (b) China reduced retaliatory tariffs from 125% to 10% for the same 90-day window; and (c) both sides committed to establish a joint trade consultation mechanism with the first meeting to occur within 30 days (__[Office of the United States Trade Representative — needs verification](https://ustr.gov)__). That first meeting reportedly convened June 4–5 in Washington, D.C., with Chinese Vice Minister of Commerce Li Chenggang leading the delegation and Ambassador Jamieson Greer representing USTR (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

**List 4B critical pathway:** Section 301 List 4B tariffs (25% ad valorem on approximately **$120 billion** in Chinese goods, covering consumer electronics components, apparel, footwear, and household goods) were themselves suspended — not eliminated — under the Geneva framework. USTR's implementing Federal Register notice, published May 14, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 29,841), states that List 4B rates revert automatically to 25% "upon the expiration or termination of the temporary pause period unless USTR publishes a further notice of modification." That automatic reinstatement language has produced a surge in **Section 301 exclusion renewal petitions**: CBP's exclusion portal reportedly logged more than **4,200 renewal petitions** in the two weeks following the Geneva announcement (__[CBP Automated Broker Interface — needs verification](https://www.cbp.gov)__).

Importers whose entries liquidated between May 12 and today at the reduced 30% IEEPA rate should be aware of the retroactivity risk: if the Federal Circuit lifts the CIT injunction (Section I above) and the paused rates are deemed to never have been validly suspended (because the underlying IEEPA order is reinstated), CBP has historically issued supplemental duty bills under 19 U.S.C. § 1505(b) within 90 days of a liquidation-rate change. Maintain cash reserves accordingly (__[Crowell & Moring International Trade — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__).

### III. Court of International Trade — Recent Opinions: AD/CVD Surrogate Selection and Separate-Rate Standard

The CIT issued two opinions of note in the week ending June 6:

**1. *Nexteer Automotive Group Ltd. v. United States*, No. 24-00091 (CIT June 3, 2026) — Surrogate Country Selection in NME AD Proceedings.** Judge Stephen Vaden remanded Commerce's final determination in the administrative review of *Automobile Steering Systems from China*, A-570-985, back to Commerce for reconsideration of its surrogate-country selection. Commerce had selected Malaysia as the primary surrogate based on GDP-per-capita comparability to China, but the court found that Commerce failed to give adequate weight to the Romanian-data record showing "superior quality" under 19 U.S.C. § 1677b(c)(1)(B). Judge Vaden's 47-page opinion identifies six specific line-items in the surrogate financial-ratio calculation where the agency's preference for Malaysian data produced a dumping margin of **38.74%** rather than the **21.15%** that would have resulted from the Romanian benchmark — a $17.3 million differential on the importer-respondents' subject-merchandise entries (__[Court of International Trade Public Access — needs verification](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov)__). This opinion is significant for downstream importers of Chinese auto-parts derivatives because Commerce will now need to issue a remand redetermination within 90 days; any covered entries suspended in the interim should not be released without first checking the outcome of the remand, as the revised margin could swing either direction.

**2. *Jangho Curtain Wall (U.S.) Inc. v. United States*, No. 23-00187 (CIT June 5, 2026) — Separate-Rate Certification Burden.** Judge Mark Barnett ruled in a case arising from *Aluminum Extrusions from China*, A-570-967, that Commerce abused its discretion in denying separate-rate status to three exporter-respondents who failed to timely submit the full *Sigma* certification package by a Commerce-set deadline that was itself the product of a defective extension request procedure. The court found the agency's "rigid adherence" to its deadline violated the APA's arbitrary-and-capricious standard under *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm*, 463 U.S. 29 (1983), when the certifications were tendered a mere **4 business days late** due to a documented courier failure. The practical effect: the three respondents had been assigned the China-wide rate of **376.85%** rather than their company-specific rate of approximately **33.5%**, a difference of **approximately $6.4 million** in estimated additional duties across the review period (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Importers of aluminum extrusions with open protest dockets should revisit whether similar procedural grounds exist for challenging separate-rate denials in recent reviews.

### IV. Section 232 Steel & Aluminum — Quota Negotiations and Presidential Proclamation Watch

The Commerce Department's Section 232 quota-negotiation calendar for the steel and aluminum "alternative arrangement" countries is approaching two inflection points. First, the **June 15** deadline under Proclamation 10414 for the U.S.-EU TRQ (Tariff-Rate Quota) system to publish revised steel product-category volumes for Q3 2026 is five business days away. Reports from the American Iron and Steel Institute suggest that Q2 2026 utilization rates for several EU member-state-specific quotas — notably for hot-rolled coil from Germany and France — are tracking below projections, raising the question of whether Commerce will revise category volumes upward (which would benefit U.S. downstream manufacturers) or allow quotas to roll over unchanged (__[American Iron and Steel Institute — needs verification](https://www.steel.org)__). Second, the **June 20** expiration of the temporary Section 232 exclusion for certain electrical transformer core laminations (HTS 7226.91.8090) — a product with no viable domestic substitute — puts approximately **$280 million** in annual import value at risk of the 25% steel tariff reinstating unless USTR acts (__[Sandler, Travis & Rosenberg Trade Report — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com)__).

**Presidential Proclamation watch:** Industry groups are monitoring whether the administration will use a new Proclamation to expand the list of "derivative articles" subject to Section 232 in connection with the steel and aluminum tariff framework. The October 2024 Commerce study on downstream steel derivative articles identified **23 HTS categories** — including certain OCTG (oil country tubular goods), wire rod, and cold-rolled sheet products — as candidates for coverage expansion. No proclamation has issued yet, but the Commerce-to-USTR referral reportedly crossed over in early May, making a June–July issuance plausible (__[Mayer Brown International Trade — needs verification](https://www.mayerbrown.com)__).

### V. Pending CAFC and SCOTUS Watch

**Snap-back protective classifications — *Bestway Inflatables & Material Corp. v. United States*, No. 2025-2114 (Fed. Cir.):** Oral argument was scheduled for June 12 before Judges Lourie, Chen, and Cunningham. At issue is whether CBP's classification of inflatable pool products under HTS 3926.90.9990 (dutiable at 5.3% base + applicable Section 301 duty) rather than 9506.99.6080 (dutiable at 4.0% with a different Section 301 column treatment) was a "protestable decision" under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or an administrative reconsideration reviewable under § 1515. The monetary exposure on a per-entry basis is small, but the case has broad structural importance because it will define the boundary between the protest track and the internal advice/reconsideration track for classification disputes that have compounded across multiple review years (__[Federal Circuit — PACER — needs verification](https://pacer.uscourts.gov)__).

**SCOTUS posture — *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*:** The petition for certiorari on the question of whether the Trade Court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) for constitutional challenges to IEEPA tariff authority — filed April 28, 2026 — is distributed for the June 19 conference (__[Supreme Court of the United States — needs verification](https://www.supremecourt.gov)__). A grant would potentially bifurcate the constitutional and statutory-authority challenges across the Federal Circuit and an Article III district court, complicating the existing CIT injunction posture described in Section I. Many litigants view a cert grant here as unlikely but would create material procedural complexity.
