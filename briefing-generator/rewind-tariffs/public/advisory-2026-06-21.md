## Analysis & Developments

### The Decisive Fault Line: DOJ's Federal Circuit Appeal Has Landed — and $30B+ Hangs on It

The litigation has crossed a threshold. 
On June 3, the Government filed its appeals in the Federal Circuit, again pressing its universal-injunction argument.
 The appeal consolidates the government's challenge to Judge Richard K. Eaton's refund orders in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, Case No. 25-595 (CIT), the operative lead vehicle since *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States*, Case No. 26-1259 was abandoned after the plaintiff withdrew its complaint. 
On March 27, 2026, following the Supreme Court's decision in *Learning Resources v. United States*, 607 U.S. __ (2026), the CIT ordered the government to refund all IEEPA tariff payments, including those for finally liquidated entries. The order was first entered in *Atmus Filtration v. United States*, Case No. 26-1259. After the plaintiff in Atmus withdrew its complaint, the CIT on April 7 entered a materially similar refund order in *Euro-Notions Florida v. United States*, Case No. 25-595. This transfer of the order to a new case pushed back the 60-day window for DOJ to appeal and may have materially increased the number of "finally liquidated" entries as a result.


The government's core Federal Circuit argument is narrow but consequential: 
the Government will rely heavily on *Trump v. CASA*, 606 U.S. 831 (2025), arguing that Judge Eaton's orders are impermissible universal injunctions because they extend relief beyond the named plaintiffs. The Government also likely will argue that any importer has an adequate remedy by filing its own Section 1581(i) action.
 Importers who cleared that threshold are in a materially different posture. 
On June 3, the Government filed its appeals in the Federal Circuit, again pressing its universal-injunction argument. Then, at the June 9th hearing, a CBP representative stated that CAPE Phase 3 — covering, among other things, finally liquidated entries — will begin processing refunds, but only for importers that filed Section 1581(i) actions.


The dollar figure at stake in the "finally liquidated" dispute is not trivial. 
The most immediate issue involves "finally liquidated" entries, with the U.S. Government arguing it can retain $30 billion or more in unlawfully collected IEEPA tariffs.
 The broader concern extends to 
other categories outside CAPE Phase 1, including reconciliation entries and entries involving antidumping or countervailing duty (AD/CVD) issues, which may also face delayed, uncertain, or disputed refund treatment depending on how CAPE Phases 2 and 3 develop and the full arguments the DOJ chooses to raise on appeal.

### CAPE Pipeline: Phase 2 Launches June 29; Phase 3 Targeted Late July — But Each Phase Carries Its Own Exclusion Risk

The refund machinery is functioning at scale for CAPE Phase 1 entries. 
EAC Thomas reported at the June 9 hearing that Phase 1 of CAPE has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, and that approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund. To underscore the scale of this effort: in all of last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type. In just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP has processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


The pipeline now turns to Phase 2. 
CAPE Phase 2 (reconciliation and antidumping/countervailing duty entries) is set to launch June 29, 2026, and Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries — available only to importers who have filed at the CIT) is on track for late July 2026.
 Phase 2's scope, however, is more limited than it appears on its face. 
Phase 2 will not cover all reconciliation entries. Entries filed before roughly May 31, 2025, that liquidated on a standard 314-day cycle will have liquidated more than 80 days before June 29, 2026, and fall outside Phase 2's scope. Importers with reconciliation entries from the earliest months of the IEEPA tariff period should not assume Phase 2 will capture those entries and should plan accordingly.


Phase 3 carries the government's most consequential position: 
the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT. Approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected, if CBP proceeds as planned, to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined, regardless of liquidation status.
 Non-filers face a bifurcated risk: delays at minimum, permanent non-recovery of finally liquidated entries at worst. 
Importers that have filed Section 1581(i) protective actions are best positioned to recover their full IEEPA refunds. Importers that have not filed are likely, at a minimum, to face significant delays while the appeals process unfolds. Depending on the ultimate outcome, they may not recover certain refunds at all.

### The Class-Certification Gambit in *V.O.S. Selections* and What It Means for Non-Filers

A potentially significant procedural development landed on June 4. 
Terry Precision Cycling, a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) case, moved in *V.O.S. Selections* to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows for class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response is due June 25, 2026.


The legal theory is architecturally clever. The government's *CASA*-based attack on universal injunctions concedes that class actions retain their traditional reach — a class certified under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23 (or its CIT analog) can bind a defined universe of claimants even after *CASA*. If Judge Eaton certifies the class before the Federal Circuit addresses the universal-injunction appeal, it could thread the needle and preserve broad refund access for non-CIT filers. The government's response due June 25 will be worth reading closely; any indication that the government also intends to attack class certification on the merits would be a materially adverse signal.

Separately, 
the government separately appealed the order requiring Commissioner Scott to appear, and the Federal Circuit promptly stayed that order, leading Judge Eaton to modify the order to permit other CBP representatives to appear instead. Meanwhile, on June 4, the parties in *V.O.S. Selections* briefed whether the stay of compliance should remain in place. The government again argued that the refund orders were impermissibly universal, while plaintiffs disagreed but acknowledged that immediate full compliance was not necessary while the appeal is pending.

### Section 122 Surcharge: 33 Days to Hard Expiration — Federal Circuit Stay Holds, Section 301 Replacement Imminent

The Section 122 10% global surcharge (Proclamation No. 11012, HTSUS 9903.03.01) is on a 33-day countdown. 
On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel of the CIT held that President Trump exceeded the statutory authority under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 when he imposed the administration's temporary 10% "balance-of-payments" tariffs.
 The case caption is *State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026). 
The 2-1 majority, comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly, held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted and, instead, improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.


The injunction, however, remains administratively stayed. 
On May 8, the day after the CIT's opinion, the government appealed to the Federal Circuit. On May 12, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion for an immediate administrative stay such that the CIT's permanent injunction is temporarily stayed while the appellate court considers a more prolonged stay. While the Federal Circuit grapples with whether to issue a stay pending appeal, Section 122 duties will continue to be collected on plaintiffs' entries and non-plaintiff importers prior to the July 24th expiration date.


The practical upshot for most importers is that the stay means collection continues. 
The permanent injunction issued against the Section 122 tariffs extends only to the three importer plaintiffs found to have standing — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.


Section 122's July 24, 2026 hard expiration is not a relief event. 
Section 122 has functioned as a legal bridge following the Supreme Court's invalidation of IEEPA tariffs in February 2026. Ongoing Section 301 investigations into foreign industrial overcapacity are expected to produce replacement tariffs that could take effect on or around the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs. The practical burden on importers is likely to continue in some form regardless of how the Section 122 litigation ultimately resolves.
 Specifically, 
the U.S. Trade Representative is conducting two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations, which are expected to conclude before the Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026.

### The Emerging Private-Party Dispute: Who Keeps the Refund When It Arrives?

A new layer of litigation risk is crystallizing beneath the government-vs.-importer frame. 
As CBP continues implementing refunds through the CAPE process and related reliquidation procedures, a new issue is beginning to emerge. For many companies, the most significant disputes associated with IEEPA refunds may no longer involve the government at all. Instead, they may involve customers, suppliers, distributors, shareholders, bankruptcy stakeholders, and other private parties seeking to claim a portion of the resulting recoveries.


The legal mechanics of this dispute turn on the pass-through analysis. 
The importer of record is the entity that initially pays all tariffs and thus is the entity that would receive any IEEPA tariff refunds. Nonetheless, behind the scenes there often are a variety of mechanisms importers of record may have used to handle the often unexpected tariffs, including pushing back on suppliers for price concessions, using surcharges to pass along tariffs, or generally increasing price. In many cases, other parties may be looking to share any potential refunds.


For companies in restructuring, this framing intersects directly with claims trading, plan confirmation, and distribution disputes. The importer of record may have filed for bankruptcy between February 2025 and today; the refund receivable may now be property of the estate under 11 U.S.C. § 541, contested by both the debtor and any counterparties who bore the economic burden of the tariff. Expect contract-review motions and adversary proceedings in active Chapter 11 cases as CAPE Phase 1 disbursements arrive.

## Recommended Actions

For GCs, compliance officers, and CFOs tracking IEEPA refund recovery: if your company has not filed a 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) protective action in the CIT, the June 3 Federal Circuit appeal filing makes that decision urgent — CAPE Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries, targeted late July 2026) will, per CBP's own June 9 testimony, process refunds only for CIT-filers, and the government is arguing it can retain $30 billion or more from non-filers; file before the Federal Circuit resolves the universal-injunction question, because any adverse ruling could permanently bar your recovery on finally liquidated entries. For Phase 2 entries (reconciliation and AD/CVD), confirm your entry dates: entries predating roughly May 31, 2025 that liquidated on a standard 314-day cycle likely fall outside Phase 2 and require independent action. For Section 122 duties paid under HTSUS 9903.03.01 from February 24 through July 24, 2026, begin documenting and segregating all payments now — the CIT ruled the surcharge unlawful on May 7, the Federal Circuit administrative stay is pending, and the USTR's 76-determination Section 301 replacement package is expected before July 24, so the refund math on Section 122 will follow the same contested-liquidation pattern as IEEPA; preserve your protest rights under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 at the 160–170 day post-liquidation mark. For companies that passed tariffs through to customers, customers, suppliers, or distributors via price surcharges, begin the contract-review analysis now — CAPE disbursements are arriving, and third-party claims to those receivables are beginning to materialize; engage restructuring counsel if the company is also in or near bankruptcy. Finally, monitor the government's June 25 response to Terry Precision Cycling's class-certification motion in *V.O.S. Selections* — if the government attacks class certification on the merits rather than conceding the procedural exception, that is a materially adverse signal for non-filer recovery.
