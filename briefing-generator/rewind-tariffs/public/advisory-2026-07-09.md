## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: Fifteen Days to Zero — Section 122 Sunset, Section 301 Hearings Now Live, and the Refund Clock Running in Parallel

The trade landscape this morning turns on a single countdown: 
the 10% global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) is scheduled to expire by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 That is fifteen days from today. Three developments converge this week. 
USTR's public comment period on the proposed forced-labor Section 301 tariffs closed Monday, July 6; public hearings on the matter opened Tuesday, July 7.
 Separately, 
importers have until Friday, July 10 — tomorrow — to submit comments to USTR about Chinese-origin products that should receive tariff relief under the newly chartered US-China Board of Trade, which will consider up to $30 billion in "non-sensitive" imports from China for potential duty reductions.
 And in parallel, the $175 billion IEEPA refund pipeline — through CBP's CAPE mechanism — remains open for entries carrying codes 9903.01.xx and 9903.02.xx, entirely separate from the Section 122 regime.

### Section 122 Appellate Posture: Federal Circuit Stay Intact, Sunset Supersedes Injunction


On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the CIT, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 The cases carry CIT docket numbers 26-01472 and 26-01606, Slip Op. 26-47. 
The 2–1 majority, comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly, held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted and, instead, improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 
Judge Stanceu dissented; in his view, the majority read too much into legislative history, resolved the dispute prematurely on an expedited record, and should have allowed further proceedings before entering summary judgment.


The government moved immediately. 
On May 8, 2026, the day after the CIT's opinion, the government appealed to the Federal Circuit. On May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion for an immediate administrative stay, temporarily pausing the CIT's permanent injunction.
 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion for a stay pending appeal, allowing the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 import surcharges — including from the three importer plaintiffs who had previously won relief: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.


The practical consequence: 
the surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality. The CIT held the surcharge unlawful, and the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, so CBP has continued to collect the duty on entries through July 23. The July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period; the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the appellate outcome.


That is the critical point for refund claimants. 
The previous IEEPA cases provide a useful analog: following the Supreme Court's ruling on the IEEPA tariffs, the CIT issued an order directing nationwide refunds of unlawful IEEPA tariffs, including through liquidation of unliquidated entries and reliquidation of certain liquidated entries — even for importers that were not parties to the litigation.
 Whether that model extends to Section 122 duties will depend on the Federal Circuit's ruling on the merits. 
The fact that the CIT did not grant a universal injunction as part of its Section 122 opinion may indicate that — even if the CIT's decision is upheld on appeal — further litigation could be required for additional importers to obtain tariff refunds.

### Section 301 Architecture: Hearings Open, Rates Proposed, Clock Synchronized


On June 2, 2026, USTR determined under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 that the acts, policies, and practices of 60 economies related to the failure to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor are unreasonable and burden or restrict U.S. commerce, and are thus actionable under Section 301(b) of the Trade Act.
 The rate structure: 
for economies that impose a forced labor import prohibition, or that have committed to impose and enforce such a prohibition, USTR proposes 10% additional duties; for all other economies, 12.5%.



The proposed action breaks down as follows: a 10% Section 301 tariff for 15 trading partners — including Canada, Ecuador, the EU, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, El Salvador, Guatemala, Malaysia, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom — and a 12.5% rate for the remaining 45 partners, including China, India, Vietnam, Thailand, Turkey, and South Korea.
 
Separately, on June 1, 2026, USTR announced a new Section 301 investigation on intellectual property protection and enforcement in Vietnam, and on May 29, 2026, announced a new investigation on forced labor practices there.
 
Brazil faces a separate Section 301 investigation with a proposed 25% tariff — appearing intended to stack on the 12.5% forced labor rate, for a potential combined rate of 37.5% on Brazilian imports.



USTR likely intends to be ready to impose the tariffs under this forced-labor action by the time the 10% global Section 122 tariff expires on July 24, 2026.
 
Section 301 has no statutory rate cap and no time limit — which is exactly why the administration chose it.
 Treasury Secretary Bessent has publicly predicted tariffs will return to pre-SCOTUS levels by August 2026. The administration's sequencing — Section 122 as a 150-day bridge, Section 301 as the permanent replacement — was explicit from the start: 
this two-stage approach enabled rapid tariff implementation under Section 122 while using the 150-day window to develop and implement Section 301 tariffs as longer-term trade measures.


One structural difference importers must absorb: 
the shift from Section 122 to Section 301 is not a simple swap — the architecture is meaningfully different. Section 122 was flat: one rate, applied globally.
 Section 301 tariffs are country- and sector-specific, which means landed-cost modeling must be rebuilt from scratch for each origin and product category. 
The USTR notice does not address whether these Section 301 tariffs would "stack" on other Section 301 tariffs currently being developed; absent contrary guidance, the default rule is that tariffs stack on each other.

### IEEPA Refund Pipeline: CAPE Mechanics, Liquidation Backlog, and the Section 122 Firewall

The foundational ruling remains *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 24-1287, consolidated with *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, No. 25-250. 
The Supreme Court released its decision on February 20, 2026. The majority opinion, written by Chief Justice Roberts and joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson, stated that IEEPA does not give the president the power to set tariffs.
 
As a result, IEEPA duties ceased collection starting February 24, 2026. On March 4, 2026, Judge Richard K. Eaton of the CIT ruled in *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* that CBP must refund IEEPA duties on all unliquidated entries for all importers. The decision did not fully address liquidated entries, with the court ordering reliquidation and refunds only for those entries where liquidation is not yet final.


The refund universe is estimated at $175 billion. 
The decision effectively invalidates billions of dollars in tariffs collected under IEEPA authority, potentially opening the door to an estimated $175 billion in refunds for affected businesses.
 The CBP vehicle is CAPE. 
On February 22, 2026, CBP issued CSMS #67834313 announcing that it would stop assessing IEEPA duties on goods entered for consumption or withdrawn from warehouse. However, the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) continued liquidating previously filed entries with assessed IEEPA tariffs even after the Supreme Court's decision in *Learning Resources*. This creates practical complications for importers monitoring liquidation status and protest deadlines.


The Section 122 firewall is non-negotiable: 
CAPE covers only IEEPA tariffs — duties collected under codes 9903.01.xx and 9903.02.xx. Section 122 was imposed under entirely different statutory authority and is not part of the IEEPA refund program. There is no announced refund mechanism for Section 122 duties. If entries from late February 2026 onward show 9903.03.xx codes, those duties cannot be recovered through CAPE.


The protest deadline under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 governs liquidated entries. For unliquidated entries, Post-Summary Corrections are the preferred vehicle. 
Importers should still consider filing a Post-Summary Correction in respect of unliquidated entries, or protests for liquidated entries, to advance their place in line for refunds.
 ACE continues to liquidate entries; each liquidation triggers the 180-day protest clock under § 1514, and entries liquidated without refund of IEEPA duties must be protested within that window or the claim is forfeited.

### Background: Structural Precedent and Successor Tariff Risk


The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump administration that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 
China's effective rate in July 2026 stands at 10% Section 122 plus 25–100% Section 301, for an effective rate of 35–110%.
 Post-July 24, once Section 122 drops, China-origin goods revert to existing Section 301 rates only — which is actually a reduction for many categories — until new Section 301 tariffs under the forced-labor and overcapacity investigations take effect. That gap, however narrow, is the window.


Congress has not passed extension legislation, and bipartisan support is uncertain — some members have introduced the "Reclaim Trade Powers Act" aimed at constraining presidential tariff authority rather than extending it.
 Congressional extension requires affirmative action and a 60-vote Senate threshold; it is not the base case.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and refund claimants face three parallel deadlines this week and next. **First**, submit any comments to USTR on the US-China Board of Trade before tomorrow, July 10 — this is the last opportunity to seek relief on Chinese-origin goods before that record closes. **Second**, by July 23, catalog every open entry carrying HTS codes 9903.03.xx (Section 122) with entry number, entry date, liquidation status, and duty amount; the July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward, but potential refund rights on already-paid duties under *Oregon v. United States* (CIT Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606) depend entirely on preserving the claims record now — no protest, no recovery if the Federal Circuit eventually rules for plaintiffs. **Third**, for IEEPA entries (9903.01.xx / 9903.02.xx), confirm CAPE filing status and check ACE daily for liquidation events; each liquidation without IEEPA-duty refund starts the 180-day § 1514 protest clock. Separately, model landed cost under both the Section 301 forced-labor proposal (10% or 12.5% by country, stacking on base MFN and existing China Section 301) and the overcapacity investigation's successor rates — Section 301 carries no rate cap and no expiration, so the July 24 drop from 10% Section 122 may be brief. Companies with high-volume sourcing from Vietnam, India, Thailand, South Korea, or Brazil are at greatest risk of rate escalation above current Section 122 levels.
