## Analysis & Developments

### The 11-Day Window That Reshapes the Entire Refund Landscape

The single most consequential development this morning is the one happening on the calendar rather than in a docket: 
the 10% global import surcharge President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2132, is scheduled to expire by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24. Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending. Absent congressional action, the surcharge terminates on its own terms.
 As of today, July 13, the clock stands at eleven days. That sunset is legally separate from the Federal Circuit appeal — but both threads converge on July 24 in ways that require immediate attention.

The operative question for every importer this week is what replaces the Section 122 surcharge the moment it expires. That answer is largely determined by whether USTR can finalize Section 301 tariff determinations before the 150-day window closes — a race that, based on the administrative record, appears likely to produce at least a partial Section 301 framework on or around July 24, but with material residual rate uncertainty for 46 countries. 
USTR faces a July 20, 2026 completion deadline on the two Section 301 investigations it launched March 11 (excess manufacturing capacity in 16 economies; forced-labor enforcement across 60+), and the proposal on the table is 12.5% Section 301 duties on 46 countries — including China, Vietnam, India, Thailand, Japan, and South Korea. Section 301 has no statutory rate cap and no time limit, which is exactly why the administration chose it.
 The refund math changes materially depending on whether the Section 301 overlay arrives on July 24 or experiences even a brief gap: 
if Section 301 tariffs are not in place by that date, imports would revert to pre-IEEPA duty rates — MFN plus any existing Section 232 and prior Section 301 tariffs on China.

### CAPE Phase 3 and the DOJ Appeal: The Divided Importer Universe

The IEEPA refund pipeline continues to move, but at different speeds for different classes of importers — and the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal has made that divergence structural, not temporary.


According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 The Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system launched April 20, 2026 has processed Phase 1 at significant scale: 
CBP has processed more than 16 million entries under Phase 1, accepted more than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA refunds for processing, and of that total, roughly $22 billion has moved to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for disbursement.
 Phase 2 — covering reconciliation-flagged entries (types 01, 02, and 06) where the reconciliation entry has not yet been filed — 
became effective June 29, 2026, accepting entries limited to those unliquidated or within 80 days of liquidation.


Phase 3 is the contested frontier. 
Phase 3 of CAPE will be ready by the end of July 2026. CBP confirmed that timeline at the June 9, 2026 hearing before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton in V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States, Court No. 25-00066.
 But technical readiness and legal authorization are different things. 
The federal government continues to contest the legal basis for refunds on finally liquidated entries, so it remains unclear whether refunds will actually flow once the technical capability is in place.


The DOJ's position is explicit and aggressive. 
In its June 4 court filing, DOJ stated that "Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final," and that to make refunds available for such entries, "an importer must file suit in this Court, and the Court must enter an importer-specific order."
 
On June 2, 2026, the DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's Refund Order; DOJ contests the CIT's authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries that are both liquidated and past the 80-day window in which CBP can reprocess them. The DOJ previewed its arguments in its May 29, 2026 court filing.
 The companion appellate docket is 
Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States, CIT Case No. 25-00595, now on appeal at the Federal Circuit as Case No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026.



The government's appeal has divided the importer universe into two groups: those who filed at the CIT and those who have not. For importers in the latter category, the risk is no longer theoretical. The government's current position is that importers who did not file at the CIT may wait indefinitely — and perhaps permanently — for refunds of finally liquidated entries.
 The statutory clock sharpens that risk: 
the CIT's authority to hear tariff refund cases has a two-year time limit. The deadlines for importers whose entries were among the earliest to be hit with IEEPA duties (starting in early 2025) will begin to expire in early 2027. Importers who adopt a "wait and see" approach risk losing their right to seek a refund entirely if the two-year window closes before they file suit.


Practically, for CAPE filers, 
importers and authorized brokers should anticipate that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.
 One critical operational wrinkle that took effect last week: 
warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on CAPE Declarations effective July 7, 2026. Any warehouse entry submitted on or after that date will be rejected with an "ENTRY TYPE NOT ALLOWED" error.

### Section 122 Litigation: Federal Circuit Stay, CIT Ruling, and the Refund Hypothetical

The Section 122 surcharge has its own appellate track, and the posture as of today matters for a distinct category of potential recovery: duties paid under Proclamation 11012 (HTSUS 9903.03.xx) from February 24 through July 23, 2026.

The CIT's ruling came down on May 7, 2026. 
The U.S. Court of International Trade held that Proclamation 11012, which imposed a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122, was invalid — in State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606.
 
In a 2–1 decision, the CIT majority comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted, and instead improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 Judge Stanceu dissented.

The remedy is narrow. 
The permanent injunction issued against collection of the Section 122 tariffs extends only to the three importer plaintiffs who were found to have standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.


The Federal Circuit then stayed even that limited relief. 
On June 11, 2026, the CAFC granted the federal government's motion to stay the CIT's injunction, allowing the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 surcharges from the plaintiffs who had previously won relief — including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. The court concluded that three of the four stay factors favored the government, with the fourth neutral.
 The practical result: 
while the ruling calls into question the legal basis for the Section 122 surcharge, the surcharge remains in effect and CBP continues to collect it, because the CAFC's administrative stay suspends the CIT's injunction.


The Section 122 surcharge and IEEPA CAPE are separate programs with no overlap. 
CAPE covers only IEEPA tariffs — duties collected using HTS codes 9903.01.xx and 9903.02.xx. Section 122 was imposed under entirely different statutory authority and is not part of the IEEPA refund program. There is no announced refund mechanism for Section 122 duties. If your entries from late February 2026 onward show 9903.03.xx codes, those duties cannot be recovered through CAPE.

### Section 301 Replacement Architecture: Rate Proposals, Hearing Record, and Finalization Timeline

The administration's longer-term tariff rebuilding effort moved significantly through its administrative process last month and now stands at the post-hearing rebuttal phase.


On June 2, 2026, USTR announced proposed tariffs under Section 301 following an investigation into alleged forced labor related policies and practices of 59 countries and the European Union. The proposed tariff rate is 10% for most goods of 15 trading partners, and 12.5% for most goods of 45 trading partners.
 
USTR accepted written comments through July 6, 2026, and held a public hearing on July 7, 2026.
 That hearing record is now closed, and USTR is in the post-hearing rebuttal window.

The separate excess-manufacturing-capacity investigation, initiated March 11, 2026 against 16 economies including China, EU, Japan, India, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan, and South Korea, runs on a parallel track. 
July 24, 2026 is the unofficial target date for completion of the investigations and remedy determinations, according to USTR and President Trump. USTR has stated that it intends to conduct these investigations on an expedited basis, aiming to complete the process and be prepared to impose tariffs by around July 24, 2026.



The hearing date in July 2026 closely tracks the expiration date of the Section 122 tariff action, and the proposed rates would provide continuity after the end of the Section 122 tariff action.
 However, there is an important carve-out: 
EU goods moved to the trade deal's 15% all-inclusive ceiling on July 1, insulating them from both the Section 122 expiry and the Section 301 reset.


On the Brazil front, 
USTR has proposed applying tariffs of 25% on all goods from Brazil under a separate Section 301 investigation into digital trade, electronic payment services, unfair tariffs, and anticorruption enforcement, with certain exemptions.
 The Germany pharmaceutical track is also newly active: 
USTR initiated a Section 301 investigation into Germany's persistent underpayment for innovative pharmaceutical products on June 18, 2026.


For companies sourcing from Vietnam specifically: 
on May 29, 2026, USTR initiated a new Section 301 investigation of the acts, policies, and practices of the Government of Vietnam related to intellectual property protection and enforcement
 — running concurrently with Vietnam's exposure on the forced-labor and excess-capacity tracks. The compound exposure across three simultaneous investigations for Vietnam-origin goods warrants immediate review.

### Refund Mechanics: Framework Agreements, Interest, and the Protest Deadline Clock

Two operational points that practitioners frequently misjudge:

First, on refund calculation under CAPE for goods from framework-agreement countries (EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland/Liechtenstein): 
the framework agreements required that Column 1/General Duty be paid along with IEEPA duties, such that the combined duty rate equaled 15%. The total 15% could be reported on a single line on the entry summary filing. Importers will not receive a refund of the total 15%, because a portion of that 15% accounts for the Column 1/General Duty rate, which remains in effect.
 Importers who paid a blended 15% rate and are expecting full restitution should recalibrate.

Second, interest runs. 
Refunds reflect the difference between amounts paid and adjusted duties, plus statutory interest accrued under 19 U.S.C. § 1505(c) from the date of deposit through the date of refund.
 For entries going back to February 2025, accrued interest is material and should be modeled into recovery projections. And for CAPE filers with entry errors: 
if you know the underlying entry has errors, fix those errors first via Post Summary Correction before filing the CAPE Declaration. CBP has strongly inferred it is using CAPE data for enforcement purposes, and a CAPE entry for an incorrect entry could cause more hardship than correcting the entry first.

## Recommended Actions

With eleven days to the Section 122 sunset and the USTR Section 301 hearing record now closed, the critical path for clients this week is threefold: **(1) IEEPA refund posture —** any importer with entries that liquidated more than 80 days before the Phase 1 CAPE launch date and who has not filed an individual lawsuit at the CIT (Court No. 25-00066 track) should evaluate whether filing before early 2027 is warranted — the two-year statutory bar under 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i) means the earliest 2025 entries begin expiring in early 2027, and the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal in Case No. 26-1898 makes the non-filer position increasingly risky; separately, any warehouse-entry filer who submitted a Type 21 or 22 CAPE Declaration between April 20 and July 6 without a corresponding withdrawal submission must file a corrective CAPE Declaration covering the withdrawals themselves before reliquidation; **(2) Section 122 recovery optionality —** companies that paid the 10% surcharge on 9903.03.xx entries from February 24 through today should preserve 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest rights now for entries within the protest window, even though no formal Section 122 refund mechanism exists, so that if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's Slip Op. 26-47 ruling on appeal, the administrative record supports recovery; and **(3) Section 301 transition planning —** the post-hearing rebuttal window for the forced-labor investigation is either closing or closed as of today, so any company that failed to build a company-specific record faces a significantly weaker position to seek exclusions; firms with Vietnam, India, Thailand, Japan, or South Korea exposure should immediately map HTS-level exposure against Annex A exclusions, and model the delta between a 12.5% Section 301 stack, existing Section 232 rates, and the pre-IEEPA MFN rate — the Section 301 framework carries no rate cap and no expiration, so what is proposed at 12.5% is a statutory floor, not a ceiling.
