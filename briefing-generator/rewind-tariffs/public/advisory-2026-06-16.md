### I. IEEPA Refund Litigation: CAPE Phase 2 Launch and the Class-Certification Gambit

On February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court held — 6-3, in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* — that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, affirming the Federal Circuit's prior ruling. The downstream implementation question is now unfolding rapidly before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton at the CIT.

Subsequent proceedings required CBP to develop a scalable refund process applicable not only to litigants but also to non-plaintiffs. According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries. In response, CBP developed the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system as an electronic, consolidated refund mechanism within ACE. CBP launched Phase 1 of CAPE on April 20, 2026, limited in scope to certain unliquidated entries and entries liquidated within 80 days of the CAPE submission date.

Since CAPE Phase 1 launched, more than 16 million entries have been processed; more than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA duty refunds have been accepted for processing; and approximately $22 billion in refunds have been completed and sent to the Treasury Department for disbursement.

At the June 9 hearing, CBP specified that additional progress has been made on CAPE Phase 2 addressing reconciliation entries, which will launch on June 29, 2026. At that same hearing, a CBP representative testified that CAPE Phase 3 entries — a category including finally liquidated entries — will start being processed for refunds, though CBP repeated its earlier argument that such entries could only be processed for importers that have filed Section 1581(i) actions before the CIT.

That contested position has generated parallel class-action maneuvering. On June 4, Terry Precision Cycling, a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) case, moved in *V.O.S. Selections* to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows for class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response is due June 25, 2026.

The mandamus front has resolved: the U.S. Government withdrew its petition for a writ of mandamus at the Federal Circuit, and on June 9, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the Government's request to withdraw the petition and dismissed the mandamus proceeding in *In re United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 26-144, before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton. DOJ had filed that separate mandamus petition seeking Federal Circuit intervention to prevent CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott from being compelled to testify at the CIT. Following the Federal Circuit's involvement — including a stay of Judge Eaton's order compelling Commissioner Scott's testimony — on June 5, 2026, the CIT amended its order to permit substitute testimony. Though the mandamus petition is gone, the underlying dispute about whether the CIT has authority to order relief extending beyond the parties before the court remains central to the government's merits appeal.

### II. Section 122 Front: Federal Circuit Stay Puts 10% Surcharge Back in Play

The administration's Section 122 tariff posture remains the most acute near-term risk for import counsel. On February 20, 2026, following the Supreme Court's decision striking down the IEEPA tariffs in *Learning Resources*, President Trump issued Presidential Proclamation 11012, imposing a 10% surcharge on all imports from all countries, other than certain specified products, effective February 24, 2026 through July 24, 2026.

On May 7, 2026, the CIT ruled in two companion cases that the tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2132, was unlawful. The consolidated cases are *State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026). The CIT's majority found the administration's interpretation of Section 122 impermissibly expansive: both plaintiff groups alleged that the President's 10% surcharge was an unlawful invocation of Section 122, arguing that changes to the international economic system — including the U.S. move away from the gold standard — made it impossible in 2026 to have the kind of "balance-of-payments deficits" contemplated by the 1975 legislation. The 2-1 decision resulted in a permanent injunction prohibiting the collection of Section 122 duties, but only with respect to the prevailing plaintiffs: Burlap & Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington.

The decisive move this week: on June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion for a stay pending appeal, pausing enforcement of the CIT injunction on the collection of Section 122 duties against the State of Washington and two private businesses — Burlap and Barrel, Inc. and Basic Fun, Inc. The CIT's judgment had declared Proclamation No. 11012 "invalid as contrary to law" and ordered permanent injunctive relief for those plaintiffs, but declined to enter a universal injunction. The Federal Circuit's willingness to grant the stay — which requires a showing of likely success on the merits — signals meaningful appellate skepticism of the CIT's narrow balance-of-payments analysis.

The Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026 in any event, absent congressional reauthorization, meaning the Federal Circuit will need to move expeditiously on briefing if it intends to issue a merits ruling before the statute's 150-day window closes. The tight timeline structurally disadvantages importers seeking universal relief via this vehicle, and reinforces the importance of preserving protest rights and CIT filing positions independent of the *Burlap and Barrel* outcome.

### III. USTR Section 301 Forced-Labor Overlay: 60 Economies, $0 in Effect Yet, $X Billion in Potential Exposure

On March 12, 2026 — less than three weeks after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs in *Learning Resources* — USTR initiated 60 parallel Section 301 investigations (against 59 countries and the European Union) for forced labor enforcement failures.

On June 2, 2026, USTR issued its determination in those 60 Section 301 investigations concerning whether trading partners prohibit and effectively police imports made wholly or in part with forced labor. USTR found the conduct of each investigated economy actionable and proposed a new tariff overlay: additional duties of 10% or 12.5%, depending on the economy, subject to product and program-specific exclusions.

Under the proposal, a 10% additional duty rate would apply to economies that impose a forced labor import prohibition, have committed to do so through a reciprocal trade agreement, or have established a partial regime to prevent such imports, while a 12.5% rate would apply to all other investigated economies. Of the 60 economies investigated, 54 have neither imposed nor effectively enforced a forced labor import prohibition. The remaining six — Canada, Ecuador, the European Union, Indonesia, Mexico, and Pakistan — maintain prohibitions but have failed to enforce them effectively.

The tariff action includes some exemptions under Annex A for certain agricultural products, aviation parts and equipment, industrial inputs, minerals, pharmaceutical goods, and goods subject to separate tariffs under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962.

If finalized, the tariffs may offer a more process-heavy — but potentially more legally durable — alternative to emergency tariff authorities, provided USTR develops a reasoned record, responds to significant comments, and connects the remedy to its statutory findings.

The procedural calendar is critical: June 22, 2026 is the deadline for requests to appear at the public hearing; July 6, 2026 is the deadline for written comments; July 7, 2026 is the first day of public hearings, which will continue over subsequent days if needed. USTR identified docket number USTR-2026-0265 for written comments and rebuttal comments, and USTR-2026-0266 for requests to appear at the hearing. The duties are not in effect yet, but for companies that import, source, price, or move goods across borders, it is important to start measuring exposure now, while the comment record is still open.

### IV. Replacement Tariff Architecture: Section 15% Global + Section 301 China Overlay + June 11 U.S.-China Deal

The post-IEEPA tariff architecture has stabilized into three concentric layers. The current framework spans duties under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 addressing China's purported unfair trade practices, duties under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 related to national security, and a 15% global tariff in effect from February 24, 2026, announced after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs.

On the China bilateral: on June 11, Trump announced a trade deal with China that would leave in place the current 20% "fentanyl" and 10% "reciprocal" tariffs (for a total of 30%), pausing higher tariffs for 60 days. Due to the multitude of existing duties, the effective tariff rate on many Chinese goods shipped to the U.S. remains close to 30% — still the highest of any country.

As of June 2, 2026, USTR also proposed a new additional tariff of 12.5% on China as a result of a separate Section 301 investigation into forced labor, meaning that if the forced-labor tariff proposal is finalized, Chinese goods would face a Section 301 forced-labor overlay on top of the existing 30% combined rate — a cumulative exposure that supply-chain counsel and procurement teams must begin stress-testing immediately.

On March 11, 2026, USTR also initiated Section 301 investigations into 16 economies' acts, policies, and practices relating to structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors, establishing yet another investigatory docket that could generate additional tariff actions in Q3/Q4 2026.

## Recommended Actions

Import-side counsel and in-house trade teams should attend to three parallel tracks with tight calendar pressure this week. First, on IEEPA refund recovery: confirm that all Phase 1-eligible CAPE declarations have been submitted or are in queue, and begin preparing entry-level documentation for Phase 2 (launching June 29) covering reconciliation entries filed under 19 U.S.C. § 1484b and AD/CVD-overlay entries — Post Summary Corrections may no longer be used to request IEEPA refunds and CAPE is the exclusive mechanism; clients whose entries fall outside Phase 1 should verify whether a Section 1581(i) CIT complaint is needed to access Phase 3 finally-liquidated-entry processing, given CBP's contested position that such entries are only available to CIT filers. Second, on Section 122 exposure: with the Federal Circuit's June 11 stay restoring collections for *Burlap and Barrel* and *Basic Fun* plaintiffs and the statute expiring July 24, 2026, non-plaintiff importers who paid Section 122 duties should file CBP protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 now to preserve refund rights contingent on the Federal Circuit's merits ruling — the ruling does not automatically extend relief to other importers, and protest deadlines run 180 days from liquidation. Third, on the Section 301 forced-labor overlay: submit written comments to USTR docket USTR-2026-0265 by July 6, 2026, prioritizing Annex A exclusion requests for products with demonstrably clean supply chains, while simultaneously modeling the cumulative rate exposure if the 10% or 12.5% overlay stacks on top of existing Section 301, Section 232, and 15% global duties — particularly for Chinese-origin goods that could face a combined rate exceeding 40% once the forced-labor overlay is finalized.
