## Analysis & Developments

**Today's Delta:** The most consequential development of the past 72 hours in the IEEPA refund docket is the Federal Circuit's June 9 dismissal of the government's mandamus petition in *In re United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 26-144 — clearing the immediate procedural fight over CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott's compelled testimony, while the government's substantive merits appeal of the CIT's April 17 universal refund injunction remains very much alive. Simultaneously, the USTR's June 2 Section 301 forced-labor proposed tariff action — covering 60 economies at tiered rates of 10% or 12.5% — advances on a comment-and-hearing schedule that tracks precisely to the July 24, 2026 expiration of the stopgap Section 122 global import surcharge, making the next six weeks a critical policy inflection window for importers across virtually every supply-chain sector.

### I. *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* — CIT Refund Docket: Mandamus Dismissed, Merits Appeal Pending, Class Certification Motion Filed

The IEEPA refund litigation has entered its most procedurally complex phase yet. The U.S. Government withdrew its petition for a writ of mandamus at the Federal Circuit, and on June 9, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the Government's request to withdraw and dismissed the mandamus proceeding in *In re United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 26-144, before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton. The DOJ had previously appealed the CIT's April 17, 2026 order issuing a universal injunction directing CBP to refund IEEPA duties, and in addition had filed the separate mandamus petition seeking Federal Circuit intervention to prevent CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott from being compelled to testify at the CIT regarding CBP's anticipated timing for complying with refunds.

Following the Federal Circuit's involvement — including a stay of Judge Eaton's order compelling Commissioner Scott's testimony — the CIT on June 5, 2026 amended its order to permit substitute testimony. The Federal Circuit's dismissal of the mandamus petition removes the immediate procedural fight over Commissioner Scott's testimony, but does not resolve the underlying refund dispute, because the Government's substantive appeal of the CIT's April 17 universal refund injunction remains pending. Although framed as a dispute about compelled testimony, the mandamus petition had also teed up broader arguments about whether the CIT has authority to order relief extending beyond the parties before the court — an issue that remains central to the Government's merits appeal.

On the substantive appeal: On May 29, 2026, the federal government notified the CIT in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, that it intends to appeal the CIT's universal injunction requiring CBP to issue refunds of IEEPA tariffs. On June 2, 2026, the Trump administration formally appealed the CIT's universal injunction, which had directed CBP to reliquidate certain entries that had already been "finally liquidated," without applying IEEPA tariffs. The government's appeal targets the scope of the CIT's remedial authority — not the underlying determination in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* — and if successful, could result in narrowing of relief for nonplaintiff importers with finally liquidated entries. The government's prior concessions, the CIT's jurisdictional posture, and the Uniformity Clause are among the legal issues to be addressed on appeal.

A critical new plaintiff-side offensive move emerged on June 4: Terry Precision Cycling, a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) case, moved in *V.O.S. Selections* to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows for class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response is due June 25, 2026. If the class is certified, it would create an alternative pathway to refunds for importers with finally liquidated entries who did not file their own CIT suits — directly blunting the government's appeal strategy.

The appeal does not halt the refund process. CBP continues to process CAPE submissions and has already refunded approximately $85 billion in IEEPA duties on unliquidated and nonfinal entries. The universality question — which is the crux of the Federal Circuit merits appeal — turns on whether the prohibition on universal injunctions articulated in *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, 606 U.S. 831, 865 (2025), applies to the CIT. Judge Eaton's order in *Euro-Notions Florida* concluded that the CASA prohibition does not apply to the CIT because its statutory authority differs from that of ordinary federal district courts. Whether this reasoning survives appellate review remains to be seen — the Federal Circuit previously vacated the CIT's universal injunction in *V.O.S. Selections* and remanded for reconsideration in light of CASA, though that remand was directed at the original merits-phase injunction, not the post-judgment refund order now at issue.

### II. The Section 122 Cliff and the Section 301 Forced-Labor Tariff Regime: The Post-IEEPA Architecture

The administration's broader tariff-replacement strategy has accelerated materially in the past two weeks. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, invalidating all IEEPA tariffs under review. In response, President Trump promptly issued new executive orders imposing tariffs under alternative statutory authorities. The short-term statutory replacement — Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — carries a hard sunset. USTR is widely expected to finalize replacement tariffs prior to July 24, 2026, when the 10% global tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act is set to expire.

The primary replacement vehicle is now the Section 301 forced-labor investigation. On June 2, 2026, USTR published a Federal Register notice announcing determinations and proposed actions arising from 60 investigations authorized under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, into the alleged failure of governments to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor. The proposed tariff rate is 10% for most goods of 15 trading partners and 12.5% for most goods of 45 trading partners. The proposed Section 301 action follows the invalidation of the IEEPA-based tariffs and is intended to serve as a more durable platform for broad-based tariffs than Section 122 or IEEPA.

USTR initiated the forced labor Section 301 investigation on March 12, 2026 as part of a broader strategy to rebuild the IEEPA-based tariffs that the Supreme Court ruled were unlawful. The public is invited to provide written comments by July 6, 2026 on the proposed actions, and USTR will hold hearings on July 7, 2026. The timing is precise and intentional: the hearing date in July 2026 closely tracks the expiration date of the current Section 122 tariff action, and the proposed rates are designed to provide continuity after the end of that action.

Legal durability of the Section 301 vehicle is not assured. USTR determined that all 60 jurisdictions are acting "unreasonably" and burdening or restricting U.S. commerce by not imposing or effectively enforcing forced labor import prohibitions — a conclusion already subject to legal and factual criticism. The notice does not address whether these Section 301 tariffs would "stack" on other Section 301 tariffs that USTR is currently developing. Absent contrary guidance, the default rule is that tariffs stack. For importers currently operating under Section 301 China tariffs, the stacking concern is acute: USTR proposes to impose a 25% tariff on imports from Brazil, which appears intended to "stack" on the 12.5% tariff proposed under the forced labor investigation, for a cumulative rate of 37.5%.

A parallel Section 301 investigation into Vietnam — announced March 29, 2026, targeting unfair trade practices and IP protection, following Vietnam's designation as a "Priority Foreign Country" in the 2026 Special 301 Report — is also proceeding. The proposed rates for Vietnam start at zero percent in 2026, increasing to 10% on January 1, 2027, and 15% starting January 1, 2028, and the tariffs would most likely stack on top of any other applicable duties, including reciprocal tariffs.

### III. U.S.-China Trade Architecture: The June 11 Deal and the Section 301 Overlay

On June 11, Trump announced a trade deal with China that would leave in place the current 20% "fentanyl" and 10% "reciprocal" tariffs (for a total of 30%), pausing higher tariffs for 60 days. The announcement complicates the Section 301 forced-labor overlay: the June 2 USTR notice covering 60 economies nominally includes China, and whether the June 11 deal provides exemptions from, or supersedes, the forthcoming Section 301 China rate has not been publicly clarified by USTR or the White House. That ambiguity will be a central issue in the July 6 written-comment period.

The president's team has negotiated several bilateral deals — called Agreements on Reciprocal Trade, or ARTs — with other countries to adjust U.S. tariff rates and product coverage in return for a variety of concessions; as of May 22, 2026, nine ARTs have been signed and several more are under active negotiation. The ARTs construct a legal architecture to pull U.S. trade partners away from China and constrain China economically, pushing partners to keep supplying the U.S. market on terms that reduce space for Chinese firms, capital, and technology, as well as China-linked transshipment. Importers sourcing through third-country hubs — particularly Vietnam, Malaysia, and Thailand — face layered Section 301 exposure across multiple investigation tracks simultaneously.

### IV. CAPE Mechanics, Liquidated-Entry Refund Risk, and the Government's "Must-File" Argument

The practical mechanics of refund recovery remain contested even as $85 billion in CAPE-processed refunds have already been disbursed. At the June 9 hearing, a CBP representative testified that CAPE Phase 3 entries — a category including finally liquidated entries — will start being processed for refunds, but repeated the government's earlier argument that such entries could only be processed for importers that have filed Section 1581(i) actions before the CIT. This position — that filing a CIT suit is a prerequisite to refund eligibility for finally liquidated entries — is the precise issue the Terry Precision Cycling class-certification motion is designed to defeat.

Key issues remaining for the CIT to address include: the mechanics and timing of refunds, treatment of liquidated versus unliquidated entries, administrative implementation by CBP, and potential offsets such as credits in lieu of refunds or defenses raised by the government. Typically, to recover unlawfully collected duties, an importer must first file a protest with CBP. If CBP denies the protest, the importer may seek relief at the CIT under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(a). The government has argued throughout that this protest-first pathway is unavailable for IEEPA duties — because the collection and assessment of IEEPA duties are not CBP decisions subject to protest, but rather nonprotestable actions taken pursuant to executive orders — meaning that importers who did not file § 1581(i) suits before the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling may face an extinction of finally liquidated entry claims if the government's appeal succeeds and the class is not certified.

CBP continues to develop additional CAPE functionality for more complex entry categories, including reconciliation entries, drawback claims, and historically liquidated entries. Importers with drawback claims or reconciliation entries should be cataloging those positions now, as the CAPE Phase 3+ processing timeline will track closely to Federal Circuit briefing in the merits appeal — a schedule not yet set as of today's briefing.

## Recommended Actions

GCs, trade counsel, and claim-recovery practitioners should treat the next 39 days — from today through the July 6 Section 301 comment deadline and July 7 USTR hearing — as a dual-track sprint: first, file or coordinate written comments on the USTR's June 2 Section 301 forced-labor Federal Register notice (Docket No. USTR-2026-0003) addressing proposed duties of 10% or 12.5% on 60 economies, paying particular attention to stacking with existing China Section 301 duties, the June 11 U.S.-China deal's interaction with the proposed China rate, and the Vietnam phase-in schedule (0% → 10% → 15%); second, immediately audit open import-entry portfolios to identify which entries remain unliquidated, which are nonfinal-liquidated (CAPE Phase 1/2 eligible), and which are finally liquidated (Phase 3 / § 1581(i)-dependent) — because the government's Federal Circuit merits appeal of the CIT's universal injunction in *V.O.S. Selections*, Court No. 25-00066, directly threatens refund eligibility for the finally-liquidated tranche for non-CIT filers, and the June 25 deadline for the government's response to Terry Precision Cycling's class-certification motion will be an early leading indicator of whether a class-action pathway remains viable as a fallback; importers who have not filed § 1581(i) actions and hold finally liquidated entries should consult trade litigation counsel immediately, before the Federal Circuit sets a briefing schedule on the merits appeal, as any stay motion from the government — if granted — would further freeze CAPE Phase 3 processing on a universe that already represents a contested multi-billion-dollar refund exposure.
