## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: Two Colliding Forces — Refund Acceleration Meets New Tariff Architecture

This morning's picture is simultaneously good news for importers holding IEEPA refund claims and a sharp reminder that the administration's tariff-replacement machine is fully operational. On the refund side, Treasury data released July 13 confirmed that 
gross customs duty collections in June were $23.6 billion but refunds reached $49.2 billion, producing a net outflow of $25.6 billion for the month
 — the clearest signal yet that the CBP CAPE pipeline is pumping at scale. On the tariff-offense side, USTR Jamieson Greer announced late Wednesday that 
the United States plans to impose a 25% tariff on some Brazilian imports from next week, after a yearlong investigation concluded that the country had engaged in "unfair" trade practices
 — the first major new country-specific levy erected since the Supreme Court's February ruling eliminated IEEPA authority. Both developments are material for anyone modeling net landed cost, refund recovery timing, and the durability of the post-IEEPA tariff regime. And both intersect with an eight-day countdown: 
the 10% global import surcharge President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) is scheduled to expire by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24.

### CAPE Pipeline: $71 Billion Out, $95 Billion More Queued — Phase 3 the Pivotal Test

The Treasury monthly statement is not merely a budget story; it is an operational readout on CAPE throughput. 
Refunds amounted to $49.2 billion for June after nearly $22 billion for May — totaling more than half of the estimated $166 billion the government is estimated to be on the hook for paying back.
 
Combined, the two months of payouts account for around $71 billion — roughly 42% of the $166 billion in IEEPA-based duties that U.S. Customs and Border Protection collected and that are now owed back to importers.


CBP's own testimony before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, at the June 9, 2026 CIT hearing provides the granular operations picture. 
CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas reported that Phase 1 of CAPE has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected and that approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to Treasury for refund.
 To put the processing velocity in context: 
in all of the last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


Phase 2, which expanded CAPE to cover reconciliation-flagged entries (entry types 01, 02, and 06 under CSMS #69066837), launched on schedule June 29. 
EAC Thomas estimated Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total. Critically, Phase 2 will not cover all reconciliation entries — those filed before roughly May 31, 2025, that liquidated on a standard 314-day cycle will have liquidated more than 80 days before June 29, 2026, and fall outside Phase 2's scope. Importers with reconciliation entries from the earliest months of the IEEPA tariff period should not assume Phase 2 will capture those entries.


Phase 3 is where recovery rights diverge sharply. 
CAPE Phase 3 will address finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs; according to EAC Thomas, programming will be ready in late July 2026.
 But the technical readiness does not resolve the legal question. 
The federal government continues to contest the legal basis for refunds on finally liquidated entries, and it remains unclear whether refunds will actually flow once the technical capability is in place.
 In its June 4 court filing, 
DOJ stated that "Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final" and that to make refunds available for such entries, "an importer must file suit in this Court, and the Court must enter an importer-specific order."
 The Federal Circuit appeal on this precise question — whether the CIT's universal reliquidation order constitutes an impermissible universal injunction under the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in *Trump v. [CASA]* — is live. 
On June 2, 2026, DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's refund order; the government is expected to argue that the CIT's refund orders constitute impermissible universal injunctions extending relief to importers who never filed protective actions at the CIT, and a successful appeal could significantly slow or restructure the refund process.


The operational implication is direct: importers whose entries are finally liquidated and who have not yet filed a protective complaint at the CIT risk falling entirely outside the Phase 3 disbursement, regardless of when the programming goes live. The 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest window runs 180 days from liquidation; for entries liquidated in early calendar 2026, that clock is already running or has closed. Filing a § 1581(i) complaint now is the only mechanism that preserves access to Phase 3 refunds if DOJ prevails on appeal. 
The Trump administration has been expanding the categories of tariffs eligible for refunds through the portal, meaning additional businesses are expected to be able to apply in coming months
 — but that administrative generosity does not extend to finally liquidated entries absent a court order.

One operational hazard: 
importers should not assume that voluntarily processed CAPE refunds will be insulated from later government efforts to recoup or offset — CBP confirmed that IEEPA refunds returned through CAPE remain "available to offset amounts owed with respect to other duties."
 Entries with unresolved AD/CVD exposure or pending audits warrant particular caution before rushing to receive the CAPE disbursement.

### Section 122: Eight Days to Statutory Sunset — No Congressional Extension, Refund Path Unsettled


Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending; absent congressional action, the surcharge terminates on its own terms.
 That termination is now eight days away. The legal history matters because it determines whether importers who paid the 10% surcharge under HTS heading 9903.03.01 (CSMS #67844987) between February 24 and July 23 have a realistic refund path, separate from and following the IEEPA CAPE process.

The procedural posture as of today: 
on May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel of the CIT held that Proclamation 11012 exceeded statutory authority under Section 122, in *The State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026).
 The majority, Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly, held that 
the President did not identify a balance-of-payments deficit as Congress understood that term in 1974; the Proclamation instead pointed to the trade deficit, the current account deficit, and the negative U.S. net international investment position.


The CIT's remedy was party-specific. 
The CIT declined to issue a nationwide injunction; instead it enjoined CBP from collecting Section 122 duties only from the prevailing importer plaintiffs and authorized refunds, with interest, for duties those plaintiffs already paid. For non-party importers, the surcharge remains in effect unless and until broader relief is granted.


The government appealed May 8, and 
on June 11, 2026, the CAFC granted the federal government's motion to stay the lower court's injunction, allowing the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 surcharges from the plaintiffs — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — with the appellate court concluding that three of the four stay factors favored the government.
 Crucially, on the merits, 
the CAFC concluded the government had made a sufficient showing it is likely to succeed on the merits, explaining that the CIT's interpretation — that "balance-of-payments deficit" is limited to deficits measured by liquidity, official settlements, and basic balance — "may be incorrect," and that the legislative history cited by the government "strongly call[s] into question" whether those were the only methods Congress intended.


The appellate posture is therefore adverse to importers on Section 122 refunds. 
The government's initial brief is due to the CAFC in July, making it unlikely this litigation is resolved prior to the July 24, 2026 expiration for the Section 122 duties.
 
The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality; the July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period — the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the
 appellate outcome. For the vast majority of importers who were not named plaintiffs in *Oregon/Burlap & Barrel*, 
if the CIT is confirmed on appeal, importers who paid the 10% surcharge may have a path to refunds — but no mechanism currently exists for that scenario.
 Preservation steps under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 — specifically, a timely protest of each liquidated Section 122 entry — are the only available hedge. 
For liquidated entries, track the 180-day protest deadline from liquidation.

### Brazil Section 301: 25% Tariff Effective July 22 — Forced-Labor Add-On Could Push Effective Rate to 37.5%

The administration's tariff-rebuilding strategy moved into execution yesterday. 
The tariffs, announced by USTR Jamieson Greer, come as Trump has repeatedly targeted Brazil with tariff threats and has sanctioned Brazilian officials while defending his close ally, former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro.
 
The tariffs, which are to go into effect July 22, follow a yearlong investigation of Brazilian policies launched July 15, 2025.



The action, taken under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, targets Brazilian practices, such as orders directing American technology firms — including X, Meta, and Google — to remove certain political content and suspend accounts belonging to U.S. residents, as well as preferential tariffs for Mexico.
 
The tariff will apply to a host of Brazilian products, but will exempt certain major exports including coffee, beef, avocados, Brazil nuts, petroleum oils, and aircraft parts, according to a notice published by the Office of the USTR.


The rate architecture could get worse for Brazilian importers. 
A separate U.S. probe into forced-labor enforcement could see an additional 12.5% duty on Brazilian goods on top of the 25%, with the decision due next week.
 If that probe concludes as signaled, the composite Section 301 burden on Brazilian goods reaches 37.5% — well above the IEEPA rates that were struck down by the Supreme Court earlier this year.


Trump has sought to reinstate his tariff power by launching Section 301 probes, which allow him to impose levies on countries found to have engaged in unfair trade practices without additional congressional authorization.
 Section 301 carries a distinct procedural record — a documented finding of unreasonable or discriminatory foreign practices — that distinguishes it from the IEEPA emergency framework the Supreme Court rejected in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (Feb. 20, 2026). The administration's bet is that Section 301 is legally durable where IEEPA was not. 
WTO Chief Economist Robert Staiger told CNBC that the U.S. keeps changing the legal mechanism by which it can impose tariffs, but that the overall tariffs it's imposing have stayed broadly similar to when they were first imposed in 2025.



Brazil said it will immediately initiate procedures to activate instruments provided for in its Reciprocity Law, approved unanimously by the National Congress, and will resume the issue within the framework of the WTO dispute settlement mechanism.
 A WTO challenge would take years. For companies with Brazil-origin supply chains — particularly in petroleum, petrochemicals, agricultural inputs not on the exemption list, and manufactured goods — the July 22 effective date is imminent. Duty drawback under 19 U.S.C. § 1313 is structurally available for Section 301 duties, but only on merchandise that is subsequently exported; domestic-consumption supply chains have no drawback offset.

The Brazil action is also the template for what comes next. 
Beginning in March 2026, USTR initiated two waves of Section 301 investigations targeting most major U.S. trading partners — one focused on forced labor enforcement covering 60 economies, the other on structural excess manufacturing capacity covering 16 economies including China, the EU, Japan, and Vietnam.
 Brazil is the first country to receive a final Section 301 tariff action. The remaining 59 forced-labor investigations are pending, with 
USTR having already made findings in the forced labor investigation and proposed responsive actions, with written comments due July 6, 2026, and hearings scheduled for July 7.
 Tariff actions on additional countries are, on this timeline, a matter of weeks rather than months.

### Fiscal Footprint: Refunds Macro-Significant, But Deficit Widening Temporary — Evercore ISI

The budget numbers are the clearest indication of how seriously the refund program is being taken at the Treasury level. 
The June deficit was a sharp turnaround from the $27 billion surplus in June 2025 that was due partly to the imposition of import duties.
 
For the fiscal year to date, the deficit rose $29 billion, or 2%, to $1.367 trillion.
 
Evercore ISI analysts including Matthew Aks wrote in a July 10 note that "the refund program has become large enough to be macroeconomically, fiscally and market-significant this year," though the payouts "represent a temporary increase in the deficit," given that the Trump administration is in the process of erecting a new tariff wall.


The Evercore framing is accurate. The refund-driven deficit expansion will compress as CAPE throughput normalizes and as Section 301 actions build a new duty base. But the $166 billion total liability is not yet discharged — 
refunds of $49.2 billion for June and nearly $22 billion for May total more than half of the estimated $166 billion the government is estimated to be on the hook for paying back.
 Approximately $95 billion in IEEPA duties remains outstanding, concentrated in Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries) and the small-dollar tail of Phase 1 and Phase 2 entries still in the ACH pipeline.

## Recommended Actions

GCs, compliance officers, and CFOs with IEEPA refund exposure should treat Phase 3 eligibility as an urgent threshold question, not a future issue: any importer with entries that liquidated more than 80 days before a CAPE submission date has no automatic access to Phase 3 refunds under the current DOJ litigation position, and the Federal Circuit stay in the *V.O.S. Selections* line means the government's argument may prevail — making CIT protective complaints the only structural hedge available before that appellate question is resolved. Separately, all entries bearing HTS heading 9903.03.01 (Section 122 surcharge, February 24–July 23, 2026) should be calendared against the 180-day § 1514 protest deadline from each entry's liquidation date; the CAFC stay in *State of Oregon v. United States*, Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805, signals the CIT ruling will not be vindicated quickly, but a timely protest is the only mechanism that preserves recovery rights if that ruling is ultimately affirmed on appeal. For Brazil-origin supply chains, a landed-cost remodel incorporating a 25% Section 301 tariff effective July 22 — and potentially 37.5% if the forced-labor probe concludes as signaled next week — should be completed before that date; the USTR exemption list (coffee, beef, petroleum oils, aircraft parts) provides targeted relief but covers a narrow band of Brazilian exports, so most manufactured and agricultural inputs are in-scope. Companies watching the broader Section 301 campaign across 60 economies should note that Brazil is the first final action: the procedural template, timeline, and rate structure are now visible, and supply-chain adjustments for other affected origins can be modeled against the Brazil precedent. Finally, verify ACH banking information in the ACE Portal immediately — 
over 4,000 consolidated refunds have not been transmitted to Treasury due to a lack of ACH banking information.
