## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: CIT's July 15 Order Clears the Path for Phase 3 — But Only for Litigants

The controlling development this week is the July 15, 2026 order issued by Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton of the U.S. Court of International Trade. 
On July 15, 2026, the CIT directed CBP to reliquidate, without IEEPA duties, plaintiffs' entries that had been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which plaintiffs had deposited estimated IEEPA duties.
 
In that July 15 order, Judge Eaton announced that he plans to order CBP to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries in approximately 3,700 pending IEEPA tariff refund cases once CAPE Phase 3 launches.
 The order is the most direct action yet on the contested finally-liquidated tranche — but its scope is deliberately bounded. 
The July 15 order does not itself extend relief to non-litigants.


The lead-case posture has also shifted. 
The CIT has selected a new lead case, as the plaintiff in Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States (Court No. 25-00595) intends to voluntarily dismiss its case; the new lead case will be Freestyle World Inc. v. United States.
 That substitution matters practically: Euro-Notions had served as the administrative vehicle for managing refunds for non-plaintiff importers, and its withdrawal narrows the government's exposure in that docket at the very moment Phase 3 is set to launch. Separately, 
during a CBP webinar on July 15, 2026, CBP special advisor Elena Ryan stated that the next CAPE phase will allow entries filed on behalf of plaintiffs who filed court cases with the CIT that have reached final liquidation.
 
The court ordered the government to file another CAPE progress report by August 4, 2026, with a closed settlement conference scheduled for August 5, 2026.

### CAPE Phase Scorecard: Where the $166 Billion Stands


According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 
As of July 2, 2026, about $90 billion in claims had been accepted and roughly $24 billion transmitted to the Treasury for payment.


The three-phase structure is now largely set:

**Phase 1** (live since April 20, 2026): 
Phase 1 launched April 20, 2026 and is limited to (a) certain unliquidated entries, and (b) certain entries liquidated within 80 days of the CAPE submission date.
 
According to CBP, that scope covered an estimated 63 percent of affected entries.


**Phase 2** (launched June 29, 2026): 
Effective June 29, 2026, importers and authorized customs brokers can include in their CAPE declarations certain reconciliation-flagged entries (entry types 01, 02, and 06) for which the reconciliation entry (entry type 09) has not been filed.
 
EAC Thomas estimated Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.
 One important carve-out: 
entries filed before roughly May 31, 2025 that liquidated on a standard 314-day cycle will have liquidated more than 80 days before June 29, 2026, and fall outside Phase 2's scope.


**Phase 3** (targeted late July 2026): 
Phase 3 will address finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs; according to EAC Thomas, programming for Phase 3 will be ready in late July 2026.
 The government has quantified the stakes in this tranche: 
a large category of entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund clock started — remains contested, with the government estimating the dollar exposure at potentially more than $30 billion.
 Technical readiness is not the binding constraint. 
Technical readiness does not guarantee that refunds will follow; the DOJ continues to argue that CBP lacks authority to reliquidate finally liquidated entries without an individual lawsuit; that position is now before both the CIT and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.


A discrete operational change that took effect July 7 merits tracking: 
effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on a CAPE Declaration; any warehouse entry submitted on a CAPE Declaration on or after that date will be rejected with the existing "ENTRY TYPE NOT ALLOWED" error message.
 Importers who submitted warehouse entries between April 20 and July 6 without corresponding warehouse withdrawal submissions face additional exposure — 
such entries will not be (re)liquidated with an IEEPA duty refund; filers in this position will need to submit a separate CAPE Declaration covering the warehouse withdrawals on which IEEPA duties were paid.


CBP's processing timeline is also relevant: 
importers and authorized brokers should anticipate that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60 to 90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.

### The Federal Circuit Appeal: DOJ's Structural Argument and What It Means for Non-Litigants

The central legal risk for the broader importer universe remains the government's Federal Circuit appeal of Judge Eaton's universal-refund orders. 
On May 29, 2026, the DOJ filed a notice of appeal and motion to amend the court's order in V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States (Court No. 25-00066) before the CIT, challenging Judge Richard K. Eaton's universal order directing CBP to refund approximately $166 billion in duties collected under IEEPA.


The government's theory has two prongs. 
The motion made explicit the government's position that (1) "[o]nce an entry is finally liquidated, CBP has no authority to reliquidate or refund money without a court order," and (2) "the universal injunction exceeds the Court's jurisdiction and equitable authority under Trump v. CASA, Inc.," a 2025 Supreme Court decision that limited a lower court's power to issue relief to non-litigants.


The government also has to reckon with its own prior concessions. 
In AGS Company Automotive Solutions v. CBP (Slip Op. 25-154), the DOJ affirmatively represented to the CIT that it "will not object to the [c]ourt ordering reliquidation of plaintiff's entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful." On January 8, 2026, the government further stipulated in the CIT that it would refund IEEPA tariffs for "all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs" following a "final and unappealable decision."
 Those prior representations underpin a judicial-estoppel argument that the CIT endorsed; 
the Court's position is that its injunction requires CBP to reliquidate and refund all such entries regardless of whether the importer has filed suit, because the underlying duties were unlawfully collected, while the government's position is that once an entry has liquidated and become final, CBP has no statutory authority to reliquidate absent a court order in a case to which that specific importer is a party.


The Federal Circuit previously vacated one CIT universal injunction in V.O.S. Selections on CASA grounds, though 
that remand was directed at the original merits-phase injunction, not the post-judgment refund order now at issue.
 How the Federal Circuit handles the distinction between institutional CIT authority and ordinary district court authority will determine whether roughly 326,000 non-plaintiff importers retain administrative access to Phase 3 or are relegated to individual litigation under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i). 
In the appeal, the Federal Circuit's treatment of the universal injunction question may determine whether all importers, including those who have not sued, can obtain refunds for finally liquidated entries without individual litigation.


For importers currently on the sidelines: 
under Administrative Order 25-02, new cases challenging tariffs based on IEEPA and the relevant executive orders are automatically stayed upon filing; no order of assignment is issued before entry of the stay, and the Clerk maintains a schedule of all stayed cases.
 Filing now is a low-friction protective step. 
Approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected, if CBP proceeds as planned, to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined, regardless of liquidation status; under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE.

### Section 122 Expires in Five Days — and What Comes Next


The 10 percent global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) is scheduled to expire by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 
Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending; absent congressional action, the surcharge terminates on its own terms.


The Federal Circuit stay issued June 11 on the CIT's Section 122 injunction means the surcharge has been collected from all importers — including the named plaintiffs (the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.) — through July 23. 
On June 11, 2026, the CAFC granted the federal government's motion to stay the lower court's injunction; the court concluded that three of the four stay factors favored the government, with the fourth neutral, effectively pausing relief to all named plaintiffs and ensuring the 10% surcharge continues to be collected throughout the duration of the appeals process.



The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality; the CIT held the surcharge unlawful and the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling pending the government's appeal in State of Oregon v. United States, so CBP has continued to collect the duty on entries through July 23; the July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period.
 The disposition of those already-collected duties remains tied to the Federal Circuit appeal. Critically, 
CAPE covers only IEEPA tariffs, and there is currently no administrative refund channel for the Section 122 surcharge
 — Section 122 claimants have no CAPE equivalent and must proceed through protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or individual CIT actions.

### The Section 301 Succession: No Expiration Date, No Rate Cap

The administration is not waiting passively for the Section 122 sunset. 
On June 2, 2026, USTR announced proposed tariffs under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 following an investigation into alleged forced labor policies and practices of 59 countries and the European Union; the proposed tariff rate is 10% for most goods of 15 trading partners, and 12.5% for most goods of 45 trading partners.
 
USTR held public hearings beginning July 7, 2026, with post-hearing rebuttal comments due five days after the hearings conclude, and the agency intends to have a final action ready by the time Section 122 lapses.



Section 122 expires July 24, 2026, and USTR's Section 301 completion deadline is July 20 — with proposed 12.5% duties on 46 countries.
 The structural difference is dispositive for planning purposes: 
Section 301, unlike Section 122, has no statutory rate cap and no built-in expiration date.
 
Section 232 actions are proceeding in parallel, including 100 percent duties on patented pharmaceuticals and active pharmaceutical ingredients effective July 31 for larger companies and September 29 for others.



If USTR finalizes by the July 20 deadline, the 12.5% Section 301 duties can take effect at or near the July 24 handoff, and the practical outcome for the 46 listed countries is a rate increase: 12.5% instead of today's 10%, on top of MFN and any existing surcharges.
 
Carve-outs in an Annex cover certain agricultural goods, aviation parts, industrial inputs, minerals, pharmaceuticals, and goods already subject to Section 232 duties.
 Treasury Secretary Bessent has been explicit about the administration's intent: 
Treasury Secretary Bessent has publicly stated tariffs will return to pre-SCOTUS levels by August 2026.


The Section 301 replacement is legally durable in a way IEEPA and Section 122 were not — it carries no temporal ceiling, has a well-developed administrative record, and reflects a targeted findings-based framework that courts have historically upheld. Importers who structured refund recovery strategies around the assumption of a sustained post-Section 122 tariff holiday should revise those models immediately.

### Duty Drawback Under 19 U.S.C. § 1313: The Parallel Recovery Track

One avenue that has received less attention than CAPE but remains live for affected importers is duty drawback under 19 U.S.C. § 1313. 
Drawback is available for the additional Section 122 duties.
 The mechanism is distinct from CAPE and protests: where an importer re-exported goods or used imported inputs in articles subsequently exported, drawback filings under § 1313(a) (manufacturing drawback) or § 1313(j) (unused merchandise drawback) can recover up to 99% of duties paid, including Section 122 surcharges, without waiting for the outcome of the Federal Circuit Section 122 appeal. Drawback claims must be filed within five years of the date of importation, giving importers a longer runway than the 180-day protest window under § 1514 — but the five-year clock for the earliest February 2025 IEEPA entries will begin closing in February 2030, and early-period entries with manufacturing drawback potential should be identified and queued now.

## Recommended Actions

**For importers with finally liquidated IEEPA entries:** The July 15 CIT order has created a bifurcated track — litigants get a court-authorized Phase 3 reliquidation route; non-litigants do not. If you have not filed at the CIT, file now under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) to secure Phase 3 eligibility; the filing is low-cost under Administrative Order 25-02's automatic-stay regime, and it is the one path the government concedes. Map every finally liquidated IEEPA entry against its 180-day protest window under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 and calendar each deadline — protest windows are closing on a rolling per-entry basis with no single cliff date. For warehouse entries submitted on CAPE Declarations before July 7 without corresponding withdrawal submissions, file corrected declarations covering the withdrawals immediately. For Section 122 duty exposure (entries from February 24 through July 23, 2026), note that CAPE provides no administrative refund channel; preservation of refund rights requires a timely § 1514 protest or individual CIT action; the July 24 sunset does not automatically generate refund rights. Model landed costs under a 12.5% Section 301 baseline effective July 24 — USTR's completion deadline is July 20, four days away — and review Annex carve-outs for agricultural goods, aviation parts, industrial inputs, and pharmaceuticals before reconfiguring sourcing strategy.
