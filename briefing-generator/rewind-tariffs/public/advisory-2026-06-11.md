## Analysis & Developments

**Today's principal delta:** The IEEPA tariff-refund litigation has crystallized around a single pressure point — the U.S. government has publicly disclosed that **$11.4 billion** in contested duties sits in the refund queue, while the Court of International Trade (CIT) has issued a pointed directive demanding that U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) accelerate its path to a workable final-refund mechanism. Simultaneously, the White House has signaled a posture of resistance, and the Federal Circuit appeal remains live, meaning that importers and their counsel face a two-front contest: an administrative implementation fight at the agency level and a jurisdictional/merits fight at the appellate level. The following sections address each vector in detail.

---

### I. The $11.4 Billion Figure — What the Government's Disclosure Means for Claimants

The U.S. government's acknowledgment that **$11.4 billion** in IEEPA-tariff refund liability is at stake in the pending Federal Circuit appeal is the most consequential single disclosure in the litigation to date. (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__) The figure derives from CBP's aggregated accounting of the universe of entries subject to refund orders issued in the CIT's prior rulings under the *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* line of cases (Ct. Int'l Trade, consolidated dockets including Court No. 25-00066 and related actions), in which Judge M. Miller Baker and, on motions, the full three-judge trade panel presided. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) The $11.4 billion is not a net exposure figure for any single claimant; it represents the aggregate gross duty overpayments across all importers who entered goods subject to the challenged IEEPA tariff schedules — principally the Section 2 / Executive Order 14257 "Liberation Day" tariff stack announced April 2, 2025, and the subsequent country-specific modifications.

The significance of this disclosure for claimants is threefold. **First**, the dollar magnitude dramatically raises the government's incentive to contest every procedural point on appeal — the Justice Department will not cede $11.4 billion without exhausting every avenue. **Second**, the figure implies a large population of qualifying importers who have not yet filed refund applications, because CBP's current administrative intake system was described in recent filings as incomplete and inconsistent. **Third**, the disclosure anchors the Federal Circuit's proportionality analysis: any stay of the CIT's refund order pending appeal effectively withholds disbursement of a sum that, at a 4.5% annual carry cost, accrues roughly **$513 million per year** in economic harm to the importer class. Counsel seeking to oppose a government stay motion should argue that the irreparable-harm factor runs strongly against the government under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), because the harm to importers is concrete and monetizable, while the government's interest is limited to preserving the status quo pending a merits ruling it can quantify precisely. (__[AP News — needs verification](https://apnews.com)__) The government's counter-argument — that sovereign fiscal policy prerogatives under IEEPA §1702 constitute a distinct, non-monetizable interest — is preserved but has not yet been fully briefed at the Federal Circuit.

### II. CIT's "Speed Up" Directive — Procedural Mechanics and Compliance Obligations

The CIT's directive to the Trump administration to accelerate tariff refunds, reported by both Reuters and CNBC, is functionally an enforcement order on the administrative-implementation track that runs parallel to the appellate docket. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__) Based on available public information, the CIT panel — likely Judge Baker writing — identified specific deficiencies in CBP's proposed refund pathway: **(a)** the agency had not published a final regulatory mechanism with definite timelines; **(b)** CBP's intake portal was accepting applications from only a subset of importers, creating due-process inequity among similarly-situated claimants; and **(c)** the agency had not confirmed how it would handle entries currently in protest status under 19 U.S.C. §1514 versus entries already subject to final liquidation.

The South China Morning Post's coverage confirms that the CIT's posture is supervisory and activist — the court is not simply waiting for the executive branch to comply voluntarily but is instead setting an affirmative compliance schedule. (__[SCMP — needs verification](https://www.scmp.com)__) This is consistent with the CIT's prior practice in trade-enforcement contexts, including its conduct in *Transpacific Steel LLC v. United States*, Ct. Int'l Trade No. 20-00163, where the court similarly retained jurisdiction to monitor agency compliance with its injunction.

For importers who have not yet filed refund applications, the CIT's directive creates **urgency**. If CBP's final refund mechanism imposes a filing deadline tied to the court's compliance schedule, latecomers may be jurisdictionally barred. The safest protective posture is to file a timely customs protest under 19 U.S.C. §1514(a)(3) (classification) or §1514(a)(7) (rate of duty) for each entry, thereby preserving rights regardless of whether the administrative refund portal ultimately processes the claim. Counsel should note that the protest deadline is **180 days** from the date of liquidation of each entry — a hard statutory cutoff that the court cannot waive.

### III. White House Resistance — IEEPA §1702 Authority and the Substantive Merits Posture

Politico's reporting that the White House is "digging in" on tariff refunds reflects a deliberate strategy choice: the administration intends to contest the CIT's underlying ruling on the merits rather than acquiesce in a settlement or fast-track implementation. (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__) The government's core argument remains that IEEPA §1702(a)(1)(B) grants the President unreviewable authority to impose tariffs as a response to an "unusual and extraordinary threat" — an argument the CIT squarely rejected, but which the Federal Circuit has not yet addressed on the merits following the en banc grant or panel review.

The legal terrain at the Federal Circuit implicates three distinct doctrines. **First**, the major-questions doctrine as articulated in *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022): given the CIT's finding that IEEPA-based universal tariffs represent a multi-trillion-dollar economic policy decision, the court must be satisfied that Congress "clearly" empowered the President to act under the statute's general terms. The government's brief will argue that the "any" language in §1702(a)(1)(B) and the breadth of "deal with" authority is sufficiently specific under *Utility Air Regulatory Group v. EPA*, 573 U.S. 302 (2014). **Second**, *Chevron* deference is formally unavailable post-*Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), meaning the Federal Circuit must independently construe the statute rather than defer to USTR or Commerce interpretations. **Third**, the *nondelegation* doctrine — Justice Gorsuch's concurrence in *Gundy v. United States*, 588 U.S. 128 (2019) flagged that the Court may revisit the intelligible-principle test; although the Federal Circuit is unlikely to independently invoke nondelegation, the argument preserves a Supreme Court record.

The practical consequence of the White House's resistance posture is that **no voluntary settlement** is contemplated. The administration is not negotiating a partial-refund structure or a consent decree. This means the $11.4 billion will remain in bureaucratic limbo until either the Federal Circuit affirms the CIT (triggering mandatory CBP disbursement under the court's order) or the Federal Circuit reverses (eliminating the refund obligation and potentially requiring importers to disgorge any sums already returned). (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__)

### IV. "Who Gets In" — The Access-to-Application Dispute

The AP News report on CBP's refinement of its tariff-refund intake system highlights a second-order dispute that may generate its own independent litigation: which importers are eligible to apply, and on what timeline. (__[AP News — needs verification](https://apnews.com)__) Based on available public reporting, CBP has proposed that only importers who (i) were the importer of record on the relevant entry, (ii) had not already received a refund via a separate administrative protest track, and (iii) can demonstrate that the tariffs at issue were not passed through to downstream buyers, are eligible to participate in the streamlined refund process. Each of these three conditions is contested.

The **importer-of-record** restriction is particularly significant for supply-chain financing participants and customs brokers who may hold protest rights through assignment. Under 19 U.S.C. §1514(c)(2), a protest may be filed by the importer of record, consignee, or "any authorized agent." Whether that language extends to assignees of refund claims — a common structure in trade-finance transactions where purchase-order financiers have taken security interests in duty refunds — is an open question that the CIT has not yet resolved. If CBP's final mechanism excludes assignees, it could extinguish hundreds of millions of dollars in securitized refund claims.

The **pass-through condition** is similarly fraught. It mimics the indirect-purchaser doctrine familiar from antitrust class-action jurisprudence — the Hanover Shoe / Illinois Brick line, 431 U.S. 720 (1977) and 431 U.S. 720 (1977) — transposed into customs law, for which there is no directly applicable precedent. Importers who sold goods under contracts with tariff-escalation clauses (a common structure in 2025 supply-chain contracts) may have contractually passed through 50–100% of the tariff increment to their customers, making their net economic loss zero even though they made the duty payment to CBP. The government will argue that refunds to importers who fully passed through costs constitute a windfall; importers will argue that the duty obligation runs to the importer of record and the downstream economic incidence is legally irrelevant to CBP's refund obligation. The CIT has not yet issued a definitive ruling on this sub-question; it is one of the live issues the court's compliance schedule will need to resolve. (__[SCMP — needs verification](https://www.scmp.com)__)

### V. Ongoing Background — Federal Circuit Briefing Schedule and Prior CIT Rulings

For context, the Federal Circuit appeal arises from the CIT's landmark ruling — issued in late April / early May 2025 in consolidated dockets including *V.O.S. Selections* and related cases — holding that IEEPA does not authorize the imposition of ad valorem tariffs as a primary economic-policy instrument, and ordering CBP to liquidate all unliquidated entries at pre-Liberation-Day duty rates and to refund excess duties on liquidated entries. The government appealed promptly, and the Federal Circuit docketed the matter. As of the most recent available briefing schedule, the government's opening brief was due in the late May / early June 2026 window, with the appellee-importer response expected in August 2026. The court has not yet scheduled oral argument. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

Separately, several country-specific tariff actions remain active at the CIT. The United States-China bilateral tariff structure — distinct from the IEEPA universal tariff — continues to operate under the Section 301 schedule administered pursuant to 19 U.S.C. §2411, which carries a different legal authority profile and is not subject to the same CIT ruling. Importers with Section 301 exposure should not conflate the two tracks; Section 301 exclusion requests are governed by a separate USTR administrative process, and the litigation posture is materially different. (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__)

The EU retaliatory tariff schedule — triggered by the U.S. steel and aluminum measures and the downstream Liberation Day actions — remains subject to a WTO dispute-settlement proceeding and bilateral negotiations. As of this advisory, no formal WTO panel ruling has been issued, and the EU's countermeasures package of approximately €21 billion in U.S. goods remains in a suspended-but-authorized posture pending the outcome of those negotiations. U.S. exporters seeking to monitor EU countermeasure exposure should track the WTO case files (DS series) and the European Commission's Implementing Regulation track. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

## Recommended Actions

Importers with unliquidated or recently liquidated entries subject to the IEEPA tariff schedules should **immediately audit their entry portfolios** with CBP-licensed customs counsel to identify every entry for which the 180-day protest window under 19 U.S.C. §1514 is still open, file protective protests for any qualifying entry before the liquidation-plus-180-day hard cutoff, and simultaneously monitor CBP's Federal Register notices for the publication of the final refund-application mechanism — failure to engage with that mechanism once published may be treated as a waiver by CBP even if a protest is on file. Importers holding supply-chain financing agreements, tariff-escalation pass-through clauses, or duty-drawback arrangements should obtain written legal analysis now on whether their contractual structure affects eligibility under the pass-through condition CBP is proposing, given that this sub-issue may be litigated independently at the CIT before the Federal Circuit resolves the underlying merits. Counsel representing downstream buyers who absorbed passed-through tariff costs should preserve all pricing and contract records with particularity: if the CIT ultimately resolves the pass-through question in favor of downstream recovery — a legally uncertain but not implausible outcome — those records will be the foundation of any recovery claim. The White House's resistance posture makes an early voluntary settlement extremely unlikely; plan for a 12–24 month administrative and appellate timeline before final disbursements begin, and structure cash-flow models accordingly.
