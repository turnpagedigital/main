### I. The $11.4 Billion Figure: Scope, Composition, and the Liability Architecture

The **$11.4 billion** aggregate refund figure disclosed by the government in proceedings before the Court of International Trade represents the estimated universe of IEEPA Section 232/301-adjacent tariff duties collected under emergency executive authority that have been placed in legal jeopardy following the CIT's adverse liability rulings in the consolidated docket. The figure encompasses both (a) duties collected on entries that are the direct subject of pending refund claims and (b) estimated contingent exposure on entries for which no claim has yet been filed but which fall within the statutory window (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMitAFBVV95cUxOMUlFVE82VlRpWTdmQ21yekxYLUpIUEI3VWJESFE3WnpoT1RmdFhkRXNKTEVWQnpaTXZTeFVBTDlua1M0YjFEY3F1bmpJNVB0M2lyZWR4Y05rUFRVTHlNc1ZLSG1tck43VnF0WGtqTm9PR2VjcE05Sy16WklXaG9jdExMc0NWcjhndkxWVmxtMmEybUxiTTUzaDM2VXc2OUhzYXFmTXdINVFlOHBIc3hTcldpVEs?oc=5)__).

The statutory predicate for refund eligibility is the judicial invalidation of tariff lines imposed pursuant to the **International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708**, which the administration invoked beginning in February 2025 to impose "reciprocal" tariff schedules on the majority of U.S. trading partners. The CIT's lead three-judge panel — comprising Judges **Gary S. Katzmann**, **Timothy C. Stanceu**, and **M. Miller Baker** — held in consolidated actions (*V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, and companion cases) that IEEPA does not supply authority for open-ended, revenue-generating tariff regimes of the kind at issue, and issued a declaratory judgment requiring CBP to cease collection and process refunds on affected entries (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiqAFBVV95cUxPdW5ZRGtyaHJoX0pLSnpOMzlHNmFlQkx6Z2Zmb19aWGtKYlBUY0FLajZYRmtpYkpqYXBIVWJPWWZTeXFfVDBzdTFvRnAxdVJkbjVqZUZ2REFsYzI1ZUFaU0ZQbjFxb052cmRSWnFVVFA4S3Noc1RUZE9mZUZGTEgzUl9PQWpVUFFhQkZyQ1VoakNGRldjaGFPckl5S1d6ZjVZT3hEZFJPWHk?oc=5)__). The Federal Circuit appeal — *United States v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, Appeal No. 25-1812 — has stayed only the prospective injunction against *future* collection; refunds on completed entries remain subject to the CIT's active remedial jurisdiction (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiqAFBVV95cUxPdW5ZRGtyaHJoX0pLSnpOMzlHNmFlQkx6Z2Zmb19aWGtKYlBUY0FLajZYRmtpYkpqYXBIVWJPWWZTeXFfVDBzdTFvRnAxdVJkbjVqZUZ2REFsYzI1ZUFaU0ZQbjFxb052cmRSWnFVVFA4S3Noc1RUZE9mZUZGTEgzUl9PQWpVUFFhQkZyQ1VoakNGRldjaGFPckl5S1d6ZjVZT3hEZFJPWHk?oc=5)__).

The $11.4 billion disclosure therefore substantially exceeds prior informal estimates circulated by trade counsel and signals that CBP's internal entry-reconciliation process has, at minimum, identified the population of potentially refundable entries — an administrative predicate that importers' counsel should treat as an implicit concession that the refund-calculation infrastructure exists and can be operationalized.

### II. The CIT's Remedial Order: Acceleration Mandate and CBP's Processing Posture

The CIT's most recent intervention — described in reporting by Reuters and CNBC as a directive to "speed up" refund processing — appears to arise from a status conference or remedial scheduling order in the consolidated docket, in which the panel expressed dissatisfaction with the pace of CBP's administrative processing protocol (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiqAFBVV95cUxPdW5ZRGtyaHJoX0pLSnpOMzlHNmFlQkx6Z2Zmb19aWGtKYlBUY0FLajZYRmtpYkpqYXBIVWJPWWZTeXFfVDBzdTFvRnAxdVJkbjVqZUZ2REFsYzI1ZUFaU0ZQbjFxb052cmRSWnFVVFA4S3Noc1RUZE9mZUZGTEgzUl9PQWpVUFFhQkZyQ1VoakNGRldjaGFPckl5S1d6ZjVZT3hEZFJPWHk?oc=5)__) (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMipgFBVV95cUxNeFF4SEk0WUlZRXVoaTVnb2pGZFpEYXlUQWYycERDdWNvMXU3TEROZnVZN3lnbWlrbHRsNzg1T2NMc0NlNXNiUkFyb0hrbTJvX0JmdWlkYzBzbnVIV3dtc1hCdllVZm93SkxHLWJHUGNWbG1ya04yLUtqbkdHdE1pemFOUEZMZ3VrbXhtNUtVeW9UelA3cUVYZ0NjaHJmcWphazRfNjd30gGrAUFVX3lxTFBrQjRQdDI2RDJGR2syM1QwZzlZMEJxWTR4bVNQeHl0WmhuTG1CRVRMWDN6Y0gxSm5hMjBENjQtNzl1cGJxV0NXeUhCMEJadjRzMVN0QXJ2THhBelNGcXlINEo2THl4cVRpcGxtUjI0TnN2SFk0ci1wRjVtbWxmZl9OWGVJcTQyNng0aFVyUWItVnJsTTl5WFcyNGxXNTNfUnFUcTV1dDhFT0EyNA?oc=5)__). From a procedural standpoint, the most significant legal mechanism available to the CIT at this stage is a **compliance and contempt framework** under 28 U.S.C. § 2643(c), which grants the court authority to order reliquidation of entries and to hold agency officers in contempt for failure to comply. Courts of international trade have rarely resorted to contempt enforcement against executive agencies, but the $11.4 billion aggregate and the White House's reported resistance make this a more credible threat than in prior trade-remedy contexts. Importers' counsel tracking the docket should monitor for any issuance of a **show-cause order** directed at CBP, which would crystallize the enforcement posture and create a structured timeline for disbursement.

CBP's administrative processing of refund claims operates through the **ACE (Automated Commercial Environment)** portal entry-reliquidation workflow. Claimants who filed **protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514** within the 180-day statutory window following liquidation of each affected entry are in the strongest procedural posture; those who did not protest timely must pursue relief via a **Section 1520(c)** clerical-error petition or, if that is unavailable, seek equitable tolling arguments that the CIT has thus far declined to formally address.

### III. The Eligibility and Standing Dispute: CBP's Resistance and the Access Gateway

A critical new fault line, surfaced in AP News reporting, is CBP's challenge to **which importers qualify to participate in the refund protocol** (__[AP News — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMinAFBVV95cUxQS1plVDlPeGlBN0tUZng0RlNXZFdjVGJ4VnlsTDA3ZmtHX1RXN2hHTWFHUmN4cUZiN1MwejRsYUlOTzJ0c3FUNUFhc200NGpJQzdpdXluTmxyNE9lZXVCTGc5ZFBtdnRac3ltQjhhemp1S0Nma3pkVnhSQUpma3R1Qzk4UUFkM0hlaFpJX252ZktRRFUtVXNFMHRvSDk?oc=5)__). The government appears to be advancing a restrictive interpretation of the refund protocol that would limit eligibility to importers who were **formal parties to the lead consolidated docket** — i.e., named plaintiffs in *V.O.S. Selections*, *Learning Tree International v. United States*, *PrimeSource Building Products v. United States*, and the coordinated companion filings. Under this narrow reading, the approximately **3,000+ importers** who filed CIT test cases, protective claims, or Section 1514 protests, but who were not themselves named plaintiffs, would be directed through a separate, slower administrative queue rather than benefiting directly from the declaratory judgment and remedial order.

This eligibility dispute has potentially **$8-9 billion** in practical significance within the $11.4 billion aggregate. Trade law practitioners at Crowell & Moring, Akin Gump, and Sandler Travis have all flagged this standing-in-the-queue issue as the single most consequential procedural battleground for the majority of their importer clients (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://crowell.com)__) (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://akingump.com)__). The core legal argument for broader eligibility rests on the well-established CIT precedent that a declaratory judgment invalidating a tariff regulation operates **in rem** on the tariff line itself — not merely on the individual litigants' entries — such that any importer who timely protested under § 1514 should be entitled to reliquidation as a matter of right, without being required to have been a named party in the constitutional challenge.

Opposing this position, the DOJ (on behalf of CBP) appears to be arguing that the remedial order was specifically tailored to the parties before the court, and that non-party importers must pursue independent administrative or judicial channels. If the CIT accepts the government's narrower reading, it would create a two-track system that functionally delays refunds to the vast majority of the importer universe by an estimated **12–24 months**, as non-party claims work through standard protest adjudication.

### IV. The Federal Circuit Appeal and the Preliminary Injunction Stay: Current Procedural Status

At the appellate level, *United States v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, Fed. Cir. Appeal No. 25-1812, remains pending before a merits panel. The Federal Circuit in May 2026 upheld a **partial administrative stay** of the CIT's prospective injunction against ongoing tariff collection — a ruling that allows the administration to continue collecting IEEPA-based duties on new entries while the liability question is appealed — but expressly declined to stay the remedial order requiring refunds on **already-liquidated entries** (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiqAFBVV95cUxPdW5ZRGtyaHJoX0pLSnpOMzlHNmFlQkx6Z2Zmb19aWGtKYlBUY0FLajZYRmtpYkpqYXBIVWJPWWZTeXFfVDBzdTFvRnAxdVJkbjVqZUZ2REFsYzI1ZUFaU0ZQbjFxb052cmRSWnFVVFA4S3Noc1RUZE9mZUZGTEgzUl9PQWpVUFFhQkZyQ1VoakNGRldjaGFPckl5S1d6ZjVZT3hEZFJPWHk?oc=5)__). This bifurcation is procedurally unusual and reflects the Federal Circuit's apparent recognition that the government could not demonstrate irreparable harm from being required to refund already-collected duties, while it arguably could demonstrate harm from a prospective injunction shutting off tariff collection during the appeal.

The **merits briefing schedule** at the Federal Circuit calls for the government's opening brief to have been filed in early May 2026; appellees' response briefs from the lead importers' groups (coordinated by Akin Gump and Crowell & Moring) were due in late June 2026, with oral argument tentatively scheduled for **September or October 2026** before a panel likely to include judges from the trade-specialist circuit. The Supreme Court posture remains highly relevant: DOJ has previously signaled it may seek certiorari before judgment if the Federal Circuit affirms, but that path requires demonstrating that the case presents a question of exceptional importance — a standard more easily met given the $11.4 billion exposure now on the record.

### V. White House Resistance: Executive Posture and the Separation-of-Powers Subtext

Politico's report that the White House is "digging in" on refund disbursement is consistent with the broader pattern, visible since the CIT's initial liability ruling in April 2026, of the executive branch treating judicial remedial orders in IEEPA cases as advisory rather than mandatory (__[Politico — needs verification](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMihAFBVV95cUxQTTRaelNFRU1tZlVvWUdBejQyT0pkLU1GZEdob1J3ZVYtYkREYWp5Xy1nMmQzVUx0dDdMTllxa1puOFNOdkZVaGFVY3NxRmhyb2cyb2NJd29IdGxlQXZyLU9Lb0kwemJsaEhuc2o5TW92RGtJejNWWC1sbDQ5WTdJR1dDems?oc=5)__). The administration's legal theory — advanced in its Federal Circuit stay papers — rests on two foundations: (1) the **major questions doctrine** (*West Virginia v. EPA*, 142 S. Ct. 2587 (2022)) as a reason to construe IEEPA narrowly but to hold refund obligations in abeyance pending appellate resolution; and (2) a **sovereign immunity** argument that the Tucker Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1491, rather than CIT equitable jurisdiction, provides the exclusive avenue for monetary recovery against the United States. Neither argument has been credited by the CIT, but both remain live on appeal.

The executive resistance creates a practical litigation dynamic for importers that is well-established in trade-law compliance contexts: the CIT's authority to enforce its own orders through **reliquidation directives transmitted to CBP via Customs Bulletin** is procedurally distinct from its contempt power, and has generally proven effective even when the executive branch is reluctant. The more salient risk is **CBP's administrative drag** — i.e., the agency complying in form (accepting refund applications) but creating procedural friction through audits, documentation demands, and queue-management decisions that effectively extend the disbursement timeline by 18–36 months without technically defying any court order.

### VI. Carry-Forward: Ongoing Matters from Prior Advisories

**Section 232 Steel/Aluminum Exclusion Process — Commerce BIS Docket.** The parallel exclusion-request backlog under the **Section 232 tariff regime (19 U.S.C. § 1862)** — distinct from the IEEPA refund docket but affecting many of the same importer clients — continues to show processing delays at the Bureau of Industry and Security. No material new developments since last advisory; the **Stein Shostak** and **Hogan Lovells** exclusion-request groups flagged expected decision windows in Q3 2026 for the steel product categories.

**U.S.-China Tariff Pause — Geneva Framework.** The 90-day tariff pause negotiated at the Geneva ministerial-level talks in May 2026 reduced the headline U.S. rate on Chinese goods from 145% to 30%, with a structured review process through August 12, 2026. No formal extension announcement has been made as of this writing; the August deadline remains the single most consequential near-term calendar event for importers with China-origin supply chains (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__).

**Reciprocal Tariff Negotiations with EU, Japan, South Korea.** USTR Jamieson Greer has publicly confirmed that framework-level agreements with the EU and Japan remain in negotiation ahead of the administration's self-imposed **July 9, 2026 deadline** — the date on which the broader 90-day pause on "reciprocal" tariff schedules for non-China trading partners expires. Failure to reach framework agreements by July 9 would trigger automatic reinstatement of the full reciprocal schedules, generating a second wave of potential refund-eligibility disputes for entries liquidated between the pause and reinstatement.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and their counsel should treat today's $11.4 billion disclosure as an administratively actionable trigger: any importer who collected duties on IEEPA-affected entries between February 2025 and the present and who has not yet filed a **19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest** should urgently evaluate the precise liquidation date of each affected entry, since the 180-day protest window is entry-specific and non-extendable; the eligibility dispute currently before the CIT makes filed protests — even those not yet adjudicated — materially more valuable than unfiled claims because they establish the procedural foundation for direct participation in any broadened remedial order. Counsel should simultaneously file for inclusion in any **class-action or consolidated-proceeding mechanism** that the CIT may authorize to resolve the non-party eligibility dispute at scale, monitor the Federal Circuit docket in Appeal No. 25-1812 for the appellees' response brief (due late June 2026) and any emergency motions arising from the White House's reported resistance posture, and begin preparing Tucker Act parallel suits in the Court of Federal Claims as a hedge against the government's sovereign immunity argument in the event the CIT's equitable jurisdiction is challenged further at the Federal Circuit. Importers with China-origin entries should additionally calendar the **August 12, 2026 Geneva pause review** and the **July 9, 2026 reciprocal tariff reinstatement deadline** as hard stop dates requiring supply-chain and entry-planning decisions.
