# IEEPA TARIFF ADVISORY | May 4, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The week ahead represents a critical inflection point for the post-IEEPA tariff landscape, with three convergent developments testing the durability of the refund framework that has taken shape since the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*. CBP's CAPE portal, now entering its third week of operation, has processed over 75,300 declarations covering 11.22 million entries, of which approximately 1.74 million have cleared all validations and entered the refund pipeline — a throughput rate that, while representing only about 3% of submitted entries, places the agency on track to begin disbursing the first actual refund payments on or around May 11, per Judge Eaton's April 30 confirmation. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-tariff-refunds-ieepa-timeline-may-2026/)) The velocity of processing, however, obscures a significant access gap: NPR reported Saturday that more than two-thirds of eligible importers have not yet filed CAPE declarations, with small businesses in particular struggling with portal access errors, multi-hour CBP helpline hold times, and data-formatting rejections that have affected roughly one-third of submitted claims. ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/03/nx-s1-5805045/tariff-refunds-small-business)) The Cato Institute has warned that the federal government could retain billions in duties owed to importers who simply fail to navigate the administrative process — a structural inequity that compounds as the 90-day voluntary reliquidation window narrows for the earliest IEEPA entries.

Tomorrow's opening of the USTR Section 301 public hearings at the International Trade Commission marks the formal acceleration of the administration's tariff replacement strategy, with testimony on structural manufacturing overcapacity across 16 economies — including China, the EU, Japan, India, and Mexico — scheduled to run through May 8. ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/ustr-launches-awaited-section-301-investigations)) Trade Representative Greer has stated publicly that the administration intends to conclude these investigations and propose remedies before the July 24 expiration of the 10% Section 122 global surcharge, a timeline that would require determinations within approximately 80 days of the hearing's conclusion. The parallel forced labor investigation covering 60 economies, for which hearings concluded April 28, adds a second channel through which new tariffs could be imposed without the statutory rate caps or durational limits that constrain Section 122. ([Baker Botts](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/march/trade-policy-plan-b-goodbye-ieepa-tariffs-hello-section-301-investigations)) Companies should understand that Section 301 tariffs, once imposed, carry no sunset provision and no rate ceiling — the administration is building the legal architecture for a permanent replacement of the IEEPA regime the Court struck down.

The consumer pass-through litigation wave continues to expand in both scope and dollar figures. The April 27 class action against Walmart, filed in the Northern District of Ohio, alleges $10.2 billion in potential "double recovery" — the largest single-company refund figure cited in any consumer lawsuit to date — claiming Walmart passed IEEPA tariff costs to shoppers through higher prices while simultaneously seeking full government refunds. ([Claim Depot](https://www.claimdepot.com/cases/walmart-class-action-alleges-102b-tariff-refund-windfall-at-consumers-expense)) This follows earlier suits against Costco, Lululemon ($240 million), Nintendo, and FedEx, with Arnold & Porter and Covington both publishing analyses warning that any company with IEEPA refund claims exceeding seven figures should anticipate consumer class action exposure. ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/03/the-next-wave-of-tariff-litigation)) The legal theory — that companies which raised consumer prices to offset tariff costs and then recovered those same costs from the government have been unjustly enriched — remains untested at the merits stage, but the sheer volume of filings and the congressional attention now focused on pass-through accountability (Representatives Horsford and others sent letters to major retailers on April 23) suggest this will be a sustained litigation front through 2026 and beyond. ([Horsford Letter](https://horsford.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/horsford.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/quill-letter-l36017-letter-to-corporations-re-tariff-refund-accountability-version-3-04-23-2026-11-05-am.pdf))

The government's continued silence on appeal remains the most consequential non-event in the refund timeline. With the approximate deadline for seeking rehearing of the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling approaching in late May to early June, and the separate appeal deadline for Judge Eaton's CIT refund orders in *Euro-Notions Florida v. CBP* falling around June 8, the absence of any DOJ filing, motion for extension of time, or public signaling of appellate intent now carries meaningful weight. The administration has instead invested institutional capital in building and operating CAPE — a posture inconsistent with challenging the underlying judgment that requires the refunds. Market pricing for tariff refund claims, however, continues to discount for residual appeal risk alongside setoff concerns and the consumer litigation overhang, with Sidley Austin's April 30 analysis flagging Anti-Assignment Act complications for outright claim purchasers and recommending obligation-to-pursue structures for the refund financing market. ([Sidley Austin](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2026/04/ieepa-tariff-refund-claims-key-considerations-for-lenders-borrowers-and-claims-purchasers))

## Recommended Actions

Companies with unliquidated IEEPA entries or entries within the 90-day voluntary reliquidation window should treat CAPE declaration filing as urgent — the access difficulties reported by small importers underscore the importance of engaging a licensed customs broker or trade counsel to navigate data-formatting requirements and avoid the one-third rejection rate that has affected early filings. Importers who have not yet verified their ACE portal credentials and importer-of-record documentation should do so immediately, as CBP helpline wait times have extended to multiple hours during peak periods. Companies anticipating IEEPA refunds above seven figures should proactively engage litigation counsel to assess consumer class action exposure, particularly if tariff costs were passed through to end consumers via price increases during the IEEPA period, and should consider whether voluntary pass-through programs or pricing adjustments may mitigate the "double recovery" theory now being tested in multiple jurisdictions. For entries that have already liquidated and fall outside the 90-day reliquidation window, the pathway remains filing a protest under 19 U.S.C. §1514 within 180 days of liquidation or pursuing an individual action at the CIT under 28 U.S.C. §1581(i), and companies should calendar these deadlines against their specific entry liquidation dates without delay.
