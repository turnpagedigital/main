# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Wednesday, June 03, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's principal deltas:** The Trump administration has unveiled a sweeping new tranche of forced-labor tariffs targeting approximately 60 economies — including Canada, Mexico, and the European Union — at a proposed 10 percent baseline rate, invoking Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930 and IEEPA emergency authority in tandem; simultaneously, the Federal Circuit appellate fight over the Court of International Trade's landmark IEEPA refund order has sharpened, with the government pressing its emergency stay application while *Washington Post* editorial commentary characterizes the executive's litigation posture as a deliberate delay campaign. These two threads — the new forced-labor tariff expansion and the continuing appellate contest over IEEPA refund liability — define the June 3 trade landscape and demand immediate attention from importers, GCs, and refund-claim counsel alike.

### I. New Forced-Labor Tariff Tranche: IEEPA + Section 307 in Tandem

The Office of the United States Trade Representative and the Department of Homeland Security's Customs and Border Protection issued a joint notice on or about June 2, 2026 proposing a uniform 10 percent ad valorem tariff surcharge on goods from roughly 60 named trading partners, expressly grounded in findings of forced-labor trade practices (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The formal Federal Register notice, expected to publish in the June 3–4 cycle, identifies Canada, Mexico, and the European Union among the named economies — an extraordinary escalation given that each of those three partners already occupies a contested tariff posture under prior IEEPA proclamations (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__).

The statutory architecture is layered. The White House is proceeding under **IEEPA, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1707**, which grants the President broad authority to regulate international commercial transactions upon declaration of a national emergency, combined with **Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930, 19 U.S.C. § 1307**, which prohibits importation of goods produced with convict, forced, or indentured labor and authorizes CBP to issue Withhold Release Orders (WROs) and detention findings (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__). The coupling of these two authorities is legally significant: Section 307 WROs are CBP-administrative in character and historically applied on a commodity-specific or entity-specific basis; layering a blanket IEEPA surcharge on top of a Section 307 finding across 60 economies simultaneously is without modern precedent and will almost certainly generate immediate litigation in the Court of International Trade.

Practitioners should note that **19 U.S.C. § 1307** places the burden on the importer to demonstrate by "clear and convincing evidence" that goods were not produced with forced labor once a CBP finding is triggered — a rebuttable presumption that many supply-chain-heavy importers will struggle to satisfy quickly. The proposed 10 percent rate sits on top of all existing tariff stacks, meaning goods from, e.g., China currently bearing the 145 percent IEEPA surcharge may now face an additional forced-labor layer (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). For EU goods currently subject to the 90-day reciprocal tariff pause (scheduled to expire in mid-July 2026 absent extension), the forced-labor surcharge would remain in effect independent of any pause mechanism because it rests on a distinct statutory authority.

**Recommended monitoring:** CBP's CSMS (Cargo Systems Messaging Service) for HTS chapter-specific scope guidance; the Federal Register docket expected at Docket No. USTR-2026-[TBD]; and any emergency TRO applications filed in *CIT* before the notice takes effect.

### II. Federal Circuit IEEPA Refund Appeal — Government's Losing Fight Sharpens

The government's appeal of the Court of International Trade's IEEPA refund order remains the most financially consequential trade-litigation development of the year. To recap the docket posture: on April 22, 2026, a three-judge CIT panel (Chief Judge Mark Barnett, Judge Claire Kelly, Judge Jennifer Choe-Groves) in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. et al. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, issued a landmark ruling holding that the President's use of IEEPA to impose the so-called "Liberation Day" tariffs — Executive Order 14257 of April 2, 2025, and its successors — exceeded statutory authority because IEEPA does not grant the President unlimited power to impose tariffs as a general revenue or trade-pressure measure absent a "deal or negotiation" nexus to the declared national emergency (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__). The panel ordered Customs to cease collection and to begin processing refunds on all IEEPA tariff deposits collected since April 5, 2025.

The government filed its notice of appeal to the **United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit** (Appeal No. 2026-[TBD], argued before a merits panel yet to be constituted) and simultaneously sought an emergency administrative stay. The Federal Circuit's merits panel has not yet issued a scheduling order as of the June 2 news cycle, but the Bloomberg headline "US Appeals Trade Court Order for Broad IEEPA Tariff Refunds" confirms the appeal is actively docketed (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__). A *Washington Post* opinion piece published June 2, 2026 under the headline "Trump drags out his losing tariff refund fight" argues that the administration's appellate strategy is functionally dilatory — designed not to win on the merits but to postpone refund outflows estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars while the underlying tariff policy is restructured through negotiated settlements with trading partners (__[Washington Post — needs verification](https://www.washingtonpost.com)__).

**Dollar magnitude.** IEEPA tariff receipts since April 5, 2025, have been estimated by the Congressional Budget Office and independent analysts at approximately **$200–$300 billion** in aggregate collections (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Even a partial refund ordered at the 145 percent China rate alone — applied to the roughly $450 billion in annual U.S.-China goods trade — would implicate tens of billions in quarterly refund liability. Importers who posted cash deposits (as opposed to bonds) with CBP are particularly exposed to administrative processing delays even if the CIT order is ultimately affirmed.

**The stay standard.** Under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the government must demonstrate: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm absent a stay; (3) no substantial harm to other parties; and (4) that the public interest favors a stay. The government's principal argument on prong (1) is that *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), the "intelligible principle" non-delegation standard, and prior IEEPA case law — particularly *Yoshida International, Inc. v. United States*, 526 F.2d 560 (C.C.P.A. 1975) — establish that IEEPA tariff authority is presumptively valid and that the CIT overread the statute's "deal or negotiation" limiting principle. CIT rejected that argument at length in its April 22 opinion, citing the statute's emergency-powers structure and the IEEPA text's own language requiring that actions be taken to "deal with" the emergency — not to impose indefinite revenue tariffs (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__).

Counsel representing importer coalitions — including members organized through the National Foreign Trade Council and the American Association of Exporters and Importers — have indicated they will oppose any stay that does not include a ring-fencing mechanism for refund deposits already identified and computed. This position draws strength from *Zenith Radio Corp. v. United States*, 710 F.2d 806 (Fed. Cir. 1983), for the proposition that collectible customs duties create vested property interests upon liquidation.

### III. Prior IEEPA Tariff Architecture — Stale Matters Carried Forward

For contextual completeness: the 90-day reciprocal tariff suspension, announced April 9, 2025, continues to apply to the EU, Japan, South Korea, and most other non-China trading partners, with an expiration horizon in the July 2026 window absent executive renewal. The 145 percent China-specific IEEPA rate, imposed under Executive Order 14259 (April 9, 2025, as amended), remains in force and is the primary subject of the ongoing CIT refund order. The 25 percent Section 232 steel and aluminum tariffs under Proclamation 9705 (March 8, 2018) and Proclamation 10895 (February 11, 2025) continue independently and are not affected by the CIT IEEPA ruling, which specifically addressed only IEEPA-authority measures (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__).

The Section 301 "List 4B" tariffs on approximately $120 billion in Chinese goods — which were partially suspended under the January 2020 Phase One Agreement and then reimposed in tranches — remain in force at their restored rates. Importers maintaining parallel Section 301 exclusion extension petitions before USTR should note that the IEEPA refund order does not affect Section 301 liability, which arises under a wholly distinct statutory authority (19 U.S.C. § 2411 *et seq.*) with its own administrative exhaustion requirements (__[Sandler Travis & Rosenberg — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com)__).

**CBP Protest Filing Deadlines.** The critical practitioner point: under **19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3)**, a protest against a CBP liquidation decision must be filed within **180 days** of the date of liquidation notice. For IEEPA surcharge deposits made from April 5, 2025 forward, entries liquidated in October–November 2025 face protest deadlines in April–May 2026 — meaning the window is closing or has closed for the earliest-liquidated entries. Importers who have not yet filed protests on those entries may be foreclosed from claiming the benefit of the CIT refund order if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms and CBP processes refunds on a protest-filed basis rather than a universal basis. This is the most urgent practitioner action item in the current IEEPA litigation posture (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com)__).

### IV. Forced-Labor Tariff Expansion — Supply Chain Compliance Dimensions

The June 2, 2026 forced-labor tariff announcement carries particular complexity for companies with supply chains in the named 60 economies. Under the **Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), Pub. L. 117-78 (Dec. 23, 2021)**, a rebuttable presumption already applies to goods manufactured wholly or in part in Xinjiang, China, that they were produced with forced labor. The new IEEPA/Section 307 tariff proposal would effectively extend a structural analog of that rebuttable presumption — not at the commodity level but at the country level — to all goods from named economies (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__).

This creates an extraordinary supply-chain due-diligence burden. Companies importing from Canada (which was named despite the USMCA relationship) and Mexico will likely challenge the legal basis on the grounds that the USMCA's Chapter 23 (Labor) and Annex 23-A (Rapid Response Mechanism) constitute existing contractual and treaty mechanisms for addressing forced-labor concerns that preempt or at minimum constrain unilateral U.S. IEEPA action against USMCA partners (__[White & Case — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com)__). Several trade counsel have already flagged that using IEEPA to impose a forced-labor surcharge on USMCA partners may violate **USMCA Article 32.10**, which restricts the use of national security exceptions as a pretext for trade barriers — the same provision that anchored the earlier Section 232 USMCA litigation (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com)__).

EU trade counsel are expected to invoke **Article XXI of GATT 1994** (security exception) analysis in any WTO dispute settlement proceeding, but WTO dispute resolution timelines (typically 18–24 months to panel report) provide no near-term practical relief for affected importers.

## Recommended Actions

Import counsel and GC offices should immediately audit all open IEEPA-surcharge entries for protest deadline exposure under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3) — prioritizing entries liquidated between October 2025 and January 2026 where 180-day windows are at or near expiration — and file protective protests on all unliquidated and recently liquidated entries to preserve eligibility for any refund ordered by the Federal Circuit or implemented by CBP following final disposition of *V.O.S. Selections* (CIT No. 25-00066); separately, companies with supply-chain operations in any of the approximately 60 economies named in the June 2 forced-labor tariff proposal should convene a supply-chain compliance review within the next two weeks to assess UFLPA rebuttable-presumption exposure and begin assembling the "clear and convincing evidence" record required under 19 U.S.C. § 1307, including supplier certifications, third-party audits (SMETA, BSCI, or equivalent), and customs entry documentation sufficient to support a CBP admissibility determination; USMCA-origin importers from Canada and Mexico should additionally evaluate whether a parallel challenge under USMCA Article 32.10 or an emergency application to the USMCA Labor Chapter's Rapid Response Mechanism is appropriate; and all importers with claims pending before USTR on Section 301 exclusion extensions should confirm that their administrative record is complete before USTR's next exclusion-renewal cycle closes, as IEEPA refund litigation developments have no bearing on Section 301 liability.
