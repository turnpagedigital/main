## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: The Government's Federal Circuit Appeal Splits the Importer Universe — and $30B+ Hangs in the Balance

The single most consequential development this week is the government's June 3, 2026 appeal to the Federal Circuit challenging Judge Richard K. Eaton's universal IEEPA refund orders as impermissible injunctions — and the practical consequence now cascades through the entire CAPE pipeline. 
On June 3, the government filed its appeals in the Federal Circuit, again pressing its universal-injunction argument.
 
The federal government is expected to argue that the CIT's refund orders constitute impermissible universal injunctions that extend relief to importers who did not file protective actions at the CIT.
 The stakes are not abstract: 
these entries — those liquidated more than 80 days ago — may account for an estimated $30 billion or more in unlawfully collected IEEPA tariff payments.


The practical division that appeal creates is now official. 
The government's appeal has divided the importer universe into two groups: those who filed at the CIT and those who have not. For importers in the latter category, the risk is no longer theoretical. The government's current position is that importers who did not file at the CIT may wait indefinitely — and perhaps permanently — for refunds of finally liquidated entries.


Meanwhile, CAPE Phase 1 has processed an enormous volume, but the finish line for the full $166 billion refund pool is not yet in sight. 
EAC Thomas reported that Phase 1 of CAPE has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected and that approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.
 The government has projected meaningful near-term disbursements: 
the government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.

### CAPE Phase Architecture — Where Your Money Is in the Queue

The mechanics now matter as much as the merits. CAPE was developed under court supervision, anchored by Judge Eaton's April 7, 2026 order in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, CIT No. 25-00595 — the successor lead case after Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States, CIT No. 26-1259, was voluntarily dismissed. 
CAPE Phase 1 opened on April 20, 2026. CAPE was developed and implemented pursuant to an April 7, 2026 order of Judge Richard K. Eaton of the CIT in Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States and Euro-Notions Florida Inc. v. United States.


As of the June 10, 2026 Brandon Lord declaration filed with the CIT, the pipeline numbers are: 
Mr. Lord's declaration filed on June 10, 2026 states that 125,576 CAPE declarations have passed validation, meaning have been accepted. Those claims involve 16.74 million entries, of which 10.60 million have been processed.


Phase architecture going forward breaks into three tranches:

- **Phase 2:** 
Phase 2 — covering reconciliation and antidumping/countervailing duty entries — is set to launch June 29, 2026.
 CBP's June 4 EAC Thomas declaration confirmed this timeline. 
CBP says it will need each importer of record's number approximately two weeks prior to deployment
 — meaning the data submission window to qualify for Phase 2 on schedule is effectively **now** (by June 15 at the outside).

- **Phase 3:** 
Phase 3 is targeted for late July 2026 and will cover finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period. Critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.


- **Excluded category:** 
CBP has said any importer whose entries have finally liquidated and who has not filed its own claim at the CIT will NOT be eligible for a refund unless and until there is a court order in favor of such importer to receive any IEEPA refunds. CBP says this issue is on appeal and so until it is settled, CBP will not expand CAPE in this context.


One procedural development warrants close attention: 
On June 4, Terry Precision Cycling, a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) case, moved in V.O.S. Selections to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows for class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response is due June 25, 2026.
 If the class certification motion succeeds, it could provide a vehicle to extend CAPE Phase 3 coverage to non-filing importers without relying on the contested universal-injunction theory — a distinction that may prove decisive at the Federal Circuit.

The government's DOJ position rests on an argument that the CIT's universal refund order collides with the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, which tightened the standard for nationwide injunctions. 
DOJ asserts that the universal injunction exceeds the CIT's jurisdiction under the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, which sharply restricted lower courts' authority to issue nationwide injunctions.
 Importers' counsel have a structural counter-argument: 
the CIT is the only court that can ever consider the IEEPA refunds issue, meaning any ruling is automatically universal.

### Section 122 — Thirty Days to Expiration; Federal Circuit Has Restored Collections

The Section 122 surcharge under Proclamation 11012, 91 Fed. Reg. 9339 (Feb. 20, 2026), entered on February 24, 2026 at 10% ad valorem, is scheduled to lapse by its own statutory terms on **July 24, 2026** — thirty days from today. 
Through Proclamation 11012, the administration imposed a 10% ad valorem surcharge on articles imported into the United States, subject to specified exceptions, effective February 24, 2026. The surcharge is currently set to expire on July 24, 2026.


The CIT (Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly, 2–1) ruled on May 7, 2026, in *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606, that Proclamation 11012 was invalid because it failed to identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficit" required by 19 U.S.C. § 2132 as it was understood at enactment in 1974. 
The CIT held, in a 2–1 split opinion, that the President's action did not meet the statutory criteria required to invoke Section 122 and that three importer plaintiffs had sufficient standing to challenge the Section 122 duties. The CIT also granted summary judgment to these importer plaintiffs and entered a permanent injunction against the United States' collection of the Section 122 duties from these three importer plaintiffs. The CIT held that the remaining state plaintiffs were not importers, thus did not have standing to challenge the Section 122 duties, and dismissed their claims.


The Federal Circuit entered a full stay pending appeal on June 11, 2026. 
On June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805), consolidated appeals from two decisions of the CIT that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs imposed under Proclamation No. 11012 and Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. While the appeal proceeds on the merits, the government may continue to collect the Section 122 "balance-of-payments" tariffs as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions, including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc., pending further order of the Federal Circuit.


The panel applied the four-factor *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009) test and found three of four factors favored the government. 
The panel found that the government made a sufficient showing of likely success on the merits, but it expressly declined to adopt any final interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit" at this stage. The court found the legislative history cited by the government and by Judge Stanceu's dissent sufficient to call into question the CIT majority's three-measure framework and was unpersuaded "at this time" by plaintiffs' nondelegation arguments.


The practical consequence: 
although the Section 122 tariff has now been held unlawful, because the injunction is limited to prevailing parties at the CIT, other importers must continue to pay the Section 122 duties until the courts or the Administration direct otherwise.
 The tariff expires on its own in thirty days. For most non-plaintiff importers, the better-leveraged question is not whether to litigate the Section 122 charge but whether to maintain records sufficient to support a future CAPE-style refund claim if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT on the merits. 
Refunds, if any, may very well be administered through the CAPE system created for the IEEPA duty refunds, rendering priority concerns moot, and the Section 122 tariff is scheduled to lapse on July 24 in any event. For lower-volume importers and those unburdened with cash flow concerns for the duration, the best course of action for now may be to pay the Section 122 duties where no exemption is available, to monitor appellate proceedings for developments, and to maintain records to be used in a future claims system.

### Section 301 — The Tariff Successor Regime Takes Shape

The administration's stated intent throughout the Section 122 litigation has been to replace the expiring 150-day surcharge with Section 301 tariffs derived from investigations initiated in March 2026. 
Many expect new Section 301 tariffs to be announced by July 24, 2026, the date the Section 122 tariffs expire.


The most recent USTR section 301 activity is broad. 
The U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) announced new tariff proposals pursuant to its Section 301 investigation into 59 countries and the EU for forced labor violations.
 A proposed 25% tariff on Brazil was announced separately. 
USTR determined that Brazil's acts, policies, and practices relating to digital trade, electronic payment services, preferential tariffs, anti-corruption enforcement, intellectual property protection, ethanol market access, and illegal deforestation are unreasonable and burden U.S. commerce, making them actionable under Section 301. USTR has proposed a 25% tariff on all goods imported from Brazil, subject to product exclusions, and is soliciting public comment.
 
Public comments are due by July 6, 2026, and public hearings are scheduled for July 7, 2026.


Unlike Section 122, Section 301 tariffs are not time-bounded, not capped at 15%, and Congress has no required approval role. 
The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump Administration that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Courts have rejected prior attempts to limit the scope of Section 232 and Section 301.
 GCs and CFOs who have modeled refund recovery based on a return to lower duty rates post-Section 122 should recalibrate: the Section 301 successor regime could restore effective duty rates in the 20–25%+ range for many HTS classifications.

### IEEPA Refund Litigation — The Forced Labor Forbes Headline and Broader Background

The Forbes headline in the live news scan — "Trump's 'Forced Labor' Tariffs: If At First You Don't Succeed…" — points to a pattern the desk has been tracking: the administration's serial invocation of alternative statutory authorities after judicial invalidation. The Section 301 forced labor investigations targeting 59 countries, combined with the administration's explicit readiness to invoke additional Section 232 modifications, confirm that the post-IEEPA tariff architecture will be layered and authority-diverse. 
The Trump administration has recently made several trade policy and tariff litigation announcements affecting companies importing goods into the United States and downstream buyers.
 The combined effect — IEEPA gone, Section 122 expiring, Section 301 and Section 232 scaling up — means the refund arithmetic for any individual importer depends critically on which authority covered which entry and at what rate.

On Section 232, a recent modification is already operative. 
On June 1, 2026, President Trump issued a proclamation further modifying the Section 232 tariff regimes on aluminum, steel, and copper imports established under Proclamations 9704, 9705, 10962, and 11021. Key changes are effective June 8, 2026.

## Recommended Actions

Importers who have not yet filed a protective Section 1581(i) complaint at the CIT should treat today's date as the effective deadline for action on finally liquidated entries: the government's Federal Circuit appeal in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 26-1898 (filed June 2, 2026), places non-filing importers outside the Phase 3 CAPE window (targeted late July 2026), and the government's position — that it may permanently retain an estimated $30 billion in finally liquidated duties from non-plaintiffs — is no longer a negotiating posture but an active appellate argument. Importers with entries approaching the 180-day post-liquidation protest window under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 should file protective protests immediately; once a liquidation becomes final, the only available pathway is a CIT complaint under § 1581(i). For Section 122 surcharges paid since February 24, 2026, maintain documentation in the CAPE format (ACE Entry Summary Detail Report ES-003, Chapter 99 HTSUS codes 9903.03.01) and monitor whether the Federal Circuit merits panel (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805) accelerates briefing ahead of the July 24 statutory expiration; a merits ruling before expiration is unlikely but would control Section 122 refund eligibility. Watch the government's June 25 response to the Terry Precision Cycling class-certification motion in *V.O.S. Selections*, CIT No. 25-00066 — if certified, a class vehicle may render the universal-injunction fight moot for non-filing importers with finally liquidated entries. Section 301 forced-labor investigation public comments close July 6; companies with exposure in the 59 targeted countries (including EU, UK, Canada, Mexico, Cambodia, Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia) should submit product-specific factual records now.
