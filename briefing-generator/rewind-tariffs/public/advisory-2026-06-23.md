## Analysis & Developments

### I. The Refund Pipeline's Critical Fault Line: Finalized Entries and the Class-Action Gambit in *V.O.S. Selections* (CIT No. 25-00066)

The dominant operational development in the IEEPA refund landscape as of this week is not in the courts of appeals — it is back in front of Judge Richard K. Eaton at the CIT, where the question of whether finally liquidated entries will ever reach the CAPE pipeline is coming to a head. 
On May 27, 2026, Judge Eaton issued an order to show cause in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump* (CIT No. 25-00066), directing CBP to explain, by June 9, 2026 at 1:30 p.m. EDT, why the court should not reinstate its prior order requiring immediate refunds for all entries subject to IEEPA tariffs.


The government's response, filed May 29, drew a line the DOJ has been walking toward for weeks. 
DOJ filed a motion in *V.O.S. Selections* to amend the court's order, asserting that CBP lacks authority to reliquidate or issue refunds for finally liquidated entries without a court order; that the court has not, to date, provided any importer-specific relief in this context; that the CAPE portal lacks the necessary functionality to process such entries; and that CBP cannot implement such relief absent a court order directing it.
 That position — issued simultaneously with a parallel CBP filing — signals that the government intends to appeal the universal scope of the refund orders. 
On May 29, 2026, CBP filed a motion with the CIT in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, clarifying that CBP does not believe it has authority to issue refunds of IEEPA tariffs that are both liquidated and finalized without a court order, and that it intends to appeal the universal injunction issued by the CIT with respect to those IEEPA tariffs.


The numbers underscore the stakes. 
CBP has noted that more than 330,000 importers paid or deposited approximately $166 billion in IEEPA duties over more than 53 million entries.
 The CAPE pipeline, as of June 10, 2026, has processed a material but still incomplete slice of that universe: 
per a declaration filed by CBP's Mr. Lord on June 10, 2026, 125,576 CAPE declarations have passed validation, covering 16.74 million entries, of which 10.60 million have been processed and the IEEPA duties approved for refund, with amounts approved for refund totaling $23.68 billion of the $166 billion total.


The newly critical legal question is the treatment of importers whose entries have finally liquidated and who have *not* filed a CIT case. 
CBP has stated that any importer whose entries have finally liquidated and who has not filed its own claim at the CIT will not be eligible for a refund unless and until there is a court order in favor of that importer to receive IEEPA refunds, and that this issue is on appeal.


The class-action play offers the sharpest available workaround. 
On June 4, 2026, Terry Precision Cycling — a plaintiff in another § 1581(i) case — moved in *V.O.S. Selections* to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows class actions to have universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response is due June 25, 2026.
 That deadline is tomorrow. The court's resolution of the class-certification motion — combined with the appeal of the universal injunction in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States* (Fed. Cir. No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026) — will determine whether the estimated tens of billions in finally liquidated entry refunds are in the pipeline or stuck in individual litigation for years.

Phase 2 CAPE is the next mechanical milestone. 
Entries subject to reconciliation — for which the reconciliation entry has not yet been filed — will be eligible for CAPE refunds during Phase 2, which begins June 29, 2026. CBP requires each importer of record's number approximately two weeks prior to deployment.

### II. Section 122 Surcharge: 31 Days to Expiration, Federal Circuit Stay Intact, Section 301 Successor Regime Racing the Clock


On May 7, 2026, the U.S. Court of International Trade held that Proclamation 11012, which imposed a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132), was invalid because the proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficits" required by the statute.
 The panel was divided: 
the CIT majority comprised Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly, who held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted, and instead improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 Judge Stanceu dissented.

Relief remains confined to three plaintiffs. 
The CIT found that only the direct importer plaintiffs — Burlap and Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington (through the University of Washington's direct import activity) — had standing. The remaining 23 state plaintiffs were dismissed because their alleged injuries, based on indirect passthrough costs, were deemed too speculative to support standing. The CIT issued a permanent injunction limited to those plaintiffs and declined to enter a universal injunction.


The government appealed the next day and obtained interim protection from the Federal Circuit. 
The DOJ appealed this decision, and on May 12, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay of the CIT's ruling while it considers the government's motion for a stay pending appeal. On May 20, 2026, the CIT denied the government's separate motion for a stay of enforcement of its judgment pending appeal. Nevertheless, the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remains in place.
 The consolidated appeals proceed under a single briefing schedule.

The practical reality: 
most importers are not covered by the injunction, and CBP is expected to continue assessing and collecting Section 122 tariffs while the case proceeds on appeal. Importers should not assume the ruling automatically results in across-the-board relief.


The clock on the underlying authority expires in 31 days. 
The surcharge is currently set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 
The administration has initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, and it reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.

### III. The Section 301 Successor: Forced Labor + Brazil, July 15 Finalization Deadline

The administration's tariff-reconstruction effort is moving faster than most importers anticipated. On June 2, 2026, USTR published findings on 60 economies under a Section 301 forced-labor investigation. 
The USTR published a Federal Register Notice announcing its determinations and proposed actions, finding that all 60 investigated economies have failed to impose or effectively enforce a forced labor import prohibition, including major trading partners such as China, Japan, the United Kingdom, India, South Korea, Brazil, Australia, and Vietnam.


On Brazil specifically, USTR proposed layering an additional 25% tariff on top of the forced-labor finding. 
On June 1, 2026, USTR published a Federal Register notice proposing a 25% Section 301 tariff on all goods of Brazil, with two major carve-outs: articles already covered by Section 232 tariffs (steel, aluminum, copper, and certain heavy equipment), and an annex listing more than 1,600 Harmonized Tariff Schedule subheadings exempt, including roughly 430 lines that apply only to civil aircraft uses.
 The policy intent is explicit stacking: 
it appears that the 25% Brazil tariff is intended to stack on the 12.5% tariff proposed under the forced labor imports investigation for a cumulative rate of 37.5%.


The procedural calendar is tight. 
Written comments are due July 1, 2026, and a public hearing is scheduled for July 6, 2026, at the U.S. International Trade Commission in Washington, D.C. USTR faces a July 15, 2026 statutory deadline for taking responsive action.
 That July 15 deadline falls nine days before the Section 122 surcharge expires on July 24, creating a window of potential overlap — or gap — in coverage. 
Between July 15 and July 24, there is a period of regulatory uncertainty, as it remains unclear whether any tariff imposed under Section 301 would be added on top of the existing Section 122 tariff or whether the two measures would be mutually exclusive.


The strategic architecture USTR is building is deliberately more litigation-resistant than IEEPA. 
USTR Jamieson Greer and other trade officials have indicated that these investigations are designed to rebuild the tariff leverage lost following the Supreme Court's February 2026 decision invalidating IEEPA tariffs. The Section 122 global tariff of 10% that replaced IEEPA is a temporary stopgap set to expire on July 24, 2026. With that expiration approaching, the Brazil and forced labor investigations represent the first major wave of a broader Section 301 regime intended to recreate the country-specific tariff structure built under IEEPA, but on a statutory foundation that is significantly harder to challenge in court.
 
These investigations mark an unprecedented use of Section 301, as there have never been this many simultaneous Section 301s on this many countries.

### IV. The Governing Framework: *Learning Resources* / *V.O.S. Selections* — Settled Law, Unsettled Mechanics

The foundational ruling has not changed since it was decided February 20, 2026. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court issued its decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250), holding 6-3 that IEEPA does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs. The same day, President Trump issued an Executive Order rescinding IEEPA tariffs and directing agency heads to terminate collection of those tariffs as soon as practicable.


Chief Justice Roberts authored the majority. 
The majority opinion, written by Chief Justice John Roberts, was joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, Neil Gorsuch, Amy Coney Barrett, and Ketanji Brown Jackson, stating that IEEPA does not give the President the power to set tariffs.
 
Justices Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Alito dissented, concluding that IEEPA authorizes tariffs and that the President acted within delegated authority.


The Court's dispositions on the two dockets were asymmetric: 
the Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs; in No. 24-1287 (Learning Resources), the judgment was vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction; in No. 25-250 (V.O.S. Selections), the judgment was affirmed.
 The jurisdictional holding — that challenges to IEEPA tariffs belong exclusively in the CIT under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) — is the procedural gateway that now channels all refund litigation through Judge Eaton.

Refund mechanics remain genuinely contested. 
The Court did not address whether refunds must be issued or how any reimbursement should operate.
 
Secretary of the Treasury Bessent, in wake of the decision, implied that if tariffs were to be refunded, it would be up to the lower courts on remand — and not Treasury — to decide how they would be paid.

### V. Legislative Track: Tariff Refund Act of 2026 (S. 3905) and Competing Consumer-Rebate Bills

Congress has not acted, but the legislative record is building. 
The Tariff Refund Act of 2026 (S. 3905) would require CBP to refund all duties imposed under IEEPA within 180 days of enactment, with interest, and would prioritize small businesses in the refund process.
 
The Trump Tariff Rebate Act (H.R. 6781) and the American Consumer Tariff Rebate Act of 2026 (H.R. 7865) are consumer-oriented tax measures that would provide rebates to individuals and families through the tax code rather than mandating CBP to expedite importer refunds. None has passed as of this writing, but S. 3905 in particular could affect the refund timeline and process if enacted.


The distinction between the bills matters operationally: S. 3905 routes relief directly to importers of record through CBP; the House bills bypass CBP entirely and deliver value to downstream consumers through the tax code. If S. 3905 advances, it would preempt the CAPE litigation entirely for IEEPA duties and potentially accelerate recovery by 12–18 months relative to the current appellate timeline.

## Recommended Actions

Counsel for importers and claim holders should take the following steps before the end of this week: (1) **Monitor June 25 government filing in *V.O.S. Selections* (CIT No. 25-00066)** — DOJ's response to Terry Precision Cycling's class-certification motion is due tomorrow; a grant or denial of class certification will determine whether non-CAPE-eligible, finally liquidated entries have a judicial path to refund without individual CIT litigation, and the government's brief will also reveal its full appellate theory on the universal-injunction question in *Euro-Notions* (Fed. Cir. No. 26-1898). (2) **Register for CAPE Phase 2 now** — Phase 2 opens June 29, 2026, for reconciliation entries; CBP requires importer-of-record numbers approximately two weeks before deployment, meaning the registration window is already open. 
Importers of record with entries that have liquidated and are at risk of becoming finalized — i.e., approaching the 180-day protest window post-liquidation close — should immediately consult legal counsel.
 (3) **File CIT protective actions under § 1581(i) for finally liquidated entries** — CBP has stated unambiguously that it will not refund finally liquidated entries without a court order specific to the importer; the protest route under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 may be running or expired for many early-2025 IEEPA entries; a § 1581(i) complaint preserves the refund right independent of CAPE. (4) **Review Section 301 exposure for Brazil-origin goods** — with written comments due July 1 and the USTR's July 15 statutory deadline, importers sourcing from Brazil should submit comments on HTS classification relative to the 1,600+ exempt subheadings annex and model the stacking scenario: 25% (Brazil § 301) + 12.5% (forced-labor § 301), net of any Section 232 exclusions. 
For lower-volume importers unburdened by cash-flow concerns, the best course on Section 122 duties for now may be to pay the surcharge where no exemption is available, monitor appellate proceedings, and maintain records for use in a future claims system, given that refunds may be administered through the CAPE system created for IEEPA duty refunds.
