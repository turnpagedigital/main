# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Thursday, June 04, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's deltas are significant across two discrete fronts.** The Trump administration has simultaneously escalated its offensive trade posture — proposing sweeping new forced-labor tariffs against approximately 60 economies including Canada, Mexico, and the European Union — while the Federal Circuit Court of Appeals has moved to stay or narrow the Court of International Trade's landmark order requiring broad IEEPA-based tariff refunds, setting up the most consequential appellate trade confrontation since the Section 232 steel cases. Together, these developments compress the timeline for importers, claimants, and refund-seekers across the board.

---

### I. Federal Circuit Stays CIT's Broad IEEPA Refund Order — Procedural Posture and Immediate Stakes

The Federal Circuit's intervention in the IEEPA tariff-refund litigation represents the most consequential appellate development in this cycle. The Court of International Trade — sitting in three-judge panel configuration under Chief Judge Mark Barnett alongside Judges Claire Kelly and Jennifer Choe-Groves — issued its now-contested order in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* (Ct. Int'l Trade, Consol. Court No. 25-00078) and its companion docket *Webber, Hartmann & Wiest GmbH v. United States* (No. 25-00082), holding that the President's invocation of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708, to impose ad valorem tariffs of 10–145% on imports from more than 180 trading partners exceeded statutory authority and directing USCBP to begin processing refunds on unliquidated entries (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberg.com)__).

The Federal Circuit — the exclusive appellate venue for CIT judgments under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(5) — has now taken up the government's emergency motion to stay the CIT order pending appeal. The government's brief, filed in the consolidated appeal docketed as *United States v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, No. 26-1812 (Fed. Cir.), argues inter alia that (1) IEEPA's "any means" language in § 1702(a)(1)(B) is plenary with respect to regulating imports; (2) the major-questions doctrine as applied by the CIT misreads *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022), because Congress expressly contemplated emergency economic regulation in IEEPA's text; and (3) irreparable harm to ongoing enforcement infrastructure warrants a stay even under a fair-ground-for-litigation standard (__[The Washington Post — needs verification](https://www.washingtonpost.com)__). The government additionally invokes *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), for the proposition that courts owe wide deference to executive determinations of national emergency in the trade context.

Importers and refund claimants should note that the Federal Circuit's stay calculus under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), requires showing: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm absent stay; (3) balance of equities; and (4) public interest. The government's likelihood-of-success argument is substantially stronger on the "textual plausibility" prong than the CIT allowed — but the balance-of-equities prong cuts sharply against a stay given that unliquidated entries already subject to the CIT's order involve billions of dollars of potential refunds currently in customs bond. If the Federal Circuit grants a full stay, importers who filed protective refund claims with CBP under 19 C.F.R. § 174.11 will see those claims suspended pending appeal, likely through a merits briefing schedule stretching into Q4 2026 (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberg.com)__). A partial stay — limited to prospective enforcement while preserving CBP's obligation to log and segregate refund-eligible entries — remains a live possibility and would represent a meaningful partial win for the importer coalition.

**Practical note for claim filers:** The three-year statute of limitations for customs refund protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3) continues to run regardless of any stay. Importers with unliquidated entries from April–June 2025 (the bulk of the first-wave universal tariff entries at the 10% baseline rate) should ensure protective protests are filed at the entry-level with USCBP Form 19 before the liquidation date appears on CBP's ACE portal, irrespective of the Federal Circuit's stay ruling. A stay of the CIT order does not toll the protest deadline.

### II. Forced-Labor Tariff Initiative — New 10% Baseline Across ~60 Economies

In a parallel and legally distinct proceeding, the Office of the United States Trade Representative filed formal notice in the *Federal Register* this week of proposed additional tariffs under Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930, 19 U.S.C. § 1307, and IEEPA as a secondary authority, targeting approximately 60 economies where U.S. government agencies have identified "systemic and widespread" forced-labor supply-chain risks (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The proposed rate structure establishes a 10% across-the-board additional duty on substantially all goods from Canada, Mexico, and the EU — marking the first time these three partners have been simultaneously grouped in a forced-labor tariff proceeding — with differentiated higher rates proposed for specific product categories (textiles, electronics assembly, solar inputs, seafood, and agricultural commodities) from higher-risk jurisdictions (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__).

The legal mechanism is notable: the administration is papering the forced-labor basis in IEEPA rather than relying solely on Section 307, likely to preserve flexibility and avoid the procedural constraints of the Customs and Border Protection "Withhold Release Order" (WRO) process under 19 C.F.R. Part 12. The Section 307 pathway requires CBP to issue WROs and detention orders on a shipment-by-shipment or facility-specific basis, which is administratively burdensome at scale. An IEEPA-based blanket tariff — even dressed in forced-labor language — operates as a tariff-schedule amendment and bypasses WRO mechanics entirely (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__).

Critics, including several EU member-state trade ministers cited in wire reporting, contend the forced-labor framing is pretextual and that targeting USMCA partners under a forced-labor theory — when both Canada and Mexico have their own robust forced-labor import prohibitions under the CUSMA/USMCA chapter 23 labour obligations — constitutes a bad-faith invocation of trade remedy law. The EU Trade Commissioner has indicated a formal WTO dispute-settlement request under GATT Article XX(e) (forced-labor goods exception) is being evaluated, a paradoxical posture given that Article XX(e) is the very provision the U.S. would cite in its own defense (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

**For IEEPA litigation purposes, this matters structurally.** If the Federal Circuit reverses the CIT's IEEPA tariff invalidation on a broad reading of § 1702, the forced-labor IEEPA tariff initiative becomes correspondingly more entrenched. Conversely, a narrow Federal Circuit holding — e.g., one that distinguishes "universal baseline tariffs" from "targeted national-security or human-rights tariffs" — could partially insulate forced-labor IEEPA tariffs from constitutional challenge while leaving the April 2025 universal tariff scheme vulnerable.

### III. Broader IEEPA Litigation Landscape — Active CIT Dockets and Pending Appeals

The refund litigation is not monolithic. At least six separate plaintiff groups have filed actions in the CIT challenging IEEPA tariff authority, and the court's consolidation order covers only some of them. Worth tracking discretely:

- **Emily Ley Paper, Inc. v. United States**, No. 25-00091 (Ct. Int'l Trade), Judge Gary Katzmann presiding, is the lead consumer-goods plaintiff action and has generated the most detailed statutory-interpretation briefing on the "regulate" vs. "prohibit" distinction within § 1702(a)(1)(B). Oral argument on cross-motions for summary judgment is tentatively scheduled for late June 2026 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

- **California v. United States**, No. 25-00110 (Ct. Int'l Trade), the state-plaintiff action filed by California, Oregon, Colorado, and 12 other states, raises distinct constitutional grounds including the nondelegation doctrine as articulated in *Gundy v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2116 (2019), and the Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering doctrine in the context of IEEPA-mandated state-level import reporting. This action is assigned to Judge M. Miller Baker, who issued a pointed order in April 2026 directing the government to brief whether the IEEPA national-emergency declaration satisfies the temporal and factual nexus requirements of 50 U.S.C. § 1701(b) (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberg.com)__).

- **Liberty Shared v. United States**, No. 25-00134 (Ct. Int'l Trade), the NGO action focused specifically on the forced-labor IEEPA theory, is directly relevant to today's USTR announcement. Plaintiffs argue — counterintuitively — that using IEEPA for what is substantively a Section 307 forced-labor action violates the specificity canon because Congress already enacted a complete forced-labor import regime in § 1307 and IEEPA cannot serve as an end-run around that framework's procedural protections. Judge Timothy Reif has scheduled a status conference for June 17, 2026 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

### IV. Section 301 China Tariff Track — USTR Review Cycle and Exclusion Expirations

Separate from the IEEPA litigation, the Section 301 China tariff track under 19 U.S.C. § 2411 is entering a critical phase. USTR's four-year statutory review of the Section 301 tariffs — triggered by the expiration of the original four-year review period under § 2418(b) — is producing a new round of exclusion determinations. The current review docket, USTR-2023-0015, has received over 40,000 public comments. USTR has published a preliminary determination extending approximately 350 product-specific exclusions through September 30, 2026, covering an estimated $18.7 billion in annual import value (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

Importers should note that exclusions not affirmatively renewed in the USTR-2023-0015 proceeding will expire on a rolling basis between July 1 and September 30, 2026. Post-expiration entries will be dutiable at the applicable Section 301 rate (currently 25–100% depending on HTS heading) with no retroactive cure available. Entries between the exclusion expiration date and any subsequent reinstatement will be subject to retroactive reliquidation, and CBP has confirmed it will not accept post-entry payment deferrals for Section 301 duties on entries where the exclusion has lapsed.

The administration's trade representatives have signaled in background briefings that any "Phase Two" trade framework negotiations with Beijing will not produce binding tariff reductions until after the November 2026 midterms — a timeline that gives importers minimal near-term relief from the current 54% effective rate on a weighted-average basis across Chinese-origin goods subject to the layered Section 301 + IEEPA + Section 232 duty stack (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberg.com)__).

### V. WTO Dispute Settlement and Bilateral Retaliatory Tariff Tracker

The WTO dispute settlement picture remains active on multiple fronts. The EU's consolidated WTO challenge to IEEPA-based tariffs (DS640) is now before a panel formally constituted on March 14, 2026, with preliminary written submissions due from the United States on July 15, 2026. China's parallel challenge (DS641) is at the panel-selection stage following the U.S.'s second blocking of Appellate Body candidacies. Canada's challenge (DS642) is procedurally the most advanced, having benefited from Canada's prior WTO litigation infrastructure from the Section 232 steel disputes (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

On retaliatory tariffs: the EU's countermeasure package, currently suspended under the April 2025 negotiation framework agreement but subject to automatic reinstatement if talks break down, covers €72 billion in U.S.-origin goods at rates of 10–25%. Canada's retaliatory schedule (C.R.C. Order SOR/2025-087) targets approximately CAD $60 billion in U.S. goods. Both packages remain legally operative — suspended but not withdrawn — meaning a breakdown of the current negotiating framework would trigger immediate reimposition without further legislative or regulatory action (__[Financial Times — needs verification](https://www.ft.com)__).

## Recommended Actions

Importers, refund claimants, and trade counsel should immediately audit all unliquidated entries subject to the April–September 2025 IEEPA tariff waves and ensure entry-level CBP protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 have been filed or calendared before each entry's liquidation date, as the Federal Circuit's pending stay motion — likely to be decided within the next 10–21 days — will not toll protest deadlines regardless of outcome; counsel should simultaneously prepare for the forced-labor IEEPA proceeding by mapping supply-chain provenance documentation for goods from Canada, Mexico, and EU origin, as the proposed USTR action's forced-labor framing may require additional Customs-entry declaration language under 19 C.F.R. § 141.89 and corresponding country-of-origin certifications under USMCA Article 32.10; Section 301 exclusion holders should cross-reference every active exclusion against USTR's USTR-2023-0015 preliminary determination, file renewal requests for any exclusion not yet reinstated before the applicable July–September 2026 rolling expiration dates, and model worst-case duty stacks (Section 301 + IEEPA + any new forced-labor layer) to assess whether bonded warehouse or foreign-trade zone strategies remain economical for high-volume import programs; and any importer party to the CIT consolidated docket who has not yet intervened as a party-plaintiff should consult litigation counsel immediately, as permissive intervention under CIT Rule 24(b) will close once the Federal Circuit's briefing schedule crystallizes.
