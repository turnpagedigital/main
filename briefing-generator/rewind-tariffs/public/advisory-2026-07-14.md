## Analysis & Developments

### LEDE: The Refund Pipeline Is Now Macroeconomically Visible — And the Clock on Section 122 Is Down to Ten Days

Yesterday's Treasury release confirmed what practitioners have been watching build in the CAPE pipeline: 
the June federal budget deficit reached $120 billion — a sharp reversal from the $27 billion surplus in June 2025 — driven by gross customs duty collections of $23.6 billion against refunds of $49.2 billion, producing a net customs outflow of $25.6 billion for the month.
 
The refunds began flowing in May after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs in February; June refunds were more than double the $22 billion returned in May, which had itself resulted in only a slight net outflow of $42 million.
 
Refunds for the two-month period totaled more than half of the estimated $166 billion the government is on the hook for repaying.
 For GCs and CFOs tracking recovery timing: the fiscal signal matters because it confirms Treasury is actually disbursing, not merely queuing — the debate now is Phase 3 scope, the DOJ's finally-liquidated legal position, and what replaces Section 122 in ten days.

### I. CAPE Pipeline: Phase 2 Open, Phase 3 Technically Ready by End of July — But DOJ Contests the Legal Basis


Approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries; CBP developed CAPE as the electronic, consolidated refund mechanism within ACE and launched Phase 1 on April 20, 2026.


**Phase 1 progress.** 
By the June 9 hearing before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* (CIT No. 25-00066), CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Susan Thomas reported that Phase 1 had accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, with approximately $23 billion approved and transmitted to Treasury for refund — this after CBP had processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, against only 338,000 refund entries processed in all of the prior fiscal year.
 
As of July 2, 2026, approximately $90 billion in claims had been accepted and roughly $24 billion transmitted to Treasury for payment.


**Phase 2.** 
Effective June 29, 2026, CAPE began accepting entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, and 06) for which the reconciliation entry (type 09) has not been filed, subject to the same 80-day liquidation window as Phase 1.
 
CBP estimates Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.


**Phase 3 and the finally-liquidated dispute.** 
Phase 3 of CAPE — which addresses finally liquidated entries — was confirmed on track for readiness by end of July 2026, per CBP's disclosures to Judge Eaton at the June 9 hearing.
 
The federal government continues to contest the legal basis for refunds on finally liquidated entries, and it remains unclear whether refunds will actually flow once the technical capability is in place.
 
In its June 4 court filing, DOJ stated that "Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final," and that to make refunds available for such entries, an importer must file suit and obtain a court order.
 
Customs officials estimated that entries under Phase 3 guidelines would account for approximately 6.9% of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected
 — roughly $11.5 billion at stake in the DOJ's contested posture.

**Warehouse entry cutoff.** 
Effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on a CAPE Declaration; warehouse withdrawals (Entry Types 31, 32, 34, 38) continue to be accepted, with refunds processed upon (re)liquidation of the associated warehouse entry.
 Importers with bonded warehouse inventory need to map their entry types immediately — this cutoff is not a proposed rule, it is operative now.

**Government appeal of refund order.** 
On May 29, 2026, DOJ filed a notice of appeal and a motion to amend the CIT's order, going to the Federal Circuit; the petition for a writ of mandamus argues that universal injunctions are unlawful.
 That appeal — docketed as *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 26-1895 (Fed. Cir.) — 
was received June 2, 2026, with the appellants' brief due August 3, 2026.
 
The government has moved to consolidate appeals No. 26-1895, 26-1897, 26-1898, and 26-1899.
 The Euro-Notions track — 
*Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, CIT No. 25-00595, on appeal at the Federal Circuit as Case No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026
 — presents the same finally-liquidated question and is a consolidation candidate. The DOJ's position, if accepted by the Federal Circuit, would cap the administratively recoverable universe at Phases 1 and 2 (~$130 billion) and require individual CIT suits for the remaining ~$36 billion.

**Practical note for non-CAPE filers.** 
CBP advises that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, absent a compliance concern requiring further review.
 
Importers with entries outside Phase 1 eligibility should be protesting within 180 days of liquidation to preserve legal rights, and should consider whether to join the group of importers that have already filed suit in the CIT under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i).

### II. Section 122: Ten Days to Statutory Expiry — No Congressional Extension, Section 301 Replacing It


On February 20, 2026, President Trump issued Proclamation 11012 invoking Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) to impose a temporary 10% ad valorem surcharge on virtually all imports into the United States, effective February 24 through July 24, 2026.
 
The Proclamation was issued the same day the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs.
 That 150-day authority expires in ten days. 
The President cannot extend Section 122 past July 24 unilaterally; the 150-day limit is statutory, and only Congress can extend it.


**The CIT litigation.** 
On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge CIT panel, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* (Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606), invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122, dealing another blow to the administration's trade agenda after the IEEPA defeat in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*.
 
The majority opinion, by Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly, held that Section 122 is not a roving license to impose tariffs whenever imports exceed exports; the phrase "large and serious United States balance-of-payments deficits" was treated as a term of art rooted in 1974 monetary context, not a synonym for a trade deficit or current account deficit.
 
Judge Stanceu dissented, arguing that the statute as written in 1974 did not limit the administration to such measures and that the proclamation had adequately identified reasonable understandings of the term.


**Appellate stay — injunction narrow.** 
The government appealed on May 8, 2026, and on May 12 the Federal Circuit granted an administrative stay, temporarily suspending the CIT's permanent injunction while the appellate court considers a longer-lasting stay.
 
The CIT found standing only for the direct importer plaintiffs — Burlap and Barrel, Basic Fun, and the State of Washington — dismissing the remaining 23 state plaintiffs for lack of standing, and declining to enter a universal injunction.
 With the Federal Circuit's stay in place and Section 122 expiring on its own in ten days, the appellate briefing on the Section 122 merits is largely academic for forward-looking duty exposure. The stakes are the refund question: 
Section 122 was imposed under entirely different statutory authority from IEEPA and is not part of the IEEPA refund program; there is no announced refund mechanism for Section 122 duties.
 
Although the legal framework regarding relief mirrors the IEEPA cases, CBP lacks discretion over whether to collect and assess Section 122 duties, making liquidations non-protestable in the absence of a universal refund order.


**Forward implication.** The Section 122 expiry is not a tariff-free event. See Section III below.

### III. Section 301 Architecture: USTR Must Finalize Before July 24 — Proposed 10%–12.5% Rates on 99.4% of U.S. Imports


On March 12, 2026, USTR initiated 60 Section 301 investigations related to the failure of various economies to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor.
 
These investigations — targeting 60+ economies — were explicitly designed to replace the IEEPA tariffs struck down by the Supreme Court, with a target timeline before Section 122 authority expires on July 24.



On June 2, 2026, USTR issued affirmative determinations in all 60 Section 301 forced labor investigations, concluding that every major U.S. trading partner has failed to adequately prohibit or enforce bans on imports of goods produced with forced labor; the 60 economies collectively account for 99.4% of all goods imported into the United States; and USTR proposed additional tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on imports.


The proposed bifurcation: 
10% Section 301 for 15 trading partners that have taken meaningful steps toward forced labor prohibition — including Canada, Ecuador, the EU, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, El Salvador, Guatemala, Malaysia, Taiwan, and the UK — and 12.5% for the remaining 45 trading partners that have failed to enforce, a group including China, India, Vietnam, Thailand, Turkey, and South Korea.



USTR accepted comments on the proposed tariffs through July 6, 2026, and held a public hearing on July 7, 2026.
 
USTR faces a July 20, 2026 completion deadline on the two Section 301 investigations it launched March 11.
 
Section 301 has no statutory rate cap and no time limit — which is exactly why the administration chose it.


**Rate-stacking risk.** 
The Section 301 tariffs, if finalized, apply on top of any other applicable duties.
 
Brazil faces a proposed 25% Section 301 tariff from the separate USTR investigation into digital trade and deforestation, which appears intended to stack on top of the 12.5% forced labor rate, for a potential combined rate of 37.5% on Brazilian imports.
 
Section 232 investigations into pharmaceuticals, pharmaceutical active ingredients, and medical devices are ongoing and could produce additional national-security tariffs in 2026 that would stack on top of any Section 301 rates.


**Key structural distinction from Section 122.** The shift from Section 122 to Section 301 resets the refund math entirely. 
The shift is not a simple swap: the architecture is meaningfully different, and for many importers, more complex. Section 122 was flat — one rate applied globally — and straightforward to model.
 Section 301 is country-specific and product-specific, with no rate cap and no sunset. Companies with DDP contracts or landed-cost budgets priced at the 10% Section 122 rate need to revise their models before July 24.

### IV. Appellate Architecture: Government's Federal Circuit Brief Due August 3 — The Finally-Liquidated Issue Will Define Phase 3

The government's consolidated Federal Circuit appeal — Nos. 26-1895, 26-1897, 26-1898, 26-1899 — with 
the appellants' brief due August 3, 2026
, is the most consequential near-term docket event for importers with finally liquidated entries. The core question is whether 19 U.S.C. § 1514 (protests) and § 1520(d) (reliquidation) give CBP the unilateral authority to reliquidate finally liquidated entries absent an individual CIT order, or whether importers must each obtain importer-specific relief under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i). 
CBP has stated explicitly that any importer whose entries have finally liquidated and who has not filed its own claim at the CIT will not be eligible for a refund unless and until there is a court order in favor of that specific importer.



Assistant Attorney General Brett Shumate wrote in a June 27 filing that "regarding importers with finally liquidated entries who have chosen not to file a lawsuit thus far, the government has consistently explained that CBP requires an order from the Court to reliquidate finally liquidated entries."
 
The plaintiffs in the case have said that individual companies should not have to each file legal challenges to get their money back and are asking the judge to certify a class, which would allow them to sue on behalf of a much larger group of importers.
 The class certification motion is the other pressure point to monitor: if Judge Eaton grants it, it transforms the litigation posture for the roughly $11.5 billion Phase 3 universe.


Practitioners advise: do not wait until the last day — get your complaint filed before the end of January 2027.
 The statute of limitations under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) for challenging agency action runs two years from the date of the entry, which for early IEEPA entries (February–March 2025) means the filing window closes in early-to-mid 2027.

## Recommended Actions

**For GCs, compliance officers, and CFOs with open IEEPA exposure:** Verify immediately whether your entries contain HTSUS codes 9903.01.xx or 9903.02.xx (IEEPA) versus 9903.03.xx (Section 122) — CAPE covers only the former, and the distinction is operative today. For Phase 1 and Phase 2 IEEPA entries, confirm CAPE Declarations have been submitted and note the 60–90-day disbursement window from CBP acceptance. For entries that are finally liquidated and not yet in CAPE, assess whether to file an individual CIT complaint under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) now rather than waiting for Phase 3 resolution — DOJ's position that CBP cannot reliquidate absent a court order is on appeal but is live policy at CBP today. On Section 122 (codes 9903.03.xx, entries February 24–July 24, 2026): there is no CAPE analog and no refund mechanism currently announced; preserve records entry-by-entry under HTSUS heading 9903.03.01, track the Federal Circuit's Section 122 merits appeal, and monitor whether USTR structures any transitional relief in the Section 301 finalization. On Section 301 replacement: audit every active DDP contract, landed-cost budget, and duty-drawback claim that was modeled against the 10% Section 122 flat rate before July 20 — the proposed replacement rates range from 10% to 12.5% (and higher for Brazil), are country- and product-specific, and carry no rate cap or sunset; the stacking risk with existing Section 232 steel/aluminum tariffs is real and needs to be quantified before the July 24 transition.
