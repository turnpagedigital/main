# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Monday, July 27, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The dominant development this week is the administration's seamless tariff handoff: the Section 122 global surcharge hard-expired at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, and USTR simultaneously activated new Section 301 forced-labor tariffs of 10–12.5% on imports from 60 economies.** For importers tracking the IEEPA refund pipeline, that transition matters on two levels: it resets forward duty exposure under a legally more durable authority while leaving the $80 billion in unresolved IEEPA refunds — and an open Section 122 refund question — still pending before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton at the CIT and before the Federal Circuit. Phase 3 of CBP's CAPE refund tool, covering finally-liquidated entries, was targeting a late-July launch as of the July 15 reliquidation order; the August 4 CBP compliance report will be the first hard checkpoint.

---

### 1. Section 301 Forced-Labor Tariffs: New Regime, New Litigation Risk


On July 23, 2026, USTR announced final action in its Section 301 investigations concerning the failure of 60 economies to impose and effectively enforce prohibitions on imports produced with forced labor. The new Section 301 tariffs took effect at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, immediately following the expiration of the temporary Section 122 duties.
 
The new duties range from 10% to 12.5%, subject to numerous product-specific and country-specific exclusions; USTR stated that the affected economies account for approximately 99.4% of U.S. imports.



On July 23, President Trump issued a memorandum directing USTR to impose tariffs on goods from 60 economies that USTR found had failed to impose or effectively enforce bans on imports made with forced labor; the memorandum sets the remedy and USTR's accompanying notice implements it by modifying the HTSUS, assigning Chapter 99 headings, establishing entry rules, and identifying applicable exclusions.
 
USTR had initiated the 60 investigations on March 12, 2026 — less than three weeks after the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize presidential tariffs.
 
The final action largely follows USTR's June proposal but expands the product exclusions, adds country-specific exclusions, adjusts the treatment of certain economies, and adopts MFN-based tariff floors for the European Union, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland.


The strategic architecture here is clear: Section 301 carries judicial precedent in its favor — courts have consistently upheld USTR's broad discretion under 19 U.S.C. § 2411 — and the administration moved to activate it with deliberate timing precision. 
By implementing the new tariffs under Section 301, the administration is relying on a tariff authority that has been upheld by U.S. courts in previous challenges.
 That said, 
it is reasonable to expect litigation against these Section 301 tariffs, with plaintiffs arguing that the Section 301 investigation into forced labor enforcement practices was predetermined and engineered to replace the Section 122 tariffs against the majority of trading partners.
 The Liberty Justice Center has already moved to challenge the new action. 
USTR created no exclusion-request process
 in the final action, which will complicate relief for importers with product-specific hardship claims. 
Products already covered by Section 232 duties — including steel, aluminum, copper and their derivatives; automobiles, automotive parts; medium/heavy-duty vehicles; buses; and timber, lumber, and derivatives — are specifically excluded from the scope of the forced-labor Section 301 action.


**Net duty calculation is non-trivial.** 
The current action reaches 60 economies, but the announced rate may not equal an importer's actual exposure — the duty turns on origin, classification, MFN rate, stacking rules, free-trade-agreement treatment, and general and country-specific exclusions.
 
The USTR determination provides broad product-level exemptions, detailed across several hundred pages of the Federal Register notice.

### 2. CAPE Phase 3 and the July 17 Reliquidation Order: The Pipeline Opens for Finally-Liquidated Entries

The refund arithmetic as of last week's court filings: 
as of CBP's July 10, 2026 court declaration, $86.3 billion has been repaid to importers (including statutory interest) out of roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties collected; $121.75 billion in claims have been accepted for processing as of July 10, up from ~$90 billion in mid-June; $104.29 billion in refunds were authorized as of the June 29 filing.
 
Analysts expect total refunds to land around $130 billion once all three CAPE phases run their course, before interest.
 Roughly $80 billion of the original pool remains unpaid.

The critical unlock for Phase 3 came on July 17. 
On July 17, 2026, CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton issued an order directing CBP to "reliquidate, without regard to IEEPA duties, any and all of Plaintiffs' entries that have been liquidated for more than 80 days and on which Plaintiffs made estimated deposits pursuant to IEEPA" — an order that followed the transfer to Judge Eaton's docket of more than 3,700 cases filed with the CIT seeking refunds of IEEPA duties ruled unconstitutional.
 
While CBP voluntarily established the CAPE system, the government's position had consistently been that a court order directing reliquidation of entries whose liquidation had become final would be necessary to proceed.
 That order is now in hand.


CBP will issue instructions to plaintiffs' counsel describing how to make the Phase 3 submission, after which plaintiffs may submit a CAPE declaration; CBP is next required to file a report on the progress of CAPE for these Phase 3 entries on August 4, 2026.
 That August 4 compliance report is the next hard date.


Critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 Importers who paid IEEPA duties on finally-liquidated entries but have not joined the litigation face a structurally different path: they will need to file protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or pursue separate CIT actions under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) before their refund claims can access the Phase 3 mechanism.

### 3. Section 122 Sunsets — Refund Question Survives the Expiration


The 10% global import surcharge that President Trump imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) expired by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 
Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation was pending; absent congressional action, the surcharge terminated on its own terms.


The expiration, however, does not resolve the refund question. 
The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality: the Court of International Trade held the surcharge unlawful, the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling pending the government's appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, and CBP continued to collect the duty through July 23. The July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period.


The underlying CIT ruling was squarely adverse to the government. 
Named plaintiffs — the State of Washington (through the University of Washington), Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — prevailed when the CIT issued Slip Opinion 26-47 in a 2-1 decision holding that Proclamation 11012, which imposed the 10% temporary import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 effective February 24, 2026, is unlawful; the majority concluded that the President did not identify a balance-of-payments deficit as Congress understood that term in 1974, with the Proclamation instead pointing to the trade deficit, the current account deficit, and the negative U.S. net international investment position.



On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit issued a stay of the CIT's injunction, which had prevented CBP from collecting Section 122 duties from the State of Washington and the two private importers.
 The Section 122 appeal now proceeds at the Federal Circuit with the surcharge itself gone but the damages question live. 
Section 122 is a separate 10% surcharge introduced February 24, 2026 under the Trade Act of 1974, and it is not refundable through CAPE.
 If the Federal Circuit affirms the CIT on the merits — which is not guaranteed given the stay — importers who paid the surcharge will need a separate CBP mechanism to recover those amounts. No such mechanism currently exists.

### 4. IEEPA Liability Architecture: What the Supreme Court Ruling Fixed and What It Did Not

The foundational posture has not changed since the last advisory, but it bears restating as importers evaluate the new Section 301 overlay. 
On February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court issued its decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* — two consolidated appeals concerning tariffs imposed under IEEPA — and, in an opinion authored by Chief Justice Roberts, held that IEEPA does not give the President authority to impose tariffs.
 
By a 6–3 vote, the Court agreed on the result, though the Justices differed in reasoning; six Justices joined the majority holding that IEEPA does not permit tariff imposition, while Justices Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Alito dissented.



On February 22, 2026, CBP issued CSMS #67834313 announcing that it would stop assessing IEEPA duties on goods entered for consumption or withdrawn from warehouse; however, ACE continued liquidating previously filed entries with assessed IEEPA tariffs — even after the Supreme Court's decision — creating practical complications for importers monitoring liquidation status and protest deadlines.


The refund architecture rests on three statutory pillars: (1) 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests for liquidated entries; (2) 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) for residual CIT jurisdiction; and (3) the CAPE administrative mechanism as CBP's operational implementation. Phase 3 operationalizes the § 1581(i) pathway for finally-liquidated entries via the July 17 reliquidation order. Importers who have not filed at the CIT before the government's stated Phase 3 cutoff face a limitations-period problem under § 1514(c) — the 180-day protest clock runs from liquidation, not from the Supreme Court ruling.

### 5. Section 301 Litigation Watch: Predetermined-Process Theory

The forced-labor Section 301 action is legally distinct from IEEPA and Section 122, but it will face a related challenge: whether the March 12 investigation initiation was designed from the outset to produce a foregone conclusion. 
Plaintiffs are likely to argue that the Section 301 investigation into forced-labor enforcement practices was predetermined and engineered to replace the Section 122 tariffs against the majority of trading partners.
 The administrative record — USTR's investigative timeline, public comment docket, and the three-day hearing — will be the battleground. 
Following investigations, public hearings, consultations with foreign governments, and review of more than 2,100 public comments, USTR finalized additional duties of either 10% or 12.5%, depending on the economy's forced-labor enforcement framework.
 That record depth will make a facial APA challenge difficult, but a targeted procedural attack on the pace and the predetermined outcome is a viable theory. 
As a practical matter, importers should expect these Section 301 tariffs to remain in place until either the investigated economy meaningfully addresses the underlying forced-labor enforcement failures or USTR modifies the action following further review, barring any court decisions in the coming months.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and their counsel should address five discrete items this week. First, verify whether your IEEPA entries are classified as finally-liquidated (more than 80 days pre-reliquidation) — if so, determine whether your entity is named in one of the 3,700-plus CIT cases transferred to Judge Eaton's docket; if not, file promptly under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or commence a § 1581(i) action before the protest window closes, as the government has taken the position that Phase 3 CAPE access is limited to active CIT plaintiffs. Second, monitor the August 4, 2026 CBP compliance report in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* (Court No. 25-00066) — that filing will confirm whether the Phase 3 CAPE technical build is operational and identify any further procedural requirements. Third, recalculate landed costs immediately for goods entering from all 60 Section 301 economies: the shift from a flat 10% Section 122 surcharge to a differentiated 10%/12.5% Section 301 regime with extensive country-specific and product-specific exclusions means prior models are stale; particular attention is warranted for goods already subject to Section 232, which are now excluded. Fourth, assess whether the Section 122 surcharge you paid (February 24 – July 23, 2026) is recoverable — it is not refundable through CAPE, the Federal Circuit has stayed the CIT's injunction, and no administrative refund mechanism currently exists; preserve your protest rights under § 1514 now and monitor *State of Oregon v. United States* at the Federal Circuit for any stay-lift or merits ruling. Fifth, evaluate whether the new Section 301 forced-labor action is vulnerable to a predetermined-process challenge — if your product or country exposure is significant, an early intervention in anticipated CIT litigation may be warranted to preserve class-of-entry arguments.
