## Analysis & Developments

### LEDE — Sixteen Days to Zero: The Section 122 Cliff Arrives July 24, and What Replaces It Is Already Being Challenged

The dominant development this week is structural, not incremental: 
the Section 122 surcharge — 10% ad valorem on virtually all imports, imposed under Proclamation 11012 and effective since February 24, 2026 — is scheduled to expire at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026.
 That is sixteen days away. 
The President cannot extend it unilaterally; Section 122 expires by law on that date.
 Congressional extension is a theoretical possibility but has attracted no material legislative traction.

The practical consequence for importers and refund claimants is a compressed, forking decision tree. The Federal Circuit appeal of the CIT's May 7 invalidation ruling is live — 
on June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit stayed the lower court ruling that had struck down the Section 122 tariffs, and went further than a routine stay, stating that the government is likely to succeed in overturning the CIT's decision.
 That stay language matters: it signals the Federal Circuit's preliminary view of the merits, and it effectively keeps the surcharge collectible through expiration for all importers. 
The stay applies even to the three plaintiffs who had previously won relief — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — meaning the 10% surcharge continues to be collected from all importers throughout the appeals process.


The administration's replacement strategy is now clear. 
Following the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling against IEEPA, the administration commenced two Section 301 investigations — one on forced labor, one on excess industrial capacity — widely expected to produce replacement tariffs before Section 122 expires.
 
On June 2, 2026, USTR published findings of the forced labor investigation and proposed new Section 301 tariffs covering 60 countries.
 
The hearing date in July 2026 closely tracks the Section 122 expiration date, and the proposed rates would provide continuity after the end of the Section 122 tariff action.
 This is the administration's stated architecture: 
having learned the lesson of the IEEPA defeat, the tariffs now come wrapped in an administrative record built through exactly the process that concludes this week — and the message of July 2026 is that the tariff era is not ending with the Section 122 sunset, it is being made permanent.

### Section 122 Appellate Posture — *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* (Fed. Cir., Nos. TBD; on appeal from Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606, May 7, 2026)

Recall the CIT's posture at the trial level. 
A 2-1 majority comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted, and instead improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 
The dissenting judge argued Section 122 should be read broadly as a delegation of congressional power, not a narrow emergency tool, and that the President satisfied the statute while courts should defer to the Executive in foreign economic matters.


The CIT's standing analysis limited the injunction's reach. 
The permanent injunction issued against collection of Section 122 tariffs extended only to the three importer plaintiffs found to have standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remained subject to Section 122 duties unless they brought their own action, the administration withdrew the tariffs, the tariffs expired, or a court ordered broader relief.


The government's appeal timeline is compressed by the hard expiration date. 
On May 8, 2026, defendants filed a notice of appeal with the Federal Circuit.
 
On May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay of the CIT's ruling while it considered the government's motion for a stay pending appeal.
 
On May 20, 2026, the CIT denied the government's separate motion for a stay of enforcement pending appeal; the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remained in place regardless.
 
The CIT characterized the government's asserted harms as speculative, noting CBP retains the ability to extend liquidation periods for up to three years to preserve collection of the surcharge if the government ultimately prevails.


The practical result: 
the Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026, in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely — including the recently-initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations, which the administration reportedly intends to conclude this summer before expiration.
 The Federal Circuit appeal will proceed as an academic exercise on the merits, but the refund question — whether importers who paid the surcharge are entitled to recover it — may outlive the surcharge itself.

### The IEEPA Refund Pipeline — *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump* and CBP's CAPE Process

The foundational posture is established. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed the Federal Circuit's en banc ruling that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs.
 
The Supreme Court's 6-3 ruling came down squarely on the side of the importers and the twelve state attorneys general who challenged the tariffs, holding that IEEPA's authorization to "regulate … importation" does not encompass the power to levy tariffs of unlimited scope, rate, and duration.
 The estimated exposure is approximately 
$175 billion in IEEPA tariff collections that the nation had accrued during the period of enforcement.



Following the ruling, the government ceased assessing and collecting importer deposits of IEEPA tariffs as of February 24, 2026; the Federal Circuit denied the Trump administration's efforts to delay litigation and repayment in the CIT for the V.O.S. Selections plaintiffs on March 2, 2026.
 
Judge Richard K. Eaton of the CIT ruled in a separate case on March 4, 2026, that all importers that paid tariffs under IEEPA were due refunds including interest from CBP, and that he would be ruling on all further actions related to these refunds; generally, importers pay estimated tariffs at the time of import, but in the ordinary "liquidation" process — taking approximately 314 days — the final tax bill is calculated and refunds or adjustments are made, as the government is expert in doing.



In March 2026, following the Supreme Court's ruling, the CIT issued an order directing nationwide refunds of unlawful IEEPA tariffs, including through liquidation of unliquidated entries and reliquidation of certain liquidated entries — even for importers that were not parties to the litigation.
 That nationwide scope is critical. Unlike the Section 122 injunction, the IEEPA refund order was not confined to named plaintiffs. The CBP Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) refund pipeline — commonly referred to internally as the CAPE process — is now processing liquidations across the importer population.

The mechanics are governed by 19 U.S.C. § 1514 (protest rights), § 1581(i) (CIT exclusive jurisdiction), and § 1520(d) (correction of clerical errors). Importers who filed timely protests under § 1514 within 180 days of entry liquidation are positioned to receive automatic refunds as CBP processes the entries through CAPE. Importers who missed that window face a harder road and may need separate § 1581(i) actions.

### Section 301 as the Replacement Architecture — Forced Labor Investigation and the Nations Pushback

The Politico-flagged development — nations pushing back on forced labor findings — is operationally significant here. 
Shortly after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA, the USTR commenced two Section 301 investigations on forced labor and excess industrial capacity of trading partners.
 
The USTR's June 2 findings are the most consequential tariff development since the Supreme Court's February ruling: the forced labor investigation covered 60 countries, with USTR determining that all 60 trading partners either lack a complete prohibition on imports of goods produced using forced labor, or fail to effectively enforce the prohibitions they have.


Pushback from trading partners on those forced labor findings is the logical precursor to two things: (1) bilateral negotiating pressure aimed at securing exclusions or reduced rates before Section 301 tariffs are finalized, and (2) reciprocal trade measures that could compress U.S. export markets. For refund-track importers, this matters because Section 301 — unlike IEEPA — has no rate cap and no time limit. 
Both Section 301 investigations were explicitly designed to produce replacement tariffs before Section 122 expires; Section 301 has no rate cap and no time limit.
 If finalized, Section 301 tariffs will apply prospectively and will not create retroactive refund rights for duties collected under IEEPA or Section 122. The refund opportunity is a legacy of the invalidated programs; it does not persist into the Section 301 era.

Country-specific exclusion petitions filed during the Section 301 comment and hearing process can, however, reset the math for importers who are currently paying the surcharge and face continued exposure. 
The proposed 10% rate for 15 trading partners closely aligns with rates that the Trump administration had negotiated with certain countries to reduce the IEEPA tariff rate on their goods; if finalized as proposed, this new Section 301 tariff action would reinstitute tariffs on those countries' goods on what the administration claims to be a more durable basis.

### Jurisdictional Backstop — *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 24-1287 (S. Ct. Feb. 20, 2026)

The Supreme Court's final holding has a jurisdictional dimension that practitioners should not lose track of. 
The government argued that the District Court lacked jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i)(1), which gives the CIT "exclusive jurisdiction of any civil action commenced against" the Government "that arises out of any law of the United States providing for tariffs" or their "administration and enforcement."
 
In the case of Learning Resources, the D.C. District Court's decision was vacated and remanded to be closed due to lack of jurisdiction, while for V.O.S. Selections, the lower court decision was upheld.
 The practical consequence: all future tariff-refund litigation — whether on IEEPA residuals or Section 122 challenges — runs through the CIT and then the Federal Circuit. There is no district court detour available.

The Supreme Court's reasoning rested on separation of powers. 
Writing for the majority, Chief Justice Roberts concluded that Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution vests the power to lay and collect taxes, duties, and imposts — including tariffs — exclusively in Congress; the Framers deliberately gave Congress alone control over the taxing power and did not vest any part of that authority in the executive branch.
 
Congress has consistently used explicit language when delegating tariff authority and has imposed defined limits on scope, duration, and procedure; in that context, the absence of tariff-specific language in IEEPA was decisive.
 This reasoning will be the template for any Section 301 challenge that emerges post-July 24 — challengers will need to distinguish Section 301's explicit grant of tariff authority, which the Supreme Court expressly noted as contrasting with IEEPA's silence.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and refund claimants should treat July 24 as a hard operational deadline across three distinct tracks simultaneously: (1) **IEEPA refund track** — confirm all entries covered by the March 2026 CIT nationwide refund order have been flagged in CBP's ACE system; where liquidation has already occurred, assess whether a timely § 1514 protest was filed (180-day window runs from liquidation date) and, where that window has closed, evaluate whether a § 1520(d) correction or separate § 1581(i) action is available; (2) **Section 122 refund track** — even though the surcharge expires July 24, the Federal Circuit appeal will determine whether the three named plaintiffs' permanent injunction (Burlap and Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and State of Washington) survives and whether the CIT's reasoning can support broader recovery; importers who paid the 10% surcharge should file protective protests under § 1514 against all Section 122 liquidated entries now, before the window closes, to preserve recovery rights if the Federal Circuit affirms the CIT on the merits; the Federal Circuit's stay language — indicating likely government success — counsels against assuming a clear path, but the cost of filing is low relative to the potential exposure; (3) **Section 301 watch** — review the USTR forced labor investigation's proposed rate schedule against your specific HTS classifications and country-of-origin exposure; comment periods and hearing dates are designed to close before July 24, meaning the Section 301 replacement architecture could take effect without a gap; identify products eligible for country-specific exclusion petitions and document the commercial disruption record required to support those petitions; note that Section 301 duties, if properly authorized, will not be subject to the same IEEPA-style major-questions challenge, and supply-chain restructuring begun now will have more lead time than adjustments made after the new rates are published.
