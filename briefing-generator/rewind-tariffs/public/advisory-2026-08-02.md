# ⚖️ Tariff Refunds | Sunday, August 02, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The week ending August 2 delivered three discrete shifts on the refund landscape that carry direct implications for recovery strategy: (1) CAPE Phase 3's deployment appears to have landed on schedule as the calendar turned to August, with court-ordered relief now flowing specifically to the ~3,700 importers who filed individual CIT suits — while non-litigants remain structurally excluded from finally-liquidated-entry refunds pending Tuesday's class certification hearing; (2) Amazon's Q2 earnings call, delivered July 30, produced the first major public data point on large-importer CAPE throughput — $600 million received, with a fraction flowing back to consumers; and (3) Holland & Knight's new alert anchors the current regime precisely: Section 122 expired at midnight July 23/24, Section 301 forced-labor tariffs activated at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24 at rates of 10% or 12.5% depending on a country's forced-labor-prohibition posture, and those new duties now constitute the operative tariff obligation for most importers going forward. Refund math and forward duty exposure have both changed materially in the past 72 hours.

---

### 1. CIT Refund Orders for Finally Liquidated Entries — ~3,700 Cases; Class Certification Hearing August 6

**Primary vehicle: *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066; *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 26-01088 — U.S. Court of International Trade, Sr. Judge Richard K. Eaton**


On July 15, 2026, CIT Judge Richard K. Eaton entered an order directing CBP to refund IEEPA duties on a plaintiff's finally liquidated entries.
 
The court indicated that substantially the same relief will be entered in the approximately 3,700 pending IEEPA cases, giving importers that filed individual CIT actions a court-ordered path to recover IEEPA duties on finally liquidated entries — while importers that have not filed suit currently have no comparable, confirmed mechanism for obtaining those refunds.


The orders are being issued case-by-case because 
the government's position is that a court order directing the reliquidation of entries whose liquidation has become final is necessary to provide CBP with the authority to reliquidate those entries and provide complete relief.



The class certification motion is particularly relevant to importers who paid IEEPA tariffs and whose entries are now "finally liquidated" — meaning duties were fixed more than 90 days ago. Those entries are the principal category of claims currently ineligible for CAPE, but the proposed class is defined by current CAPE ineligibility rather than by final liquidation alone.



Judge Eaton has said the court will lift the stay in *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 26-01088 — a separate IEEPA refund case in which a motion for class certification is currently pending.
 
The CIT's ruling on certification may determine whether affected importers can recover refunds through an administrative process or must file their own individual lawsuits.


Near-term docket: 
CBP's next CAPE status report is due by 5 p.m. ET on August 4, 2026, followed by a closed settlement conference on August 5. The class certification hearing for IEEPA duty refund plaintiffs is set for August 6, 2026.


The government's appellate posture remains hostile to universal relief. 
On June 2, 2026, the government appealed the refund order to the extent it requires CBP to issue refunds of finally liquidated entries to non-litigants, arguing this constitutes an impermissible universal injunction under *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, and that without a valid order, CBP lacks authority to issue such refunds.
 
For importers that have not filed suit, the path to refunds on finally liquidated entries remains unresolved — potential relief depends on proceedings before the Federal Circuit and unresolved class certification motions at the CIT, neither of which presently provides a certain outcome or timeline.


**Practice note:** The 180-day protest window under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 runs from liquidation independently of CAPE and of the Federal Circuit appeal. 
Filing protests now preserves refund rights through a separate channel regardless of how the appeal resolves.

### 2. CAPE Pipeline — $86.3B Repaid as of July 10; Phase 3 Deployed on Schedule


CBP's July 13 court filing shows $86.3 billion in IEEPA refunds actually repaid to importers, with $121.75 billion total accepted for processing as of July 10, 2026.
 
An analysis by the Cato Institute found that the $104.29 billion approved as of June 29 covered only approximately 30% of all IEEPA-affected import entries, meaning the dollars processed so far are concentrated in a small number of large, high-value entries while many smaller importers remain in the queue.


Phase sequencing: 
CAPE Phase 1 launched April 20, 2026, covering refunds for unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation. Phase 2 launched June 29, 2026, covering entries flagged for reconciliation where the reconciliation entry is not yet on file.
 
CAPE Phase 3 addresses finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs. Programming for Phase 3 was confirmed ready in late July 2026
 — meaning Phase 3 infrastructure should now be live as of this writing, though disbursement on finally liquidated entries for non-litigants remains legally blocked pending the Federal Circuit appeal.


Effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries are no longer accepted on CAPE Declarations; warehouse withdrawals continue to be accepted.
 
Effective June 29, 2026, CAPE accepts certain reconciliation-flagged entries (types 01, 02, and 06) where no reconciliation entry has been filed.



Per CBP court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid an estimated $166 billion across more than 53 million entries.
 At the current pace, the gap between accepted claims ($121.75B) and the full pool ($166B) — roughly $44 billion — reflects the contested finally-liquidated category, the government estimates at more than $30 billion, plus entries not yet submitted. 
Refunds include 6–7% statutory interest compounded daily; validate CBP's math against your own entry data before accepting a figure.

### 3. Amazon Q2 Earnings: $600M IEEPA Refund — Large-Importer Throughput Confirmed


On its Q2 earnings call on July 30, 2026, Amazon disclosed approximately $600 million in tariff refunds during the quarter, recorded in its North America segment. CFO Brian Olsavsky said that figure represents the significant majority of the refunds Amazon expects to receive.


The disclosure illuminates two structural points relevant to every importer's recovery posture. First, 
Olsavsky noted that Amazon received a "limited" refund amount because it worked to order and pre-position inventory in anticipation of the tariffs, and that Amazon is not the importer of record for the large majority of items sold in its store.
 Second, on pass-through: 
Amazon identified "a limited set of circumstances" where it can trace specific import charges passed to customers, and will proactively contact and automatically refund those customers — but for everything else, the money goes into pricing: "we'll utilize refunds to continue to invest in low prices for customers."


The broader corporate comparison is telling. 
Top logistics competitors UPS and FedEx have flagged nearly $6 billion in refunds they expect to pass along to customers. Retail giants including Walmart, The Home Depot, and Costco have all applied for refunds through CBP.
 
Apple reported separately that tariff refunds added 11 cents to quarterly earnings per share, a boost of approximately 5%.


The Amazon disclosure also reinforces the importer-of-record limitation: 
companies that were not the importer of record will likely have no direct recourse to seek refunds from CBP, but may have contractual or other claims against the importer of record.

### 4. Section 301 Forced-Labor Tariffs Live as of July 24 — Section 122 Expired on Schedule

The post-IEEPA tariff architecture completed its second transition. 
The new tariffs were announced around 5 p.m. ET on July 23, 2026, and took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24 — precisely when the Section 122 global surcharge under the Trade Act of 1974 expired, having served as the Trump Administration's temporary replacement for the IEEPA tariffs struck down by the Supreme Court.



The Section 301 forced-labor tariffs are either 10% for countries that USTR has determined have prohibitions on imports made with forced labor, or 12.5% for countries that have no such prohibitions.
 
Exemptions include products already subject to Section 232 tariffs, agricultural goods not produced domestically in sufficient quantities, and inputs used in U.S. manufacturing.



Section 122 expired and USTR's Section 301 replacement took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET July 24 — a two-tier 10%/12.5% tariff on roughly 60 economies, wider than the 46-country proposal. There was no gap between the two regimes.


The practical implications for refund planning: 
only tariffs paid under the invalidated IEEPA authority are eligible for refunds — duties paid under Section 122, Section 232, Section 301, or other authorities are not subject to the CIT's refund order.
 If the Section 122 surcharge litigation ultimately succeeds on appeal, a separate refund pool for February 24–July 23 Section 122 payments could open, but that remains speculative. 
Unlike IEEPA or Section 122, Section 301 tariffs carry no expiration date, and if they materialize at the scale the administration intends, they could approximate the breadth of the original IEEPA tariffs — on far more durable legal ground.
