## Analysis & Developments

### The Controlling Calendar: Twenty-Six Days to July 24

The single most time-sensitive variable in the IEEPA refund landscape right now is the Section 122 sunset. 
The Section 122 surcharge — Proclamation 11012's 10% ad valorem levy — is currently set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 That date is both a statutory floor and a strategic inflection point: if the Federal Circuit has not resolved the stay on appeal before then, the surcharge expires by operation of law regardless of how the merits come out, making the appellate question largely academic on the collection side while leaving open a substantial refund question for the $25 billion that has already been collected. 
Importers have paid an estimated $25 billion in Section 122 duties since February 24, just 72 days of collection.


The administration is not sitting still. 
The administration recently initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices, and reportedly intends for those investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.
 The race is explicit: pivot to § 301 authority before July 24 cuts off § 122 revenue. 
Companies should be aware that the administration continues to pursue alternative tariff measures, including investigations under Sections 301 and 232, and such measures may be announced before the current Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026, absent earlier judicial intervention.

### Section 122 Appellate Posture: Federal Circuit Stay Granted June 11

The critical update from last week: 
on June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's request to stay the CIT injunction pending appeal, meaning the government may continue collecting the 10% Section 122 tariffs while the appeal proceeds; in granting the stay, the Federal Circuit found that the government showed a sufficient likelihood of success on appeal and that the CIT's interpretation of Section 122 may be incorrect.
 That "likelihood of success" finding is the most significant legal signal yet that the Federal Circuit is skeptical of Chief Judge Barnett's statutory reading.

The underlying CIT ruling bears repeating for context. 
In a 2–1 decision, the CIT majority comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted and, instead, improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits.
 The case caption is *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, 
Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026).
 The dissent, by Judge Timothy Stanceu, argued that Bureau of Economic Analysis statistics on related measures could reasonably support a balance-of-payments finding, and that the majority erred procedurally by granting summary judgment without giving the government an opportunity to contest the plaintiffs' factual assertions. 
In the court's framing: "Nowhere does Proclamation No. 11012 identify balance-of-payments deficits within the meaning of Section 122 as it was enacted in 1974."



The Federal Circuit also found that the government would face irreparable harm without a stay, including trade disruption concerns and difficulties in administering tariff refunds if the injunction remained in place.
 
Attorney Josh Zive told Law360 that the government "may have a better chance of earning a favorable ruling at the Federal Circuit" because, unlike IEEPA, which does not mention the word "tariff," Section 122 provides the president with explicit authority to impose those duties under certain economic conditions.
 The practical consequence of the Federal Circuit stay is straightforward: 
continued tariff exposure for all importers; the Section 122 duties remain collectible, and the stay likely delays any plaintiff-specific refund relief that would have flowed from the CIT injunction.

### CAPE Phase 2 Launch: June 29 — Tomorrow

The IEEPA refund pipeline enters its next phase tomorrow. 
Following a recent hearing at the CIT, CBP announced that Phase 2 of the CAPE refund portal — covering reconciliation and AD/CVD entries — will launch on June 29; Phase 3, which targets entries that have finally been liquidated, is slated for late July.
 
CAPE Phase 2 is expected to handle a projected $28.7 billion in refunds in this phase, and close observers are gauging CBP's readiness to handle an estimated 2.8 million additional refund requests ahead of the launch.


Phase 2 matters because Phase 1 was structurally limited. 
CAPE Phase 1 covers approximately 82% of IEEPA entries and/or duty deposits, including unliquidated entries; entries with liquidation statuses of "Suspended," "Extended," or "Under Review"; warehouse and warehouse withdrawal entries; and entries liquidated within the preceding 80 days.
 Reconciliation and AD/CVD entries — among the most commercially significant for large-volume importers — were excluded from Phase 1. Phase 2 closes that gap.

The scale of the CAPE system is worth stating plainly. 
According to a CBP declaration filed with the CIT on April 28, 2026, importers and brokers had submitted approximately 75,300 CAPE declarations covering more than 11.2 million individual entries as of April 26, 2026, of which approximately 1.74 million entries had cleared all validations.
 
Several million entries have been rejected (about 15% overall), mostly for data entry errors or because they are not IEEPA payments and therefore not eligible.
 
CBP has not yet announced the scope of CAPE Phase 2, and only the importer of record is eligible to obtain refunds directly from CBP.
 Downstream parties — consignees, beneficial owners — must pursue contractual claims or negotiated arrangements with the importer of record.

The CAPE system operates under Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton's April 7, 2026 order in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection*, which is now the lead case. 
Judge Eaton designated *Euro-Notions* as the new lead case and issued an order mirroring the March 27 *Atmus* order, with the CIT suspending the requirement of immediate compliance to allow CBP to proceed with a phased rollout rather than requiring an immediate lump-sum payment.
 
CBP has clarified that refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed specific lawsuits, given the ongoing DOJ appeal.
 This is the sharpest practical constraint in the current posture: importers who did not join the CIT litigation — meaning the overwhelming majority — are currently channeled into the CAPE administrative process but may be locked out of Phase 3 entirely if the DOJ appeal succeeds.

### The DOJ Appeal of the IEEPA Refund Order: June Deadline and What Follows


The deadline for the government to appeal the CAPE/refund order was June 7, 2026; if an appeal was filed, several outcomes are possible: no stay granted — CAPE processing may continue while the appeal proceeds; stay granted — refund processing through CAPE could be paused pending appeal; or partial or full reversal — the scope of refund eligibility could be narrowed or otherwise modified.
 Whether DOJ filed by that deadline on the nationwide IEEPA refund order is not yet confirmed in the sources reviewed for this advisory. Importers and counsel should check the *Euro-Notions* docket directly. The risk is asymmetric: a successful government appeal could pause Phase 2 before it delivers the projected $28.7 billion.


On March 4, 2026, the CIT ordered that "all importers of record" that paid IEEPA-based duties are "entitled to the benefit of" the *Learning Resources* decision, and on March 27, Judge Eaton issued an amended order expanding the directive to cover all entries — including entries for which liquidation had already become final — and ordering CBP to liquidate or reliquidate all such entries without regard to IEEPA duties.
 
According to the government, importers have deposited or paid IEEPA-based duties on over 53 million entries, and as of March 4, 2026, about 20.1 million of those entries remained unliquidated.
 The unliquidated entries are the low-friction refund target; finally-liquidated entries require reliquidation and are the Phase 3 problem.

### The IEEPA Ruling — Ground Truth Recap

For new readers, the underlying mandate: 
on February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court issued its landmark decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, holding in a 6-3 ruling that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, and invalidating both the "Reciprocal Tariffs" first imposed on Liberation Day and the "Trafficking and Immigration Tariffs" relating to fentanyl.
 
The scale is unprecedented: Penn-Wharton Budget Model economists estimate that IEEPA-based tariff collections total approximately $175 billion to $179 billion.



The majority opinion was written by Chief Justice John Roberts and joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson.
 
In a portion of the opinion joined only by Gorsuch and Barrett, Roberts invoked the major questions doctrine, reasoning that Congress does not delegate "highly consequential power" through ambiguous statutory language.
 The dissent, by Justice Kavanaugh joined by Thomas and Alito, warned that the refund process would be a "mess" — a prediction that is tracking accurately.

The Section 232 and Section 301 tariff authorities were explicitly carved out: 
the Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump Administration that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, and courts have rejected prior attempts to limit the scope of those authorities.

### Section 301 Pipeline and the Post-July 24 Landscape


The USTR is soliciting public comments to help identify up to $30 billion in "non-sensitive" Chinese imports that could receive tariff relief under a newly chartered U.S.-China Board of Trade, with a deadline for comments of July 10.
 That process is both a diplomatic overture and a structural replacement for the tariff revenue that falls off July 24. Section 301 tariffs, once implemented, carry no 150-day cap and are subject to a different judicial review standard — one that, as Gibson Dunn noted, 
courts have historically declined to limit.


The administration's stated goal has not changed since February 20. 
Secretary of the Treasury Bessent stated that combining Section 122, Section 232, and Section 301 tariffs "will result in virtually unchanged tariff revenue in 2026," signaling the administration's resolve to find alternative avenues to reimpose the same levels of duties that were in place under IEEPA.
 For refund claimants, the practical implication is that even a successful Section 122 refund recovery does not end tariff exposure — it shifts the imposition vehicle.

## Recommended Actions

**For importers holding CAPE Phase 1 declarations:** Verify acceptance status in ACE before tomorrow's Phase 2 launch — rejected Phase 1 filings (~15% overall) will not automatically roll into Phase 2, and approximately 1.74 million entries have cleared validations as of the last CBP report. If you have not yet filed a lawsuit in the CIT, you should weigh the risk that Phase 3 refunds (covering finally-liquidated entries) will be restricted to litigating plaintiffs; the June 29 Phase 2 launch and late-July Phase 3 timeline are the operative deadlines. For Section 122 entries, continue filing protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 to preserve refund rights pending the Federal Circuit merits ruling — the June 11 stay on the *Burlap & Barrel* injunction means the surcharge remains collectible through July 24 at minimum, and any Section 122 refund path will likely mirror the CAPE model only if the CIT ruling is ultimately affirmed. Downstream parties who are not the importer of record must evaluate contractual arrangements now, before entries liquidate and the right to refund vests exclusively in the importer of record. Finally, monitor the *Euro-Notions* docket for any DOJ notice of appeal of Judge Eaton's reliquidation order, which — if filed before or around June 7 — could trigger a stay motion that pauses Phase 2 payouts; confirm directly with CIT filings rather than relying on secondary reporting.
