# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Saturday, June 06, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Today's most consequential delta is a two-front appellate collision at the Court of International Trade and the Federal Circuit: the government has moved to stay—and on at least one emergency track, has secured a partial administrative hold on—the CIT's sweeping tariff-refund order, while USTR simultaneously unveiled a new Section 301 forced-labor enforcement action proposing 10%–12.5% ad valorem duties on a broad swath of imports from countries identified as using state-sponsored or pervasive private forced labor. Both developments materially affect the Rewind Tariffs / Turnpage Digital Markets refund-recovery posture and require updated strategic positioning by claimants with pending or prospective IEEPA-basis refund claims. ### 1. Federal Circuit Emergency Stay & CIT Contempt Posture — IEEPA Refund Order

The government's emergency appeal of the CIT's tariff-refund order—reported by both Law360 and The Hill as of this week—marks a significant procedural escalation in what is now the central structural litigation over executive tariff authority. The underlying action, widely understood to involve challenges to IEEPA-based tariff schedules imposed through Executive Orders 14257 and 14259 (the "Liberation Day" and supplemental April 2025 rounds), produced a CIT merits ruling that the President lacked statutory authority under IEEPA, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708, to impose ad valorem import duties as a standalone trade-restriction mechanism untethered to a declared national emergency with the requisite nexus to the tariff remedy selected. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

The emergency posture now pending at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit involves two discrete but related procedural tracks: (1) the government's motion for a stay of the CIT's injunctive/refund-order component pending appeal, and (2) a separate administrative hold on the Customs chief's compelled deposition testimony, which the CIT had authorized as part of discovery into CBP's implementation mechanics for the refund protocol. The appellate court's decision to at least temporarily halt Customs Commissioner testimony is notable because it signals the Federal Circuit may be skeptical of the CIT's exercise of discovery jurisdiction over senior executive-branch officials in the context of an APA/IEEPA structural challenge—an issue that could eventually require the court to navigate *Cheney v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 542 U.S. 367 (2004), and its progeny on apex-official discovery. (__[The Hill — needs verification](https://thehill.com)__)

The refund order itself, if it survives the stay motion, would require CBP to process refunds of IEEPA-basis duties collected after a specified date—estimated in prior CIT proceedings at potentially $50–$80 billion in aggregate duty collections across the affected tariff classifications—though the precise universe of qualifying entries and the administrative mechanism for refund processing remain contested. Claimants with entries liquidated under HTSUS classifications carrying the IEEPA-basis Chapter 99 special program indicator codes should be tracking their protest deadlines carefully: the standard 180-day protest window under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3) runs from liquidation, and no court has yet certified a class or entered a universal tolling order that would protect latecomers. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

**Judge assignment:** The CIT proceedings have been before Judge M. Miller Baker (one of the lead judges handling the post-April 2025 IEEPA docket), though a consolidated track involving multiple importers has drawn in additional CIT judges on parallel dockets. The Federal Circuit emergency panel composition has not been publicly reported as of this morning. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

### 2. USTR Section 301 Forced-Labor Enforcement — New 10%–12.5% Duty Proposals

In a development that is analytically distinct from the IEEPA litigation but operationally significant for importers already managing stacked-duty exposure, USTR has formally initiated or advanced Section 301 investigations targeting imports produced with forced labor, proposing tariff rates of 10% to 12.5% ad valorem. The legal vehicle is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2411, which authorizes USTR to investigate and recommend retaliatory measures in response to foreign government acts, policies, or practices that are "unreasonable or discriminatory" and "burden or restrict United States commerce"—a standard that USTR has interpreted to encompass state-tolerated or state-directed forced-labor regimes as a form of unfair subsidy that distorts competitive conditions. (__[White & Case LLP — needs verification](https://whitecase.com)__)

The proposed 10%–12.5% rate is notably lower than the Section 301 China-origin rates (which range from 7.5% to 25% or higher on specific subheadings following the Section 301 List 1–4 modifications), suggesting USTR is calibrating the forced-labor Section 301 track as a targeted enforcement tool rather than a blanket escalation. Importantly, this proceeding operates entirely independently of the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), 19 U.S.C. § 4681 et seq., which imposes a rebuttable-presumption regime at CBP for goods produced in Xinjiang. The Section 301 forced-labor track can reach goods from countries and regions not currently covered by UFLPA entity lists—potentially including Malaysia, Bangladesh, Vietnam, and certain Gulf Cooperation Council states where USTR has flagged labor concerns in prior National Trade Estimate Reports. (__[White & Case LLP — needs verification](https://whitecase.com)__) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

For importers with complex supply chains, the stacking risk is acute: an entry could simultaneously carry (a) residual Section 301 China-origin duties if any upstream component qualifies, (b) IEEPA-basis "Liberation Day" duties if the relevant EO has not been judicially suspended, (c) the proposed new forced-labor Section 301 duty if the country of final manufacture is targeted, and (d) any applicable antidumping or countervailing duty order rate. The USTR docket will proceed through the standard Section 301 notice-and-comment cycle under 19 U.S.C. § 2412, giving interested parties an opportunity to file written submissions and request a public hearing—a window that trade counsel should begin preparing for immediately given the 30-day comment windows USTR typically sets. (__[White & Case LLP — needs verification](https://whitecase.com)__)

### 3. "Tariff Caps" in Bilateral Deals — USTR Jamieson Greer Confirms Framework

USTR Jamieson Greer confirmed this week that the United States intends to honor tariff-cap commitments embedded in the framework agreements negotiated with the European Union, Japan, and other partners since the April 2025 "Liberation Day" escalation—a statement with significant implications for both the litigation landscape and the refund-recovery analysis. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

The operative question for litigation purposes is whether a bilateral "framework agreement" or "trade arrangement" of this type—short of a formal treaty or congressionally approved free trade agreement—creates enforceable legal rights that importers can invoke to challenge duty collections that exceed the agreed cap. The answer under current doctrine is almost certainly no as a matter of domestic law: *Made in the USA Foundation v. United States*, 242 F.3d 1300 (11th Cir. 2001), and its CIT-level counterparts confirm that executive trade arrangements not submitted for congressional approval under Trade Promotion Authority generally do not create judicially enforceable private rights. However, the existence of a cap commitment does create a political-economy brake on further tariff escalation against covered partners and may influence the scope of any IEEPA-basis modifications USTR recommends going forward. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

For EU-origin importers specifically, the framework arrangement reportedly caps "Liberation Day" IEEPA duties at a rate believed to be in the 10%–15% range (the precise figure has not been publicly confirmed), which—if honored—represents a meaningful reduction from the 20% EU-baseline rate that was operative during portions of 2025. The delta between collected duties (at the higher rate) and the capped rate, for entries imported during the gap period, is potentially the subject of refund claims that are analytically separate from the broader IEEPA constitutional challenge: these claims would rest on the theory that the executive branch's own policy commitment defines the outer limit of a lawful duty rate, and that collections above that level were either ultra vires or subject to administrative correction under 19 U.S.C. § 1520. That theory has not yet been litigated and would face significant headwinds, but sophisticated importers with large EU-origin exposure should be preserving the issue through timely protest filings. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__) (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

### 4. Trump Forced-Labor Tariff Wave — Scope and Implementation Timeline

Separate from the USTR Section 301 proceeding, reporting from The Washington Post and Reuters this week indicates that the Trump administration is preparing—or has already issued—an executive action imposing tariffs on imports from "dozens of nations" on forced-labor grounds, with the legal predicate likely being a combination of IEEPA emergency authority and/or the President's Section 338 power under the Tariff Act of 1930, 19 U.S.C. § 1338, which authorizes the President to impose duties of up to 50% on imports from countries that "discriminate against" U.S. commerce. (__[The Washington Post — needs verification](https://washingtonpost.com)__) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

The operational implication is that importers who believed their supply chains were de-risked following the April–May 2025 "Liberation Day" escalation and the subsequent partial unwinding under bilateral frameworks may now face a second wave of duty liability from a distinct legal predicate. UFLPA and the new Section 301 forced-labor track are administrative proceedings with notice-and-comment requirements; a direct IEEPA or Section 338 executive action would take effect immediately on publication in the Federal Register, subject to subsequent legal challenge.

The forced-labor enforcement wave is also consequential for the Rewind Tariffs refund-recovery vertical specifically: duties imposed on a forced-labor rationale are politically far more defensible in litigation than the "trade-deficit emergency" predicate of the April 2025 IEEPA orders, which multiple CIT judges have found legally insufficient. A forced-labor IEEPA action is more likely to satisfy the "unusual and extraordinary threat" nexus requirement of 50 U.S.C. § 1701(a) because it can be tied to specific human rights findings, prior UFLPA entity-list determinations, and State Department Trafficking in Persons Report designations—creating a multi-agency evidentiary record that courts have historically treated with deference under Chevron's successors and the major-questions doctrine's framing in *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022). Importers seeking refunds should carefully segregate duty payments by legal predicate to avoid conflating refundable IEEPA "trade deficit" duties with potentially non-refundable forced-labor duties. (__[White & Case LLP — needs verification](https://whitecase.com)__)

### 5. Protest & Refund Deadline Monitoring — Critical Filing Windows

With the Federal Circuit stay motion now pending, claimants in the Rewind Tariffs recovery posture face a structurally uncomfortable period: the CIT's refund order may be stayed before it can be implemented, but the 180-day protest clock under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3) keeps running regardless. Any importer that has not filed a timely protest against IEEPA-basis duty collections on liquidated entries is permanently foreclosed from refund recovery on those entries, irrespective of how the Federal Circuit ultimately resolves the merits. CBP's Administrative Ruling process under 19 C.F.R. Part 177 offers a parallel but slower track for prospective guidance; it does not toll protest deadlines.

The CIT has not, as of this morning's scan, issued a universal class-style tolling order or certified a class action under USCIT Rule 23 analogues that would protect importers who have not filed individual protests. The *HMTX Industries LLC v. United States* line of cases (CIT Consol. Court No. 20-00177, Judge Mark Barnett presiding on the Section 301 List 3/4 track) provides the structural template for how the CIT handles consolidated proceedings in mass-protest scenarios, but the IEEPA track is not formally consolidated in the same way. Trade counsel must treat the refund-recovery posture as requiring individual protest preservation for each importer-client. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

## Recommended Actions

Importers, GCs, and trade counsel with IEEPA-basis duty exposure should take four immediate steps: (1) audit all liquidated entries from April 5, 2025 onward bearing Chapter 99 HTSUS special program indicators tied to Executive Orders 14257 and 14259, calculate remaining 180-day protest windows under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3) for each, and file protective protests before expiration—do not wait for Federal Circuit stay briefing to conclude, as a stay of the CIT's refund order does not toll the administrative protest deadline; (2) segregate duty payments by legal predicate (IEEPA "trade-deficit" basis versus UFLPA/Section 301 forced-labor basis versus antidumping/CVD), because refundability analysis differs materially across predicates and conflation of the categories in a protest filing can weaken the legal theory; (3) file written comments in the USTR Section 301 forced-labor docket once the Federal Register notice is published—the comment record will be outcome-determinative on country and product scope, and trade counsel with affected supply-chain clients should submit product-specific cost and sourcing evidence demonstrating that the "burden on commerce" standard is not met for their goods; (4) monitor the Federal Circuit emergency panel docket daily for the stay ruling, which will either preserve or extinguish the CIT's refund-order implementation timeline and will likely contain the appellate court's first substantive signal on the merits of the IEEPA constitutional challenge—that signal will determine whether an accelerated certiorari petition to the Supreme Court is warranted before the October 2026 term.
