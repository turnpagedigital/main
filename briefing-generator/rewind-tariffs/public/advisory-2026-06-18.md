## Analysis & Developments

### DELTA I — The CAPE Refund Pipeline Bifurcates: DOJ's June 2 Federal Circuit Appeal Creates a Two-Tier Importer Universe

The critical development since yesterday's briefing is the operational consequence of the DOJ's June 2 appeal to the Federal Circuit and CBP's June 9 show-cause testimony, taken together: the refund pipeline has split into two distinct tracks depending on whether an importer filed its own CIT suit. This is no longer a theoretical risk.


On June 2, the DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's Refund Order specifically as to its authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries that are both liquidated and past the 80-day reprocessing window.
 The government's theory, previewed in its May 29 court filing, is twofold: 
the DOJ intends to argue that CBP cannot reprocess older entries without a court order specific to each importer, meaning each importer seeking refunds on finally-liquidated entries must file an individual lawsuit in the CIT.



The government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and that, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed. On June 9, the CIT held a lengthy hearing as part of its show-cause order issued May 27, directing the government to explain why the CIT should not lift the stay of its prior refund orders. CBP's Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas testified on behalf of CBP. The hearing ended without the CIT lifting the stay, but produced important disclosures about CAPE's scope and limits.



EAC Thomas reported that Phase 1 of CAPE has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected and that approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the Treasury for refund. To underscore the scale: in all of the last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


Phase 2 is the next near-term milestone. 
At the June 9 show-cause hearing before the CIT, CBP confirmed that Phase 2 of CAPE is scheduled to launch on June 29, 2026, and will cover reconciliation entries.
 
EAC Thomas estimates Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.


Phase 3 carries the sharpest litigation risk. 
Phase 3, targeted for late July 2026, will cover finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period — but critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 
Under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE. The government's appeal has divided the importer universe into two groups: those who filed at the CIT and those who have not. For importers in the latter category, the risk is no longer theoretical.


The statute-of-limitations clock sharpens this. 
The CIT's authority to hear tariff refund cases has a two-year time limit. The deadlines for importers whose entries were among the earliest to be hit with IEEPA duties — starting in early 2025 — will begin to expire in early 2027. Importers who adopt a "wait and see" approach to the appeal risk losing their right to seek a refund entirely if the two-year window closes before they file suit. If the Federal Circuit agrees with the DOJ that the Refund Order was improper, importers who have not filed suit may be left without any way to recover their money.


The stay posture is also in flux. 
The DOJ has indicated it will ask the Federal Circuit to pause the Refund Order as to entries that are both liquidated and past the 80-day window, except for entries made by importers who file their own CIT lawsuits. If that pause is granted, refunds for older entries could be halted entirely for importers who have not filed suit.

### DELTA II — Section 122 (19 U.S.C. § 2132): Federal Circuit Administrative Stay in Place; Hard Expiry July 24, 2026

The Section 122 track moves on a different but convergent timeline. 
On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the CIT — in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* — invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly formed the majority; 
the CIT majority held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974.


The injunction is deliberately narrow. 
The permanent injunction extends only to Burlap and Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington (through the University of Washington's direct import activity). All other importers remain subject to the Section 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.
 
The remaining 23 state plaintiffs were dismissed because their alleged injuries, based on indirect passthrough costs, were deemed too speculative to support standing.


On the appellate track: 
the Trump administration appealed to the Federal Circuit, and on May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit entered an administrative stay, suspending the implementation of the CIT's order while the appeal proceeds.
 
On May 20, 2026, the CIT denied the government's separate motion for a stay of enforcement of its judgment pending appeal; nevertheless, the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remains in place.
 The practical effect: CBP continues collecting the 10% surcharge from all non-plaintiff importers.

The governing hard stop is July 24, 2026. 
The statute permits the President to impose temporary import restrictions under Section 122 — including an import surcharge of up to 15% ad valorem for no more than 150 days. Through Proclamation 11012, the administration imposed a 10% ad valorem surcharge effective February 24, 2026. The surcharge is currently set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 
The Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026 in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely. In particular, the administration recently initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations, and reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.


The dissent matters for the Federal Circuit. 
The appeal may focus on issues raised in Judge Stanceu's dissent. On the merits, Judge Stanceu argued that Section 122 does not limit "balance-of-payments deficits" to the three historical measures identified by the majority — liquidity, official settlements, and basic balance.
 That position gives the government a credible merits argument before the Federal Circuit, and the administrative stay suggests the court is not dismissing it.

### STALE MATTERS CARRIED FORWARD — The Supreme Court Foundation and the IEEPA Refund Architecture

The constitutional and statutory bedrock is not in dispute. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed the Federal Circuit's en banc ruling 6-3, holding that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts in which Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson joined.
 The consolidated Supreme Court docket carried case numbers 24-1287 (*Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*) and 25-250 (*Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*). 
Following that ruling, the Federal Circuit dissolved the stay that had held its mandate in abeyance since August 2025, ordering its mandates to "issue forthwith" in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, Nos. 2025-1812 and -1813 (Fed. Cir. Mar. 2, 2026) (en banc per curiam).


The refund universe is large. 
According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 CAPE Phase 1 launched April 20, 2026, through the ACE portal. 
Phase 1 covers certain unliquidated entries and certain entries that liquidated within the previous 80 days, subject to CBP validation and categorical exclusions — including reconciliation entries, drawback entries, USMCA duty-deferral entries, TIB entries, entries pending AD/CVD liquidation, and entries on which the surety paid the IEEPA duties.


The CBP refund mechanics are now better understood. 
Importers and authorized brokers should anticipate that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60-90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.
 
Framework Agreements with certain countries — the EU, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland/Liechtenstein — required that the Column 1/General Duty be paid along with IEEPA duties such that the combined duty rate equaled 15%. The total 15% could be reported on a single line on the entry summary filing. Importers will not receive a refund of the total 15% because a portion accounts for the Column 1/General Duty rate which remains in effect.


One structural friction point warrants attention: the Anti-Assignment of Claims Act (ACA). 
The statutory prerequisites for an outright assignment — that the claim be "allowed," the amount "decided," and a "warrant for payment" issued — are likely not met for most IEEPA claims at this time. An assignment made before these conditions are satisfied could be void or voidable.
 
Tariff refund claims could be classified as general intangibles (or payment intangibles) under UCC Article 9, perfected by UCC-1 financing statement filing
 — a structuring approach that preserves economic exposure without an outright transfer that might run ACA risk.

Legislative wildcards remain live. 
The Tariff Refund Act of 2026 (S. 3905) would require CBP to refund all duties imposed under IEEPA within 180 days of enactment, with interest, and would prioritize small businesses in the refund process.
 None of the bills before the 119th Congress has passed, but S. 3905 in particular could materially alter the refund timeline if enacted.

## Recommended Actions

Any importer holding finally-liquidated IEEPA entries that has not yet filed at the CIT should treat the DOJ's June 2 Federal Circuit appeal as the trigger to act: 
the government's appeal targets only refunds for entries past the 80-day window, and refunds for newer entries should continue to be processed through CBP's CAPE system even as the appeal proceeds
 — but that distinction means non-plaintiff importers with older entries face genuine forfeiture risk if the Federal Circuit grants a stay. File CIT suit under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) before the two-year clock on early-2025 entries starts closing in early 2027; confirm ACE Portal bank-account linkage for CAPE Phase 1 and calendar the Phase 2 launch for June 29 (reconciliation entries); file 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests on all unliquidated Section 122 entries now — the Federal Circuit's administrative stay in *Oregon v. U.S. / Burlap and Barrel* (Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606) means collections continue for all non-plaintiff importers, but preserved protest rights become material if the CIT is ultimately affirmed; and stress-test any UCC-1 / financing arrangements against ACA constraints before the Phase 2 disbursements begin. Watch the Federal Circuit's Section 122 merits docket and any signal that the court will hear argument before July 24 — if it does not, the statutory expiry effectively moots the stay and collapses the refund window into the protest track.
