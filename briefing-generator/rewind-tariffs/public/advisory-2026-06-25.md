## Analysis & Developments

### I. The Dominant Development Today: CAPE Phase 2 Goes Live in Four Days — and the DOJ Appeal Now Bifurcates the Importer Universe


On June 23, 2026 — two days ago — CBP published CSMS #69035485 confirming that Phase 2 of the IEEPA tariff refund process will be deployed in the ACE Portal on **June 29, 2026**.
 That is the single most consequential near-term date for importers with outstanding IEEPA refund exposure. 
Phase 2 covers entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, and 06) where the corresponding reconciliation entry (Type 09) has not yet been filed, and is similarly limited to unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation — consistent with Phase 1's scope.


The aggregate numbers as of the most recent court filing are large. 
The government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.
 
A June 10, 2026, declaration filed by CBP's Mr. Lord states that 125,576 CAPE declarations have passed validation, covering 16.74 million entries, of which 10.60 million have been processed and IEEPA duties approved for refund — totaling $23.68 billion of the $166 billion in IEEPA duties collected overall.


But the DOJ's June 2 appeal has materially changed the calculus for importers outside the Phase 1/2 window. 
On June 2, 2026, the DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's Refund Order — specifically contesting the CIT's authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries that are both liquidated **and** past the 80-day window in which CBP can voluntarily reprocess them.
 
CBP's May 29 filing asserted — for the first time — that it lacks legal authority to issue IEEPA refunds on finally liquidated entries without an importer-specific court judgment, a position that, if it prevails at the Federal Circuit, would require every affected importer to file individual CIT cases to recover those duties.
 
The government's appeal targets only refunds for entries past the 80-day window; refunds for newer, unliquidated or within-80-day entries should continue to be processed through CAPE even as the appeal proceeds.


The filing deadline pressure is real: 
the CIT's authority to hear tariff refund cases carries a two-year time limit, and the deadlines for importers whose entries were among the earliest to be hit with IEEPA duties (starting in early 2025) will begin to expire in early 2027 — meaning importers who adopt a "wait and see" approach risk losing their right to seek refunds entirely if the two-year window closes before they file suit.


The June 9 show-cause hearing before CIT Judge Richard Eaton — the designated lead judge for IEEPA refunds in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, CIT No. 25-00066 — produced important disclosures without lifting the stay. 
On June 9, 2026, the CIT held a lengthy hearing as part of its order to show cause issued on May 27, 2026, directing the government to explain why the court should not lift the stay of its prior refund orders; the hearing ended without the CIT lifting the stay but produced important disclosures about CAPE's scope and limits.
 
EAC Thomas reported that Phase 1 of CAPE has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected and that approximately $23 billion has been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.


Phase 3 — the category most at risk from the DOJ appeal — has a targeted launch date and a hard litigation gate attached to it. 
Phase 3 is targeted for late July 2026, will cover finally liquidated entries — those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period — and critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed **only** for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT; importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE.


The companion lead case is *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection*, CIT No. 25-00595, which is now on appeal at the Federal Circuit. 
The Euro-Notions case is on appeal at the Federal Circuit, Case No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026.
 That docket runs in parallel with the government's appeal of the broader refund order and will likely be briefed on an overlapping schedule.

### II. Section 122: Statutory Sunset in 29 Days — the Federal Circuit Stay Holds, the CIT Loss Stands

The Section 122 surcharge imposed by Proclamation No. 11012 — signed by President Trump on February 20, 2026, the same day the Supreme Court invalidated IEEPA — expires by statute on **July 24, 2026**. 
That Proclamation invoked Section 122 to impose a temporary 10% ad valorem surcharge on virtually all imports into the United States, from February 24 to July 24, 2026.
 
Section 122 authorizes the President to impose a surcharge of up to 15% for no more than 150 days to address "fundamental international payments problems," including "large and serious United States balance-of-payments deficits."


The CIT has already ruled the proclamation unlawful. 
On May 7, 2026, the Court of International Trade issued Slip Opinion 26-47 in *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* — Court Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606 — holding in a 2-1 decision that Proclamation 11012 is unlawful, with the majority concluding that the President did not identify a balance-of-payments deficit as Congress understood that term in 1974, having instead pointed to the trade deficit, the current account deficit, and the negative U.S. net international investment position.
 
The 2–1 majority comprised Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Kelly, who held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974.


But the relief is narrow and currently stayed. 
The CIT's remedy is party-specific, not universal: the permanent injunction extends only to the three importer plaintiffs who were found to have standing — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. — while all other importers remain subject to Section 122 duties.
 
The DOJ appealed on May 12, 2026, and the Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay of the CIT's ruling while it considers the government's motion for a stay pending appeal; on May 20, 2026, the CIT separately denied the government's motion to stay enforcement of its own judgment, but the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remains in place.


The practical consequence is unambiguous: 
all other importers — those not named as plaintiffs — still owe the 10% Section 122 duty rate through the July 24, 2026 sunset.
 
Only Congress can extend Section 122 tariffs beyond that date; the President cannot unilaterally renew or extend them, and if Congress does not act and the tariffs are not replaced by another authority (such as Section 301), they expire automatically on July 24, 2026.


The Section 301 backstop is in motion. 
The administration has initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices; the administration reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.
 
Historically, Section 301 probes have ranged between 6 and 18 months before the recommendation phase, though it is possible these investigations will proceed faster given the July 24, 2026 expiration deadline.
 Importers should assume the administration will make a maximum effort to have at least some Section 301 tariff determinations in place before the Section 122 clock runs out.

### III. The Supreme Court Decision — Settled Law; Refund Mechanics Are the Battlefield Now

The underlying merits question is closed. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court issued its decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250), holding 6-3 that IEEPA does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs.
 
The majority opinion was written by Chief Justice John Roberts and joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson.
 
Justices Thomas, Kavanaugh, and Alito dissented, concluding that IEEPA authorizes tariffs and that the President acted within delegated authority.



In No. 24-1287, the judgment was vacated and the case remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction; in No. 25-250, the Federal Circuit's judgment was affirmed.
 The structural holding is that tariff-setting is a branch of Congress's Article I taxing power, and IEEPA's authorization to "regulate … importation," 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B), contains no reference to tariffs or duties and cannot be read to delegate that power to the executive. 
The case now returns to the Court of International Trade, which faces the task of overseeing what could be the largest government refund obligation in American history — an estimated $175 billion in IEEPA tariff collections.


On the same day as the ruling, 
President Trump issued an Executive Order rescinding IEEPA tariffs and directing agency heads to "terminate the collection" of those tariffs "as soon as practicable."
 
Following the ruling, the government ceased assessing and collecting importer deposits of IEEPA tariffs as of February 24, 2026.


The major questions doctrine analysis — 
Parts II-A-2 and III of the Roberts opinion, containing the major questions doctrine analysis and the "clear congressional authorization" requirement — were joined only by Justices Gorsuch and Barrett, making those portions a three-Justice plurality, not a majority holding.
 This matters for follow-on Section 301 challenges: a plaintiff seeking to invoke the major questions doctrine against a Section 301 tariff action will need to locate a fifth vote that the February 20 opinion does not provide on that specific point.


The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs imposed by the Trump Administration that did not rely upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.

### IV. CAPE Mechanics — Entry-Type Exclusions and the Finally-Liquidated Fault Line

For counsel advising importers on refund strategy, the technical exclusions in CAPE Phase 1 are as important as the headline numbers. 
Phase 1 covers certain unliquidated entries and certain entries that liquidated within the previous 80 days, but excludes reconciliation entries, drawback entries, USMCA duty-deferral entries, temporary importation under bond (TIB) entries, entries pending AD/CVD liquidation, and entries on which the surety paid the IEEPA duties.
 
Importers and authorized brokers should anticipate that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.


The framework agreement interplay is a subtle but material reduction in refund exposure. 
Framework Agreements with certain countries — the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland/Liechtenstein — required that the Column 1/General Duty be paid along with IEEPA duties such that the combined duty rate equaled 15%; importers in those lanes will not receive a refund of the full 15% because a portion accounts for the Column 1/General Duty rate which remains in effect.



CBP has stated that any importer whose entries have finally liquidated and who has not filed its own claim at the CIT will not be eligible for a refund unless and until there is a court order in favor of such importer — a position CBP says is on appeal and will not be expanded until settled.
 
CBP can now deliver electronic refunds for approximately 82% of all entries that included IEEPA duty payments or deposits.
 The remaining ~18% — concentrated in AD/CVD, drawback, and finally-liquidated categories — represents the contested frontier between the CBP's administrative posture and the CIT's broader refund mandate.

Post Summary Corrections are off the table for this process. 
CBP has indicated that importers may not file PSCs to request IEEPA duty refunds; all unliquidated entries must go through CAPE.

### V. Statutory Mechanics: 19 U.S.C. §§ 1514, 1581(i), and the Protest Deadline as a Preservation Tool

For importers with finally-liquidated entries sitting outside the CAPE window, the protest mechanism under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 is the primary preservation tool. A § 1514 protest must be filed within 180 days of liquidation. For IEEPA entries from the early 2025 period — Canada/Mexico/China fentanyl tariffs first imposed under E.O. 14193 and the Liberation Day global tariffs under E.O. 14257 — the 180-day protest windows and the two-year CIT filing deadline under § 1581(i) are now running simultaneously. The outer boundary on the earliest entries approaches late 2026 to early 2027. Counsel should map each importer's entry population against both clocks before relying solely on the CAPE process. The Tariff Refund Act of 2026, S. 3905, would require CBP to refund all IEEPA duties within 180 days of enactment with interest and with small business prioritization, 
but it has not passed as of this writing.

## Recommended Actions

Clients with IEEPA refund exposure should treat June 29 — CAPE Phase 2 launch — as an operational trigger, not a passive event: confirm now that all reconciliation-flagged entries (Types 01, 02, 06 without a filed Type 09) are identified, that the importer of record number is registered in ACE at least two weeks ahead of the Phase 2 deployment window, and that bank account information for ACH refunds is current. For entries in finally-liquidated status — outside the Phase 1/2 80-day window — the DOJ's June 2 Federal Circuit appeal (*Euro-Notions*, No. 26-1898, and the companion *V.O.S.* refund docket) has made individual CIT filings a near-mandatory protective measure; the government's litigation posture is explicit that non-plaintiff importers will not receive Phase 3 refunds and may receive no refund at all if the Federal Circuit agrees with the DOJ's § 1514/reliquidation authority argument. The two-year § 1581(i) clock is already running on 2025 entries, and the earliest windows close in early 2027 — an importer who waits for appellate resolution before filing risks finding no procedural vehicle left. On Section 122: all non-named importers continue to pay the 10% surcharge under HTS 9903.03.01 through July 24, 2026; firms should be tracking every Section 122 entry now in anticipation of either a Section 301 replacement tariff or a potential second refund track if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT's Slip Op. 26-47 ruling. Do not assume Section 122 payments are unrecoverable — but also do not assume a refund mechanism will exist without individual litigation.
