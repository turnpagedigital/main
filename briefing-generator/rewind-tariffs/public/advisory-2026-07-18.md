## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: Two Converging Deadlines Define the Week — Section 122 Expiration in Six Days, and CBP's July CAPE Phase 3 Window

The operative development entering this weekend is a collision of two hard calendar events: the statutory expiration of the Section 122 global import surcharge at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24, 2026 — six days from today — and CBP's own-stated target for launching CAPE Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries) in "late July." 
CBP's court filings indicate it could begin processing finally liquidated entries — covering approximately $11.4 billion, or 7 percent of total IEEPA tariff revenue — in a forthcoming Phase 3 by late July.
 Simultaneously, the administration's Section 301 Brazil action, announced Wednesday, signals a strategic pivot: the White House is accelerating tariff re-imposition via a statutory authority the Supreme Court never touched. Both developments demand immediate attention from importers and GCs.

---

### I. CAPE Pipeline — Current Throughput, Phase 3 Risk, and the DOJ Appeal Overhang


As of June 29 — more than four months after the Supreme Court's ruling — CBP had authorized $104.29 billion in refunds and paid out $71.06 billion, including interest.
 That gap is not administrative lag alone. 
The fact that only $71.06 billion of the approved $104.29 billion has actually been paid suggests that frictions built into the CAPE process are creating obstacles; CBP claims refunds for 8,384 approved declarations have not been issued because the eligible importers lack proper ACH or banking information.


The distributional skew is more troubling. 
CBP has approved refunds for nearly 60 percent of the government's total obligations, but that $104.3 billion in processed refunds covers just 30 percent of all import entries on which IEEPA tariffs were paid.
 The practical implication: 
many smaller U.S. firms have been unable to obtain refunds through the current CAPE process, and the approved refunds mainly come from large, high-value entries — a strong indication that most refunds have thus far gone to large importers.


The DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal, filed June 2, is the structural threat that explains why Phase 3 remains contested. 
The DOJ formally appealed the order of Senior Judge Richard Eaton of the CIT compelling CBP to refund importers of record $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs declared unlawful by the Supreme Court in February.
 
The DOJ intends to challenge the Refund Order on two fronts: first, that CBP cannot reprocess older, finally liquidated entries without a court order specific to each importer; and second, that the Refund Order is an overly broad "universal injunction" that exceeds what the CIT is permitted to order.
 
That appeal challenges the injunction's applicability to entries for which liquidation has become final — i.e., more than 180 days have passed since CBP rendered a final determination — and finally liquidated entries are not eligible for Phase 1 or 2 of CAPE processing.


The DOJ's posture is in direct tension with its own prior representations. 
In December 2025, the DOJ represented in a briefing to the CIT that it "will not object to the [CIT] ordering reliquidation of plaintiffs' entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful," in *AGS Co. Auto. Solutions v. CBP* (Slip Op. 25-154).
 
The DOJ now asserts that any such order must be made on an importer-by-importer basis rather than universally, a position that appears inconsistent with the government's January 2026 concession that it would refund IEEPA tariffs to "all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs."



On June 4, plaintiffs in *V.O.S. Solutions, Inc. v. Trump* filed a motion for class certification covering all importers that paid IEEPA tariffs whose entries are not eligible for processing in CAPE Phase 1, requesting an injunction directing the government to expand CAPE to process all refund claims with interest on the basis that the class is entitled to indivisible injunctive relief.
 The government is contesting that class approach at the CIT as well, per Cato Institute analysis of July 1 CBP filings. 
Importers whose refunds could be affected by the appeal are making initial moves toward a class-action lawsuit that would satisfy the individual-suit requirement — yet the government is also challenging this approach at the CIT.


Additionally, 
CBP's refund data suggest the agency still owes another $25 billion in refunds for entries not covered in Phases 1 through 3, including entries subject to antidumping and countervailing duties, entries for which active drawback claims or administrative protests exist, and entries not filed within ACE.
 
The agency's latest filing does not provide a timeline for processing these entries through CAPE or otherwise issuing refunds.

### II. Section 122 — Six Days to Statutory Expiration, Federal Circuit Stay Intact

The 10 percent global import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2132, expires by statute at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24, 2026 — the hard terminus of the 150-day window under Proclamation No. 11012, absent a congressional extension that has not been enacted. 
Pursuant to authority claimed under Section 122, the President announced a 10 percent ad valorem duty on all articles imported into the United States, effective February 24, 2026, and set to remain in effect until 12:01 a.m. EST on July 24, 2026, unless "suspended, modified, or terminated on an earlier date" or "extended by an Act of Congress."


The Federal Circuit stay entered June 11 remains operative. 
On June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump* (Nos. 2026-1804, 2026-1805), consolidated appeals from CIT decisions enjoining enforcement of tariffs imposed under Proclamation No. 11012 and Section 122 — meaning the government may continue to collect Section 122 duties as to the parties covered by the underlying injunctions, including the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc., pending further order of the Federal Circuit.


The Federal Circuit's stay reasoning is substantively important for the merits posture. 
The CAFC concluded that the government had made a sufficient showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits, explaining that the CIT's interpretation — that "balance-of-payments deficit" is limited to deficits measured by liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance — "may be incorrect," and that the legislative history cited by the government "strongly calls into question" whether those were the only methods Congress intended.



The government's initial merits brief is due to the CAFC in July, making it unlikely this litigation is resolved prior to the July 24 expiration; CBP will continue collecting Section 122 duties on all importers until that date.
 The practical consequence: even importers who prevailed in the CIT — Burlap and Barrel, Basic Fun, and the State of Washington — are paying the surcharge through July 24 under the stay.


The Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026, in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely.
 The Section 301 Brazil action announced this week makes that point concrete.

### III. Section 301 Brazil — 25% Tariff Effective July 22, the First Salvo of Post-IEEPA Reconstruction


The United States will impose a 25% tariff on most imports from Brazil starting July 22 — the first action under the Trump administration's new tariff strategy that could eventually affect dozens of countries.
 USTR Jamieson Greer announced the measures Wednesday evening following a yearlong Section 301 investigation into Brazilian trade practices. 
The U.S. launched the initial Section 301 investigation into Brazil last July under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, designed to address foreign practices affecting U.S. commerce and competitiveness.



The tariffs would apply to thousands of Brazilian imports, including sugar, agricultural machinery, apparel, electrical machinery, paper, and steel.
 
The U.S. said it would exempt all products proposed for exemption in the June notice — except high-purity dissolving pulp and non-pharmaceutical applications of certain products — with exemptions covering beef, coffee, rare earths, energy products, aircraft, and aircraft parts; organic honey, pig iron, unflavored instant coffee, and some other products were added to the exemption list on Wednesday.


A second wave is imminent. 
Brazil has also been included in a separate Section 301 investigation by USTR, due to conclude on July 24, into connections to forced labor in supply chains across dozens of countries; that probe is expected to result in an additional 12.5% tariff, bringing the total burden for Brazilian products to 37.5%.



Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva said there was "no justification" for the tariffs and posted on X that Brazil would take countermeasures under its Reciprocity Law while also seeking a resolution through the WTO.
 
The newly announced tariffs are the first imposed under Section 301 since the Supreme Court struck down Trump's sweeping global tariffs; the high court ruled that the president lacked the authority to impose widespread tariffs using IEEPA.


The Brazil action is strategically significant beyond the bilateral. Section 301 was never challenged by the IEEPA ruling and carries a much stronger legal foundation — it survived the Uruguay Round Agreements Act era of litigation and has been upheld through the China Section 301 cycle. 
The move comes amid comparable trade negotiations with other governments around the world, including the European Union, India, Japan, and South Korea.
 The administration is building a Section 301 tariff architecture to replace the collapsed IEEPA framework — country by country, investigation by investigation.

### IV. Finally Liquidated Entries — Phase 3 Mechanics and the Protest Preservation Imperative

The finally liquidated category — entries for which CBP rendered a final duty determination more than 180 days before the refund process began — is the highest-stakes unresolved segment of the refund pipeline. 
The Refund Order directed CBP to refund IEEPA duties across three categories: entries not yet finalized; entries liquidated but still within the 80-day window for voluntary reprocessing; and entries both liquidated and past the 80-day window.
 It is that third category where the DOJ appeal bites hardest.


CBP's CAPE refund program remains the primary vehicle for importer refunds; at the June 9, 2026, hearing before Judge Richard Eaton, CBP's Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas confirmed the Phase 1 timeline: Phase 1 covers entries not yet finally liquidated when refund processing began, as well as entries liquidated within the preceding 80 days, incorporating a 10-day buffer.



CAPE Phase 1 is active; Phase 2 (reconciliation and antidumping/countervailing duty entries) was set to launch June 29, 2026; and Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries — available only to importers who have filed at the CIT) is on track for late July 2026.
 That last qualifier is critical: 
the government is arguing before the CAFC that CBP cannot issue refunds for finally liquidated entries without the importer of record filing suit at the CIT first.


For importers who have not filed, the two-year statute of limitations under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) is running. 
Non-litigant importers face the greatest risk — and the two-year deadline to file suit is running; waiting to see how the appeal plays out could mean losing the right to seek a refund altogether.
 Under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, the standard protest window is 180 days from liquidation; that mechanism remains available as a parallel preservation track for entries that have not yet exhausted protest rights.


The government has represented that more than $95 billion has been queued for refund through CAPE and, by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.
 The spread between $95 billion queued and $71 billion actually paid as of June 29 confirms that transmission lags at Treasury — not CAPE validation — is now the primary operational bottleneck for approved claims.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and counsel should treat the next six days as a hard decision window across three parallel tracks. **On CAPE and Phase 3:** any importer whose entries fall into the finally liquidated category — i.e., liquidated more than 180 days before the CAPE reference date — must assess immediately whether a CIT complaint is on file; if not, the DOJ's appeal posture means CAPE Phase 3 will not process those entries without individual court orders, and the two-year § 1581(i) window is actively running. 
For importers who have not filed suit, the DOJ's position is that they must file a lawsuit at the CIT and obtain their own court order to secure refunds for entries past the 80-day window.
 **On ACH readiness:** any importer with a validated CAPE declaration but no ACH banking information on file with CBP is among the 8,384 declarations currently blocked — that is a solvable operational problem that should be resolved this week before Phase 3 opens. **On Section 301 Brazil:** importers of Brazilian sugar, apparel, paper, steel, and agricultural machinery should model landed-cost impact from July 22 forward and check whether their specific HTS classifications fall within the exemption schedule published by USTR; the forced-labor investigation closing July 24 may stack an additional 12.5% on the same goods the same week the Section 122 surcharge expires, producing net tariff levels that are higher than the pre-*Learning Resources* baseline for Brazilian supply chains. **On Section 122 protest preservation:** importers in the Burlap/Oregon plaintiff universe who have not separately filed a CIT complaint should file 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests covering Section 122 entries now, before the July 24 expiration; if the Federal Circuit ultimately upholds the CIT on the merits, protest preservation will determine eligibility for any Section 122 refund pipeline that CBP may open through CAPE or a successor mechanism.
