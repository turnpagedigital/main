### DELTA: Judge Eaton's June 9 Show-Cause Hearing — $11.4 Billion Frozen in Phase 3 Dispute

The dominant development this week is the June 9, 2026, show-cause hearing before Judge Richard Eaton in his packed Manhattan courtroom in *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection*, CIT No. 25-00595. Eaton urged the Trump administration to back off its appeal of his order to fully refund the IEEPA tariffs overturned by the Supreme Court, saying the appeal was unnecessary and would only create more delays for importers to get their money back. At the heart of the proceeding: a customs official confirmed for the first time that the government believes about $11.4 billion is at issue in its appeal, while a Justice Department lawyer later put the figure at about $10 billion — suggesting the dispute is narrow. "Even I think that's real money," Judge Eaton shot back as many laughed (__[Transportation Topics](https://www.ttnews.com/articles/tariff-refund-appeal-10b)__).

The hearing came roughly a week after the Department of Justice formally appealed Eaton's order to U.S. Customs and Border Protection to refund the $166 billion in payments the government owes importers of record who paid the now-defunct IEEPA tariffs; the DOJ filed its notice of appeal at the Federal Circuit on June 2, 2026 (__[Thompson Hine SmarTrade](https://www.thompsonhinesmartrade.com/2026/06/trump-administration-appeals-cits-ieepa-tariff-refund-order/)__). Eaton told government lawyers: "My appeal to you is to withdraw your appeal. You win nothing if the Court of Appeals says my order is unlawful." CBP has already accepted nearly $85–$100 billion in potential and certified refunds for processing since it launched the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) in April, according to CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Susan Thomas.

The phased refund structure now in sharp relief: Phase 1 is ending, $29 billion is eligible for repayment in Phase 2, and $10 billion to $11.4 billion in tariff refunds have not yet been paid out specifically due to the Trump administration's appeal — which is focused on Phase 3. The second phase of the process is expected to begin in the third or fourth quarter of 2026, though the exact timing is yet to be confirmed.

Justice Department attorney Claudia Burke argued the appeal is necessary in part because there could "very soon" be a repeat of the refund process if the Supreme Court also overturns the 10% global tariffs Trump issued after his IEEPA levies were blocked. That forward-looking rationale lays bare the administration's true strategic concern: the Section 122 tariffs facing their own Federal Circuit challenge.

Despite his pointed rhetoric, Judge Eaton urged Trump administration officials to speed up the refunds but did not issue a strict order demanding they do so. He did, however, signal a potential alternative vehicle: taking the basis for the government's appeal head on, the victorious plaintiffs are now asking Eaton to certify a class of importers that paid the IEEPA tariffs — a move that would give him authority to order everyone in that class to receive refunds without separate court orders. The judge indicated he does not believe the Supreme Court case referenced by the government contradicts his March ruling, though he is mulling scheduling a hearing about whether the importers who paid the duties at issue could comprise a certified legal class (__[National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/what-every-multinational-should-know-about-governments-appeal-judge-eatons)__).

CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Susan Thomas provided an update on the progress of the refund process: about $85–$90 billion in refunds have been processed and approved through CAPE. She testified that about $23 billion in refunds have been sent to Treasury to be paid out, with another $480 million coming this week (__[BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/tax/update-on-cbp-ieepa-refund-progress-and-new-orders-from-the-us-court-of-international-trade)__).

### DOJ's Appeal Framework and the Judicial-Estoppel Problem

On May 29, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice filed a notice of appeal and motion to amend the court's order in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, before the U.S. Court of International Trade, challenging Judge Richard K. Eaton's universal order directing CBP to refund approximately $166 billion in duties collected under IEEPA; the formal Federal Circuit appeal followed on June 2, 2026.

Specifically, DOJ takes issue with the CIT's "universal" injunction directing IEEPA tariff refunds for non-litigants for "finally liquidated" entries that are past 90 days post-liquidation. The government's three-category framework defines the contested terrain: for tariffs paid on unliquidated or nonfinal entries, CBP is "already in the process of refunding approximately $85 billion of these tariffs — over half the total amount of IEEPA tariffs paid." For entries that are finally liquidated, the administration maintains that "CBP has no authority to reliquidate or refund money without a court order" and insists on importer-specific court orders requiring reliquidation — a position that would force individualized litigation for every affected importer (__[Cherry Bekaert](https://www.cbh.com/insights/alerts/ieepa-tariff-update-refunds-on-final-entries-in-dispute/)__).

The government's appeal strategy faces a structural legal obstacle. In December 2025, in *AGS Company Automotive Solutions v. CBP* (Slip Op. 25-154), the DOJ affirmatively represented to the CIT that it "will not object to the [c]ourt ordering reliquidation of plaintiff's entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful." The CIT found this constituted an "unequivocal position" and held the government judicially estopped from reversing course. On January 8, 2026, the government further stipulated in the CIT that it would refund IEEPA tariffs for "all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs" following a "final and unappealable decision" — a commitment covering IEEPA tariffs imposed on all countries, including Brazil and India. The government used its December 2025 and January 2026 representations in opposing importers' motions for preliminary injunctions — arguing that injunctions were unnecessary because refunds would be available. If the doctrine applies, the government would face a high bar in arguing that the CIT lacks authority to order the very relief it previously told the court was available.

On the appellate merits, the CIT asserted that the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, which held that "universal injunctions are impermissible," was inapplicable to the order because the CIT has "exclusive subject matter jurisdiction to hear claims like those presented in this case." Whether this reasoning survives appellate review at the Federal Circuit remains to be seen — the Federal Circuit previously vacated the CIT's universal injunction in *V.O.S. Selections* and remanded for reconsideration in light of *CASA*. However, that remand was directed at the original merits-phase injunction, not the post-judgment refund order now at issue.

### CAPE System: Phase 1 Status and the Phase 2/3 Gap

As of May 11, 2026, approximately 126,237 CAPE declarations had been submitted, with roughly $35.46 billion in anticipated refunds and interest for approximately 8.3 million accepted entries. 86,874 of those declarations passed file validations; 15,123,221 individual entries with IEEPA duties passed entry-specific validations and were accepted for removal of IEEPA duties; 8,338,081 of those accepted entries have been liquidated and/or reliquidated without IEEPA duties. By the June 9 hearing, CAPE declarations passing file validations covered nearly 16 million entries, of which roughly 8.5 million had been liquidated or reliquidated without IEEPA duties (__[BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/tax/update-on-cbp-ieepa-refund-progress-and-new-orders-from-the-us-court-of-international-trade)__).

As of April 9, 2026, a combined total of 56,497 importers and notify parties had signed up to receive electronic refunds; CBP can now deliver electronic refunds for about 82% of all entries that included IEEPA duty payments or deposits. Two categories remain outside clean Phase 1 processing: CBP is still determining how to approach pre-liquidation refunds for suspended entries subject to AD/CVD orders, which account for about 166,000 entries. Unlike standard entries, whose refund process is largely automated and occurs at liquidation, pre-liquidation refunds require manual processing that would likely place enormous strain on agency operations.

On May 26, 2026, CBP provided an update to the CIT on the progress of IEEPA duty refunds; following a closed conference the next day, Judge Eaton issued two orders in the two separate cases governing the refund process and directed the CBP Commissioner to appear in court on June 9 to explain the agency's slow compliance in developing Phases 2 and beyond (__[Stinson LLP](https://www.stinson.com/newsroom-publications-supreme-court-invalidates-ieepa-tariffs-recent-developments-accelerate-refund-process)__). The Court has expressed particular concern about millions of informal entries where liquidation occurred simultaneously or nearly simultaneously with the entry, making the liquidation final well before CAPE Phase 1 was implemented.

Separately, the statutory question of the 90-day reliquidation limit under 19 U.S.C. § 1501 continues to shadow Phase 3 claims. The question on the docket refers to tariff payments the government says aren't yet final and therefore cannot be reclaimed without a court order. Almost 4,000 importers have filed lawsuits at the CIT requesting refunds — the litigation class continues to grow as counsel advise entry of protective filings, and the pending Rule 23 class-certification motion would, if granted, fold non-litigant importers into the refund order without separate suits.

### Section 122 Tariffs: CIT Invalidates, Federal Circuit Issues Temporary Stay — Clock Runs to July 24

The secondary tariff regime implanted on the day of the *Learning Resources* ruling is now under its own appellate cloud. On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the U.S. Court of International Trade, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — dealing another blow to the administration's trade agenda after the previous statutory basis for its broad "reciprocal" tariffs under IEEPA was struck down by the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump.*

The CIT's reasoning turned on statutory originalism. Section 122 requires the president to identify "large and serious" U.S. balance-of-payments deficits as that term was understood and measured when Congress enacted the statute in 1974; legislative history reflected three established balance-of-payment measures recognized at the time: the basic balance, liquidity, and official settlements. The court found that the two measures relied upon by the government — the current account balance and goods trade deficits — did not constitute balance-of-payments deficits as Congress understood the term in 1974. Because Proclamation 11012 did not identify a balance-of-payments deficit measured consistently with the statute's original meaning, the CIT held the action was ultra vires and invalidated the tariffs as to the plaintiffs.

The case captions and docket identifiers: *The State of Oregon v. United States & Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 (Ct. Int'l Trade May 7, 2026). A two-judge majority (Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly) granted summary judgment for the importer plaintiffs.

Relief is at present party-specific and stayed on appeal. The 2-1 decision results in a permanent injunction prohibiting the collection of Section 122 duties — but only with respect to the prevailing plaintiffs: Burlap & Barrel, Inc., Basic Fun, Inc., and the State of Washington. The government appealed the ruling to the Federal Circuit on May 8, 2026, with U.S. Trade Representative Greer expressing confidence that the government would prevail. Key issues to watch include whether the ruling will be stayed pending appeal at the Federal Circuit past the temporary stay the appeals court granted on May 12.

The statutory clock is absolute: because of the time limits imposed by Section 122, the 10% tariffs imposed under Proclamation 11012 are set to expire on July 24, 2026 — exactly 150 days from their effective date. Congress can extend the duration beyond 150 days, but the President cannot do so unilaterally. With six weeks to that sunset, the appellate calculus narrows: a Federal Circuit merits ruling is unlikely before the tariffs expire by operation of law, which makes the Section 301 successor track the administration's real instrument.

The administration's medium-term fallback is already in motion. On March 11, 2026, USTR initiated Section 301 investigations targeting both excess manufacturing capacity (16 economies) and forced labor enforcement (60+ economies). Public comment periods closed on April 15. Ambassador Greer has signaled intent to conclude these investigations on an "accelerated timeline" before Section 122 expires. Unlike Section 122, Section 301 carries no cap on tariff rates and no statutory time limit; legal analysts expect the resulting tariffs to be similar in scope to the invalidated IEEPA tariffs.

### Foundational Record: Supreme Court IEEPA Ruling and the $166 Billion Refund Obligation

For practitioners arriving new to the matter, the chain of authority: in its 6-3 ruling on February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court decided that IEEPA does not grant the President the power to unilaterally impose tariffs of indefinite scope. Chief Justice Roberts authored the majority opinion in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 607 U.S. ___ (2026); *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, 607 U.S. ___ (2026), with Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson joining. Justices Thomas, Alito, and Kavanaugh dissented and would have held that IEEPA authorizes tariffs.

On March 4, 2026, the CIT ordered that "all importers of record" that paid IEEPA-based duties are "entitled to the benefit of" the *Learning Resources* decision and directed CBP to determine final tariff assessments "without regard to" the IEEPA duties. On March 27, Judge Eaton issued an amended order expanding the directive to cover all entries — including entries for which liquidation had already become final — and ordering CBP to liquidate or reliquidate all such entries without regard to IEEPA duties. Following voluntary dismissal of *Atmus Filtration*, Judge Eaton immediately designated *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection* as the new lead case and, on April 7, issued an order mirroring the March 27 *Atmus* order.

The Penn Wharton Budget Model projected an outer bound: reversing the IEEPA tariffs will generate up to $175 billion in refunds. Separately, a secondary market estimated at up to $100 billion has emerged around tariff refund claims as of April 2026.

Importers holding claims through Section 122 paid under HTSUS heading 9903.03.01 should note the interaction: whether Section 122 duties may be offset against IEEPA refunds is an open area to monitor. The Tariff Refund Act of 2026 (S. 3905) provides a legislative backstop: it would require CBP to refund all duties imposed under IEEPA within 180 days of enactment, with interest, and would prioritize small businesses in the refund process.

## Recommended Actions

Importers of record and their counsel face three simultaneous decision points on today's facts. **First**, Phase 1 CAPE filing is urgent for all unliquidated or recently liquidated entries — as entries liquidate, they slip from the administration's first category to the third, and once an entry moves to "finally liquidated" it becomes the contested Phase 3 category over which DOJ has filed its June 2 Federal Circuit appeal; importers that have not filed suit in the CIT face substantial risk that they will be excluded from any refund order if the appeal narrows the universal injunction, and should weigh whether the pending Rule 23 class-certification motion will cover them or whether an individual protective suit remains necessary. **Second**, importers with Phase 3 finally-liquidated exposure should immediately evaluate protective CIT suit filings under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i); for litigant and non-litigant importers alike, advisors continue to recommend filing Customs protests for entries at the 160–170 day mark post-liquidation to preserve all available procedural avenues in the event the Federal Circuit narrows the universal injunction. **Third**, on Section 122 Proclamation 11012 exposure (HTSUS 9903.03.01, effective February 24 – July 24, 2026): importers and companies that paid, absorbed, or passed through the surcharge should promptly evaluate their entries, contracts, and any steps needed to preserve potential refund, protest, litigation, or contract options before the surcharge's automatic July 24 expiration — and model successor-tariff scenarios under Section 301 and Section 232 given the USTR's stated intent to finalize Section 301 investigations ahead of that date.
