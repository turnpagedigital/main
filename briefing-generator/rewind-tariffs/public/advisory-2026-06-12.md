# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Friday, June 12, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's Delta:** The week ending June 12 crystallized three simultaneous pressure points in the post-*Learning Resources* trade litigation landscape. First, at a June 9 hearing before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton in the U.S. Court of International Trade (CIT), a CBP customs official confirmed for the first time that the government believes approximately $11.4 billion is at issue in the DOJ's appeal of Judge Eaton's universal IEEPA refund order — while DOJ counsel argued the figure is closer to $10 billion, a gap Judge Eaton found immaterial. Second, CBP confirmed at that same June 9 hearing that CAPE Phase 3 — covering finally liquidated entries — will be ready by the end of July, with CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Susan Thomas delivering the update before Judge Eaton. Third, the Section 122 parallel track continues to percolate at the Federal Circuit with the administrative stay holding while briefing proceeds. The two litigation fronts — IEEPA Phase 3 refund scope and Section 122 validity — are now on a collision course with the July 24 statutory expiration deadline and the administration's concurrent Section 301 investigation timetable.

---

### I. The June 9 CIT Hearing: Judge Eaton, CBP Commissioner Scott, and the $10–$11.4 Billion Phase 3 Dispute

The June 9 hearing in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, was the sharpest judicial confrontation yet in the IEEPA refund saga. Judge Richard Eaton urged Trump administration lawyers to withdraw the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal of his order requiring $166 billion in tariff refunds, telling government lawyers that continuing to appeal was unnecessary and would only create more delays for importers to get their money back. His admonition — "My appeal to you is to withdraw your appeal," with the pointed follow-up that "you win nothing if the Court of Appeals says my order is unlawful" — came after CBP confirmed that nearly $100 billion in potential and certified refunds have been accepted for processing through CAPE since the portal launched April 20, 2026.

The core dispute at the hearing turned on CBP's failure to extend liquidation deadlines. Judge Eaton raised the issue of liquidation deadlines, and Thomas confirmed that CBP had not extended those deadlines for entries after the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling; Eaton stated that this inaction has resulted in more entries becoming finally liquidated, and he asked Thomas to consider extending those deadlines going forward. The practical consequence is that the pool of "finally liquidated" Phase 3 entries — the category the DOJ contests — continues to grow with each passing week.

Thomas confirmed that finally liquidated entries account for roughly $11.4 billion, or 6.9%, of all IEEPA tariff payments made, and that once CAPE Phase 2 and Phase 3 capabilities are operational, approximately 95% of all entries that faced IEEPA tariffs will be eligible for refunds. Phase 2, covering reconciliation entries, is targeted for a June 29, 2026 launch; Phase 2 will address entries flagged for reconciliation — those subject to post-filing adjustments — and CBP first announced the June 29 launch date in its response to Judge Eaton's May 27 show-cause order.

As of the June 9 hearing, approximately $23 billion in refunds have been sent to Treasury to be paid out, with another $480 million coming that week. As of June 5, CBP has accepted and begun processing $94.94 billion in refunds for importers, with nearly $24 billion sent to Treasury for payment, according to a court filing.

### II. The DOJ Federal Circuit Appeal: CASA, Universal Injunctions, and the Phase 3 Standoff

The DOJ's June 2, 2026 Federal Circuit appeal is the central legal threat to non-plaintiff importers with finally liquidated entries. On June 2, 2026, the government appealed the CIT's universal injunction to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, arguing that the injunction exceeds the CIT's jurisdiction and equitable authority under the Supreme Court's ruling in *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, because the injunction applies to non-litigants, in part.

The government's three-category framework, first articulated in its May 29 filing, divides the refund universe as follows: CBP represents it is already in the process of refunding approximately $85 billion in Category 1 (unliquidated and nonfinal entries) — over half the total IEEPA tariffs paid. For Category 2 (finally liquidated entries where the importer has filed suit), the Administration maintains that once an entry is finally liquidated, CBP has no authority to reliquidate or refund money without a court order, and insists that refunds must be processed through importer-specific court orders requiring reliquidation. Category 3 — finally liquidated entries where the importer has not filed suit — is the contested frontier. The Administration's stance threatens to upend the refund process for nearly half of the roughly $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, affecting importers that have not filed suit in the CIT and preserved their refund claims for finally liquidated entries.

The government's primary appellate argument draws on *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, 606 U.S. 831 (2025), which held that universal injunctions are impermissible. The CIT's March refund order concluded that the prohibition on universal injunctions in *CASA* does not apply to the CIT, reasoning that the CIT's statutory authority differs from that of federal district courts created under the Judiciary Act of 1789. But the Federal Circuit has not yet ruled on this specific issue, and its earlier decision in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, 149 F.4th 1312 (Fed. Cir. 2025) — which held the IEEPA tariffs unlawful and was later affirmed by the Supreme Court — vacated the CIT's earlier nationwide injunction against the tariffs, instructing the CIT on remand to reevaluate the propriety of granting such broad relief in light of *CASA*.

The judicial estoppel counterargument may be the government's most significant vulnerability. In *AGS Company Automotive Solutions v. CBP*, Slip Op. 25-154, the DOJ affirmatively represented to the CIT that it "will not object to the court ordering reliquidation of plaintiff's entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful," a position the CIT found constituted an "unequivocal position" and held the government judicially estopped from reversing course; on January 8, 2026, the government further stipulated that it would refund IEEPA tariffs for "all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs" following a "final and unappealable decision."

DOJ's own rationale for the appeal reveals a secondary strategic concern: DOJ attorney Claudia Burke argued the appeal is necessary in part because there could "very soon" be a repeat of the refund process if the Supreme Court also overturns the 10% global tariffs Trump issued after his IEEPA levies were blocked. In other words, DOJ is fighting the remedial scope of Judge Eaton's IEEPA order now, in part to cabin any Section 122 refund mechanism that might follow.

The government's response on the pending motions is due June 25, 2026. On June 4, Terry Precision Cycling — a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) case — moved in *V.O.S. Selections* to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows for class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs; the government's response is due June 25, 2026. Counsel for V.O.S. Selections pointed to Terry Precision Cycling's pending class certification motion at the June 9 hearing, arguing that if the government is committed to refunds but worried about *CASA*, it should not oppose that motion — though Judge Eaton expressed reservations about that path.

### III. Section 122 Track: Federal Circuit Administrative Stay Holds; July 24 Expiration Looms

The Section 122 litigation — *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606 — remains the second battleground. On May 7, 2026, the CIT issued a divided 2-1 decision holding that President Trump's 10% global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 was unlawful, finding that the presidential action did not meet the statutory criteria required to invoke Section 122, and granting summary judgment and a permanent injunction to three importer plaintiffs.

The CIT majority, comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly, held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974 when Section 122 was enacted and, instead, improperly relied on the existence of persistent trade deficits or current-account deficits. The permanent injunction extends only to the three importer plaintiffs with standing: the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to the 10% Section 122 duties. The remaining 23 state plaintiffs were dismissed because their alleged injuries, based on indirect passthrough costs, were deemed too speculative to support standing.

The government appealed the day after the CIT's opinion, on May 8, 2026, and on May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted an immediate administrative stay of the CIT's permanent injunction while the appellate court considers a more prolonged stay during the duration of the appeal. On May 20, 2026, the CIT denied the government's separate motion for a stay of enforcement pending appeal; nevertheless, the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remains in place. The Federal Circuit consolidated the two related Section 122 appeals — involving Oregon, Washington, Burlap & Barrel, and Basic Fun — and the appeals will proceed under a single briefing schedule and consolidated caption.

The Section 122 statutory clock adds structural urgency: the Section 122 tariffs are scheduled to expire on July 24, 2026 in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely. In particular, the administration recently initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices, and reportedly intends for those investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.

A nascent downstream liability risk is emerging on the Section 122 track. The fact that the CIT did not grant a universal injunction as part of its Section 122 opinion may indicate that, even if the CIT's decision is upheld on appeal, further litigation could be required for additional importers to obtain tariff refunds; importers considering such litigation must weigh the potential for obtaining an injunction against the possibility that follow-on litigation will be stayed pending the CAFC appeal and that the Section 122 tariffs expire July 24, 2026.

### IV. Consumer Class Action Exposure: The Lululemon Downstream Theory

A materially different threat has opened for importers already pursuing CAPE refunds. Only importers of record — not consumers — have standing to seek refunds in the CIT, prompting consumers to file claims in state and federal courts under equitable theories; one example is a proposed class action filed March 27, 2026 against Lululemon USA Inc. in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, Case No. 2:26-cv-11029, alleging the company passed approximately $240 million in IEEPA tariff costs on to consumers through higher prices while simultaneously seeking a full refund of those costs from the government. The complaint asserts claims for unjust enrichment and money had and received, and seeks certification of a nationwide class covering the period from February 1, 2025, through February 24, 2026. The "double recovery" theory — an importer receiving a CAPE refund while having passed the duty cost to consumers at retail — is the central vulnerability. Any importer currently processing CAPE claims while having raised prices during the IEEPA tariff period should treat this litigation as a live exposure requiring coordination between trade counsel, outside litigation counsel, and internal pricing documentation custodians.

### V. Section 301 as the Successor Tariff Architecture; WTO Civil Aviation Clock

With IEEPA gone and Section 122 expiring July 24, the administration's long-term tariff architecture pivots to Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Although IEEPA tariff authority has been invalidated, alternative statutory trade tools remain available to the Trump administration. The administration's Section 301 investigation timetable — designed to produce results before the July 24 Section 122 expiration — means importers currently using CAPE or pursuing CIT suits should simultaneously be modeling tariff exposure under Section 301 scenarios, which carry their own protest timelines, exemption processes, and WTO retaliation dynamics.

On a parallel multilateral track, the five-year suspension of the long-running WTO Large Civil Aircraft tariff dispute among the U.S., EU, and U.K. is set to expire in July 2026, adding another potential tariff flashpoint in the aerospace sector precisely as Section 122 sunsets. Civil aviation importers that benefited from Section 122 carve-outs under Proclamation 11012 should be monitoring both the WTO suspension expiration and any Section 301 investigation scope for aircraft-adjacent goods.

## Recommended Actions

Importers with any exposure to IEEPA Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries), Section 122 duties, or both should take the following steps immediately: (1) **CAPE Phase 3 preparation** — inventory all entries liquidated more than 80 days before the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 607 U.S. __ (2026); confirm ACH account enrollment in ACE (enrollment is not automatic), as CBP has issued no paper refund checks since February 6, 2026, and failure to enroll forfeits payment regardless of entry eligibility; (2) **CIT suit evaluation** — given the DOJ's appeal in *V.O.S. Selections* (Court No. 25-00066), importers with finally liquidated entries who have not yet filed a Section 1581(i) CIT action face the real risk of exclusion from any refund order if the Federal Circuit accepts the government's CASA-based jurisdictional argument; the government's response brief is due June 25, 2026, and briefing will clarify the Federal Circuit's posture on the universal injunction question within weeks; (3) **Section 122 protest preservation** — all importers subject to the 10% Section 122 duty should be filing protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 now, as the CIT's party-limited injunction in *Oregon v. United States / Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, provides no direct relief to non-plaintiffs, and the July 24, 2026 statutory expiration does not eliminate the need to preserve refund rights on entries already assessed; (4) **Consumer class action conflict check** — any importer seeking CAPE refunds that raised retail prices during the IEEPA tariff period (February 4, 2025 – February 24, 2026) should retain litigation counsel immediately and preserve all internal pricing documentation given the Lululemon "double recovery" theory now before the Eastern District of Michigan (Case No. 2:26-cv-11029); and (5) **Section 301 contingency modeling** — with the administration targeting Section 301 investigation completion before July 24, importers should model product-specific tariff exposure under Section 301 as the successor tariff regime, paying particular attention to HTS classifications that were subject to IEEPA surcharges and are now facing potential Section 301 investigations under the "unjustifiable trade practices" standard.
