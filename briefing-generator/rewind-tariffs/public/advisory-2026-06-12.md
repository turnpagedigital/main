## Analysis & Developments

### DELTA — Federal Circuit Extends Section 122 Stay; $11.4B Phase 3 Dispute Sharpens at June 9 CIT Hearing

Two intersecting developments in the past 72 hours define this morning's posture. First, on June 11, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit extended its administrative stay blocking the U.S. Court of International Trade's May 7 permanent injunction against the Section 122 global tariff — keeping the 10% duty in place for all three prevailing plaintiffs (State of Washington / University of Washington; Burlap and Barrel, Inc.; and Basic Fun, Inc.) while the government's merits appeal plays out (__[Reuters/U.S. News, June 11, 2026](https://www.usnews.com/news/top-news/articles/2026-06-11/us-appeals-court-extends-block-on-ruling-against-trumps-10-global-tariff)__). Second, at a June 9 hearing in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, U.S. CIT Manhattan, CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Susan Thomas testified publicly for the first time that approximately **$11.4 billion** in finally liquidated entries represents the disputed slice of the $166 billion IEEPA refund pool — with DOJ attorney Claudia Burke subsequently placing her preferred figure at roughly **$10 billion** (__[TTNews, June 11, 2026](https://www.ttnews.com/articles/tariff-refund-appeal-10b)__). Judge Richard Eaton, presiding in his packed Manhattan courtroom, shot back: "Even I think that's real money." Together, these developments mean importers face a dual-track exposure: the Section 122 tariffs are now actively in collection for all non-plaintiff importers through at least July 24, 2026 (statutory expiry under Proclamation 11012), while the Phase 3 IEEPA refund window is technically on track for late July but legally contingent on the Federal Circuit's disposition of the DOJ's universality appeal.

### The IEEPA Refund Landscape: Phase Architecture, Figures, and the Phase 3 Legal Fault Line

The macro recovery picture remains large. Declarations filed with the CIT reflect that approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties, spread across more than 53 million entries. As of June 5, CBP has accepted and begun processing $94.94 billion in refunds for importers, with nearly $24 billion sent to the Treasury for payment. Phase 1 of the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) portal — launched April 20, 2026, inside the ACE Secure Data Portal — covers unliquidated entries and entries within the 90-day voluntary reliquidation window under 19 U.S.C. § 1501. CBP has processed more than 16 million entries under Phase 1 and accepted more than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA refunds; of that total, roughly $22 billion has moved to U.S. Treasury for disbursement.

Phase 2 of CAPE — which will address entries flagged for reconciliation, i.e., entries subject to post-filing adjustments — remains on track for a June 29, 2026 launch. Phase 3 — the legal flashpoint — addresses finally liquidated entries, meaning entries that were liquidated more than 80 days prior to CAPE Phase 1's April 20 cutoff. CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Thomas confirmed at the June 9 hearing that finally liquidated entries account for roughly $11.4 billion, or 6.9%, of all payments made for IEEPA tariffs. Programming for Phase 3 will be ready in late July 2026 and will require importer of record numbers to ensure refunds reach correct importers and guard against erroneous payments where verification is more complex. However, technical readiness does not guarantee that refunds will follow: DOJ continues to argue that CBP lacks authority to reliquidate finally liquidated entries without an individual lawsuit, a position now before both the CIT and the Federal Circuit.

The constitutional crux of the Phase 3 dispute is the government's reliance on *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025), the Supreme Court decision limiting lower courts' power to issue relief to non-litigants. In its May 29 filing, DOJ made explicit that (1) "[o]nce an entry is finally liquidated, CBP has no authority to reliquidate or refund money without a court order," and (2) "the universal injunction exceeds the Court's jurisdiction and equitable authority under *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*" The government's appeal thus targets the scope of the CIT's remedial authority — not the underlying determination regarding the IEEPA tariffs in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* — and, if successful, could result in narrowing of relief for nonplaintiff importers with finally liquidated entries, with potential delay; legal issues on appeal include the government's prior concessions, the CIT's jurisdictional posture, and the Uniformity Clause.

Importantly, the government faces a judicial estoppel obstacle of its own making. In December 2025, in *AGS Company Automotive Solutions v. CBP* (Slip Op. 25-154), DOJ affirmatively represented to the CIT that it "will not object to the [c]ourt ordering reliquidation of plaintiff's entries subject to the challenged IEEPA duties if such duties are found to be unlawful" — a representation the CIT held constituted an "unequivocal position" — and on January 8, 2026, the government further stipulated it would refund IEEPA tariffs for "all current and future similarly situated plaintiffs" following a "final and unappealable decision." The doctrine of judicial estoppel prevents a party from taking a position inconsistent with one it successfully maintained in earlier proceedings; the government used its December 2025 and January 2026 representations to oppose importers' preliminary injunction motions — arguing injunctions were unnecessary because refunds would be available — meaning the government now faces a high bar in arguing the CIT lacks authority to order the very relief it previously told the court was available.

Also as of June 4, Terry Precision Cycling — a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) case — moved in *V.O.S. Selections* to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs; the government's response is due June 25, 2026. Plaintiffs' counsel argued at the June 9 hearing that if the government is committed to refunds but worried about CASA, it should not oppose that class certification motion — though Judge Eaton expressed reservations about that path.

The refund machinery's administrative complexity is also deepening. While Judge Eaton urged the Trump administration to withdraw its appeal and speed up refunds, he stopped short of issuing a strict order compelling immediate compliance, but noted that the delay has led to a "growing inequity" between large importers who have hired customs brokers and small ones who cannot afford such services. Eaton also raised the issue of liquidation deadlines: Thomas confirmed that CBP had not extended liquidation deadlines for entries after the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling, meaning additional entries have been becoming finally liquidated in the interim, and Eaton asked Thomas to consider extending those deadlines.

### Section 122 Appeal: *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. v. United States* at the Federal Circuit — Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606

On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge CIT panel in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* (Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606) invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — dealing another blow to the administration's trade agenda after the IEEPA regime was struck down in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* in the Supreme Court's 6-3 February 2026 ruling. The CIT held that Proclamation 11012 exceeded the authority under Section 122 because the proclamation did not identify the type of "balance-of-payments deficits" required by the statute. Section 122 authorizes the President to impose a temporary import tariff of up to 15% for no more than 150 days when "fundamental international payments problems require special import measures," including "large and serious" U.S. balance-of-payments deficits; the President invoked Section 122 to impose a 10% tariff on virtually all imports, effective February 24, 2026, through July 24, 2026.

The 2-1 CIT decision resulted in a permanent injunction prohibiting collection of Section 122 duties, but only with respect to the three prevailing plaintiffs: Burlap & Barrel, Inc.; Basic Fun, Inc.; and the State of Washington. Dissenting, Judge Stanceu disagreed with the majority's interpretation of the legislative history that liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance are the only methods to measure "balance-of-payments," explaining that had Congress intended to limit measurement to those factors it would have been more explicit in doing so.

The government appealed on May 8, 2026, the day after the CIT opinion, and on May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motion for an immediate administrative stay, temporarily pausing the CIT's permanent injunction while the appellate court considers a longer stay during the duration of the appeal. As of June 11, the Federal Circuit has extended that block. The Federal Circuit's June 11 ruling keeps the 10% tariff in place for the three plaintiff importers while the government's appeal plays out. The 10% global tariff is scheduled to expire in July absent Congressional extension; it was imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974.

For the wider importer community, the practical exposure is significant. Because most importers are not covered by the injunction, CBP is expected to continue assessing and collecting Section 122 tariffs while the case proceeds on appeal; importers of record should not assume the ruling will automatically result in across-the-board relief. Disputes over the allocation of IEEPA refunds are already emerging among importers of record, downstream purchasers, and end-users, including class actions seeking to recover pass-through tariffs; similar disputes are foreseeable with respect to Section 122 refunds.

Strategic replacement-tariff risk also warrants monitoring. Section 122 has functioned as a legal bridge following the Supreme Court's invalidation of the IEEPA tariffs; ongoing Section 301 investigations into foreign industrial overcapacity are expected to produce replacement tariffs that could take effect on or around the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs, meaning the practical tariff burden on importers is likely to continue in some form regardless of how the Section 122 litigation resolves. The administration also retains Section 232 authority: CBP confirmed Section 232 tariff reductions for agricultural, HVAC, and mobile industrial equipment went effective June 8, 2026 (__[Green Worldwide Shipping, June 2026](https://www.greenworldwide.com/federal-circuit-temporarily-stays-section-122-tariff-ruling-pending-appeal/)__).

### Structural Case Posture: *V.O.S. Selections* / *Euro-Notions Florida* Refund Litigation — CIT Senior Judge Eaton

The active litigation docket in CIT's Manhattan courthouse spans two principal refund cases before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton. In *Euro-Notions Florida v. United States*, Case No. 25-595 (the successor order case to *Atmus Filtration v. United States*, Case No. 26-1259), the CIT entered a materially similar refund order on April 7 after the plaintiff in Atmus withdrew its complaint; this transfer pushed back the 60-day window for DOJ to appeal and may have materially increased the number of finally liquidated entries as a result; the CIT partially stayed compliance with that order and instead required the government to provide weekly closed-door status updates on its refund efforts. In *Euro-Notions*, Judge Eaton separately required another CAPE status report and scheduled a June 11 conference.

In *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, on May 29, 2026, the Trump administration filed a motion stating it will appeal the CIT's universal injunction ordering reliquidation of final entries, including by importers that have not filed suit — a stance that threatens to upend the refund process for nearly half of the roughly $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, affecting importers that have not filed suit in the CIT and preserved their refund claims for finally liquidated entries. The DOJ's motion to allow a lower-ranking official to testify in Commissioner Scott's place was immediately denied by the CIT (__[Davis Wright Tremaine, June 6, 2026](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2026/06/appeal-of-trade-court-ieepa-duty-refund-order)__).

The category of entries the government is contesting is still very large — potentially more than $30 billion — consisting of entries that were liquidated more than 80 days ago and are therefore "finally liquidated" under the government's current view of CAPE Phase 1. The government's response to the class certification motion in *V.O.S. Selections* is due June 25, 2026.

### Administrative Mechanics: Protest Deadlines, Liquidation Clocks, and Phase 3 Filing Strategy

Claim-preservation strategy is bifurcating sharply depending on entry status. The administration's challenge to the CIT's universal injunctive relief means importers who have not filed suit face substantial risk they will be excluded from any refund order if the appeal succeeds; filing suit in the CIT remains the most conservative path to preserving rights for finally liquidated entries, with the liquidation clock continuing to run — as entries liquidate, they slip from Phase 1-eligible to finally liquidated.

For Section 122 duties, a parallel protest clock is already running. Because CIT relief is only to the named plaintiffs, importers of record that have paid Section 122 tariffs but have not initiated litigation or taken other steps to preserve refund rights should evaluate whether to file suit at the CIT, submit protests to CBP, or pursue other claim-preservation strategies. The Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026, in any event, which sets a practical outer boundary for new tariff accruals under that authority, but does not resolve refund rights for duties already paid.

For IEEPA Phase 3 entries, importers must file a protest within 180 days of liquidation under 19 U.S.C. § 1514; past 180 days, the path narrows to litigation in the Court of International Trade. CBP's official guidance states that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60-90 days following acceptance of a CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review. The primary rejection causes for Phase 1 declarations — HTS classification mismatches, valuation inconsistencies, country-of-origin discrepancies, and Section 232/301 duty-stacking errors — account for the large majority of resubmissions; each resubmission cycle adds 30-60 days to the timeline (__[TariffsTool.com, May 2026](https://www.tariffstool.com/guides/cape-phase-1-vs-phase-2-ieepa-refunds)__).

## Recommended Actions

Counsel for importers, recovery claimants, and downstream purchasers should immediately triage their client portfolios along three axes: **(1) IEEPA Phase 1 / Phase 2 claimants** — confirm CAPE declarations are clean against CBP's REV-615 Entry Summary Status Report and ACH banking information is current in the ACE Secure Data Portal (electronic-only ACH model effective since February 6, 2026); any declaration with a stacking error involving Section 232 or Section 301 layers must be corrected before submission to avoid a 60-90 day resubmission delay; for reconciliation entries, mark June 29, 2026 (Phase 2 launch) as a hard calendar deadline. **(2) IEEPA Phase 3 / finally liquidated entries** — for any entry liquidated more than 80 days before April 20, 2026, immediately assess whether a protective action has been filed in the CIT under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(a) or (i), as the government's position before the Federal Circuit — that reliquidation of finally liquidated entries requires importer-specific court orders — creates a binary outcome: file suit now or potentially forfeit Phase 3 coverage; the 180-day protest clock under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 is running on entries liquidated around the February 2026 IEEPA termination date, with waiver risk materializing in August 2026 for the earliest entries. **(3) Section 122 duties paid since February 24, 2026** — file CIT suits or CBP protests to preserve potential refund rights before the July 24, 2026 statutory expiration crystallizes entries; the Federal Circuit's June 11 extension of the stay confirms that Burlap & Barrel-style injunctive relief is not broadly available to non-plaintiff importers, and the merits briefing schedule before the Federal Circuit — with the government's response due imminently — means the appellate window for Section 301 replacement tariffs (expected on or around July 24 expiry) may narrow the refund-preservation opportunity considerably. Firms should also immediately review supplier agreements, purchase orders, and downstream sales contracts for tariff pass-through and allocation provisions, as class actions and commercial disputes over IEEPA and Section 122 refund allocation are already emerging (__[Skadden, May 2026](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/us-trade-court-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs)__).
