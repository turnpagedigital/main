# IEEPA TARIFF ADVISORY | April 29, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The CAPE portal's first full operational week has yielded the earliest concrete data on the $166 billion IEEPA refund process, and the picture is mixed but ultimately constructive. Bloomberg reported Monday that approximately 15% of the 13.3 million import entries that have cleared initial review since the April 20 launch were denied for failing entry-specific validations — a figure that CBP characterized as consistent with typical data-quality issues in bulk customs processing rather than evidence of systemic gatekeeping. ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-28/in-tariff-refund-process-us-says-15-of-entries-denied-so-far)) The remaining 85% of cleared submissions represent an encouraging throughput rate for a system that processed its first declarations just nine days ago, and CBP's 60-to-90-day disbursement window for accepted CAPE declarations suggests that the earliest Phase 1 refunds — covering the $127 billion in unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation (82% of the $166 billion total) — could begin flowing to importers by late June. Yesterday's closed settlement conference before Judge Eaton in Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. U.S. Customs and Border Protection provided the Court of International Trade with its first look at Phase 1 processing metrics and, critically, opened discussions on the Phase 2 timeline that will govern the remaining $39 billion in liquidated entries requiring formal protest under 19 U.S.C. §1514 or individual CIT actions under 28 U.S.C. §1581(i). ([Thompson Hine SmarTrade](https://www.thompsonhinesmartrade.com/2026/04/cbp-provides-cit-latest-updates-on-ieepa-tariff-refunds/))

The administration's pursuit of replacement tariff authority continues to accelerate against the Section 122 clock. USTR opened two days of public hearings on April 28 covering Section 301 investigations into forced labor enforcement across 60 economies — a procedural step that lays the groundwork for new tariff actions well before the July 24 expiration of the 10% global surcharge imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. ([USTR](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/april/public-hearings-regarding-section-301-investigations-relating-failures-take-action-forced-labor)) The two lawsuits challenging Section 122 authority — one filed by 24 state attorneys general and another by the Liberty Justice Center on behalf of Burlap & Barrel and other small importers — remain pending before a three-judge CIT panel following April 10 oral arguments where at least two judges expressed skepticism that the government's reading of the statute "proves too much." ([Reason/Volokh Conspiracy](https://reason.com/volokh/2026/04/10/thoughts-on-todays-oral-argument-in-the-section-122-tariff-cases/)) A ruling could arrive before the July 24 sunset, potentially mooting the legislative question entirely. Meanwhile, a separate Section 301 hearing on manufacturing overcapacity targeting 16 economies including China, the EU, and Japan is scheduled for May 5 on an accelerated timeline, and Duane Morris published a comprehensive alert on April 26 connecting all three fronts — the Section 301 investigations, IEEPA refund mechanics, and Section 122 legal challenges — into a unified compliance roadmap for importers. ([Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/new_section_301_investigations_ieepa_tariff_refund_developments_legal_challenges_section_0426.html))

The downstream litigation landscape continues to expand in ways that complicate the refund calculus for companies. Consumer class actions alleging that importers must pass through IEEPA tariff refunds to end consumers have proliferated since March, with suits now targeting Nintendo, Lululemon, Costco, FedEx, and others under theories that companies who raised prices to absorb tariff costs and subsequently received government refunds have been "made whole twice." ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/03/the-next-wave-of-tariff-litigation)) Troutman Pepper has warned that these suits create a paradox: companies that file CAPE declarations and receive refunds may face consumer claims for the same dollars, while companies that delay filing to avoid class action exposure risk losing refund eligibility as entries liquidate beyond the 90-day voluntary reliquidation window. ([Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutman.com/insights/ieepa-tariff-refunds-may-come-with-an-unforeseen-cost-exposure-to-consumer-class-actions/)) The CBO's latest estimate compounds the fiscal significance of this landscape: the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling alone adds $2 trillion to federal deficits over a decade, while the administration's replacement trade measures recover only $800–900 billion of that shortfall. ([BigNewsNetwork/CBO](http://www.bignewsnetwork.com/news/279017431/tariff-changes-to-add-11-trillion-usd-to-us-budget-deficit-over-10-years-cbo-chief))

The appeal clock remains the single most consequential variable. The government's approximate deadline to seek rehearing of the Supreme Court's February 20 IEEPA ruling falls around late May to early June, and as of today there has been no filing, no motion for extension of time, and no public signaling of intent from the Department of Justice. The separate appeal deadline for Judge Eaton's CIT refund orders in Euro-Notions falls around June 8. Continued silence is increasingly being read by market participants and litigation funders as a signal that the administration has accepted the ruling and pivoted its tariff strategy toward Section 301 and Section 232 authorities — but until the window closes, appeal risk continues to be priced into recovery estimates and funder terms. The May 14 Trump-Xi summit in Beijing adds another variable: any framework agreement on tariff truces or fentanyl-related adjustments could reshape the trade environment significantly before either appeal deadline arrives.

## Recommended Actions

Companies with unliquidated IEEPA entries should prioritize CAPE declaration submissions immediately, ensuring clean data — correct importer-of-record tax IDs, accurate HTS classifications, and verified entry numbers — to avoid the 15% rejection rate that has characterized first-week filings. Importers whose entries have already liquidated should begin calendaring protest deadlines under 19 U.S.C. §1514 now, as Phase 2 parameters are expected to emerge from the CIT settlement discussions in the coming weeks. Companies that passed tariff costs through to consumers via itemized surcharges or disclosed price increases should consult litigation counsel before filing CAPE declarations to assess class action exposure, particularly in light of the Troutman Pepper analysis identifying the "double recovery" theory as the primary plaintiff theory. Compliance teams should monitor the July 24 Section 122 expiration and prepare for potential new Section 301 tariffs on an accelerated timeline by mapping current supply chains against the 60 economies under forced labor investigation and the 16 economies under overcapacity investigation. All importers should ensure their ACE Portal accounts are current, bank account information for refunds is on file, and broker authorizations are in place for CAPE filings.
