## Analysis & Developments

### I. Four Days to Zero: The Section 122 Hard Sunset and the Section 301 Race

The single most consequential event on this week's calendar is not a court ruling — it is a statutory clock. 
The Trump administration's Section 122 tariff expires by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026, exactly 150 days after it took effect on February 24.
 
That 150-day window is a hard statutory ceiling.
 
Section 122 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending.
 Congressional extension is not a live scenario: 
the "Reclaim Trade Powers Act" is moving in the opposite direction, aimed at constraining presidential tariff authority rather than extending it.


What replaces the surcharge — and when — turns entirely on USTR. 
USTR Jamieson Greer is finalizing a Section 301 forced-labor tariff that will apply 10% duties on 14 economies and 12.5% on 46 others, a two-tier structure that formalizes what the previous 150 days of trade policy made clear: countries that negotiated bilateral deals with the White House pay less.
 
USTR faces a July 20, 2026 completion deadline on the Section 301 investigations proposed to replace Section 122 with 12.5% duties on 46 countries.
 That deadline is today. The July 7 public hearing before the Section 301 Committee has concluded; the record is closed; the decision is the administration's to make.

The 46-country list at the 12.5% tier includes 
Algeria, Angola, Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Colombia, India, Japan, Jordan, South Korea, Vietnam, and others.
 
On July 15, USTR separately announced a 25% tariff on Brazilian imports effective July 22 — two days before the Section 122 expiry — covering approximately $7.4 billion in exports including sugar, apparel, paper, and steel.
 Brazil is therefore subject to a stacked exposure: the country-specific 25% Section 301 action plus, if finalized, the broader forced-labor 12.5% layer.

The rate arithmetic matters for refund modeling. 
The White House invoked Section 122 and raised the surcharge to 15% on February 22, 2026, producing a trade-weighted average U.S. tariff rate of 13.0%.
 
Should the surcharge lapse with no replacement in place, Capital Economics estimates the average effective rate would fall to roughly 7.2%.
 A clean lapse is not expected, but any gap between July 24 and the effective date of a Section 301 Federal Register notice creates a brief window of MFN-plus-Section-232 rates — an entry-timing opportunity that customs brokers should flag to clients today.


Section 301, by contrast, has no expiration date, has survived decades of legal challenges, and requires USTR to build an administrative record justifying each duty.
 
If finalized, the tariffs may offer a more process-heavy — but potentially more legally durable — alternative to emergency tariff authorities, provided USTR develops a reasoned record, responds to significant comments, and connects the remedy to its statutory findings.
 The vulnerability is on the administrative-record side: 
USTR received more than 450 written comments, heard testimony from nearly 60 witnesses, and conducted consultations with 46 economies before issuing its June 2 report.
 Whether that record sustains judicial scrutiny under Section 301's "unreasonable or discriminatory" standard (19 U.S.C. § 2411) will be litigated — the question is how quickly.

Two structural exemptions run through the Section 122 sunset and the Section 301 replacement alike: 
EU goods moved to the EU-U.S. trade deal's 15% all-inclusive ceiling on July 1, 2026; the sunset also does not affect USMCA-qualifying goods (0%) or Section 232 steel, aluminum, copper, and auto tariffs, which have no sunset.

### II. CAPE Phase 3 — Finally Liquidated Entries: CIT Orders Reliquidation, DOJ Contests Authority

The IEEPA refund pipeline has reached a critical juncture for the most contested category of entries. 
The U.S. Court of International Trade (CIT) has announced that it plans to order CBP to reliquidate certain finally liquidated entries in approximately 3,700 pending IEEPA tariff refund cases once CAPE Phase 3 launches.
 
Judge Richard K. Eaton issued a July 15, 2026 order
 outlining the court's intended path. 
CBP confirmed the Phase 3 timeline at a June 9, 2026 hearing before Senior Judge Eaton; Susan Thomas, executive assistant commissioner for CBP's trade office, delivered the update.
 
CAPE Phase 3 will address finally liquidated entries that paid IEEPA tariffs; programming is expected to be ready in late July 2026.


The DOJ's position, however, draws a hard line. 
DOJ continues to argue that CBP lacks authority to reliquidate finally liquidated entries without an individual lawsuit.
 
In its June 4 court filing, DOJ stated that "Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final," adding that to obtain refunds on such entries "an importer must file suit in this Court, and the Court must enter an importer-specific order."
 
Judge Eaton denied CBP's motion, and the government filed an appeal on June 2, 2026.


The practical consequence is stark: 
the court's anticipated Phase 3 order directing reliquidation will apparently apply only to importers who have already filed litigation at the CIT, leaving a significant question mark as to whether refunds for finally liquidated entries will be available to non-litigant importers.
 Importers that paid IEEPA duties on entries that have passed final liquidation and that have not yet filed a CIT case under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i) or via a 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest are at material risk of being permanently foreclosed from recovery on that tranche. 
The court ordered the government to file another progress report on CAPE by August 4, 2026, with a closed settlement conference scheduled for August 5, 2026.


Phases 1 and 2 are running. 
A CBP update as of May 11 showed 15,123,221 entries passing CAPE validations; 8,338,081 entries have been liquidated and/or reliquidated without IEEPA duties; and estimated duty refund and interest amounts stood at approximately $35.46 billion for liquidated/reliquidated entries.
 
Of the roughly $166 billion in duties CBP has collected under IEEPA, approximately $85 billion in both potential and certified refunds have been accepted for processing in CAPE
 as of the most recent reported figures. 
Effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on CAPE Declarations.
 
Warehouse withdrawals (Entry Types 31, 32, 34, and 38) continue to be accepted, with refunds processed upon reliquidation of the associated warehouse entry.


A pipeline blockage persists: 
1,880 consolidated refunds have not been transmitted to the Treasury because ACH account information has not been provided by the importer of record or authorized designee.
 Those amounts sit processed but undisbursed — a straightforward administrative fix with potentially large dollar impact for affected importers.

### III. Oregon / Burlap & Barrel Federal Circuit Appeal: Section 122 Legality Still Live

The statutory expiration of Section 122 on July 24 does not resolve — and indeed makes more consequential — the Federal Circuit appeal testing whether the surcharge was lawfully imposed in the first instance. The predicate is *State of Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, consolidated at the CIT. 
A two-judge majority (Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly) granted summary judgment for the importer plaintiffs, holding that the duties imposed through Proclamation 11012 exceeded the president's statutory authority under Section 122.
 
The divided three-judge CIT panel struck down the Section 122 tariffs but limited its relief to three named importer plaintiffs, leaving all other importers still subject to the 10% surcharge.



The government appealed the CIT ruling to the Federal Circuit on May 8, 2026, with USTR Greer expressing confidence that the government would prevail.
 
The Federal Circuit stayed the CIT ruling pending the government's appeal in *State of Oregon v. United States*, so CBP has continued to collect the duty on entries through July 23.
 The stay runs concurrent with the surcharge's statutory life — meaning the stay functionally expired with Section 122 at 12:01 a.m. on July 24 as a prospective matter. But the appeal's merits survive the sunset.

Why the merits still matter: 
the July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period; the disposition of amounts already paid remains tied to the pending appeal.
 
The Federal Circuit appeal is live — the same posture the IEEPA tariffs were in before the Supreme Court struck them down. If courts void Section 122, duties paid February 24–July 24, 2026 become refund candidates.


The CIT majority's statutory theory is that the Proclamation invoked general trade deficits rather than the specific "fundamental international payments problems" language of 19 U.S.C. § 2132(a). 
Critically, there is currently no administrative refund channel for the Section 122 surcharge — the CAPE refund mechanism covers only IEEPA tariffs.
 A successful appeal by plaintiffs at the Federal Circuit would require the court (and potentially Congress or CBP) to construct a separate refund architecture. The Section 301 replacement also cannot retroactively cure the Section 122 legal deficiency for entries already liquidated under the surcharge.

### IV. IEEPA Refunds — Broader Litigation Posture and the *V.O.S. Selections* Mandate

The foundational docket remains *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, Nos. 25-1812 and 25-1813 (Fed. Cir.). 
*Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 607 U.S. 229 (2026), held that IEEPA does not authorize the president to unilaterally set tariffs.
 
The Supreme Court held 6-3 that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts, affirmed on February 20, 2026.
 
The Federal Circuit granted the motion to issue mandates and denied the government's motion to stay; the stay of the mandates entered August 29, 2025 was dissolved; and the mandates issued forthwith on March 2, 2026.
 The case currently sits at the CIT under Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton (Court No. 25-00066) overseeing the refund administration.


According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 The gap between that $166 billion exposure and the $35.46 billion in estimated refunds disbursed (as of the May 11 data point) reflects the backlog in Phases 2 and 3. The DOJ's position on finally liquidated entries — that importer-specific CIT judgments are required — is, if upheld, the largest single variable compressing the total refund pool.


CBP has stated that valid IEEPA refunds will generally be issued within 60 to 90 days following acceptance of a CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further CBP review.
 Importers who submitted Phase 1 CAPE Declarations on April 20, 2026 should be receiving or have received their initial refunds by mid-July. Any importer that has not yet received a refund on an accepted Phase 1 CAPE Declaration should escalate through ACE portal status monitoring and, where appropriate, directly with CBP.

## Recommended Actions

Clients with IEEPA refund exposure should treat this week as a deadline cluster requiring immediate operational triage across three vectors simultaneously: (1) **Section 122 entry timing** — duty rates are fixed at the date of entry, not ship date, so goods currently on the water from non-EU, non-USMCA origins should be modeled under both a seamless Section 301 handoff at 12.5% and a brief lapse-to-MFN scenario, with instructions to customs brokers on entry-date strategy issued today before the July 24 12:01 a.m. EDT cutoff; (2) **finally liquidated IEEPA entries** — any importer that paid duties on entries that have passed final liquidation and has not filed a 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i) complaint or a 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protest at the CIT should retain trade counsel today to evaluate standing before the court signals in its forthcoming CAPE Phase 3 order that relief will be restricted to existing litigants; and (3) **ACH account verification** — the 1,880 uncleared consolidated refunds held up solely for missing ACH account information represent low-hanging fruit; importers and brokers should verify ACE portal banking details are current and designees via CBP Form 4811 are correctly on file so refunds are not held in limbo when the next CAPE transmission to Treasury is triggered.
