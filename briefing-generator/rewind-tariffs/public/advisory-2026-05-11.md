# IEEPA TARIFF ADVISORY | May 11, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The week ending May 11 marks a pivotal convergence of developments across every front of the IEEPA tariff refund landscape — from the first dollars flowing back to importers, to a new judicial blow to the administration's replacement tariff strategy, to the accelerating wave of consumer pass-through litigation that threatens to complicate recovery for the very companies filing CAPE declarations. CIT Judge Richard Eaton confirmed in a filing this week that the first tranche of IEEPA refund payments via ACH is expected to reach importer bank accounts starting today, May 11, following CBP's acceptance of roughly 21% of the more than 75,300 CAPE declarations submitted since the portal launched on April 20 — declarations covering over 11.2 million individual entries, of which approximately 1.74 million have cleared all validations and 3% have advanced to active refund processing. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-tariff-refunds-ieepa-timeline-may-2026/)) The estimated $166 billion in total IEEPA duties owed back to importers of record represents an extraordinary fiscal obligation, and the government's ability to sustain disbursement pace will be closely watched over the coming weeks, particularly as small businesses continue to report portal access difficulties including ACE account lockouts and extended customer service hold times that effectively delay their ability to file at all.

On May 7, a divided three-judge CIT panel dealt the administration a second significant judicial defeat, this time striking down the 10% global tariffs imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 in the consolidated cases *Oregon v. Trump* and *Burlap & Barrel, Inc. v. Trump*. The majority held that President Trump's Proclamation 11012, which imposed the surcharge on virtually all imports effective February 24, 2026, did not establish the existence of "large and serious United States balance-of-payments deficits" within the meaning of Section 122 as Congress understood that term when enacting the statute in 1974 — rejecting the government's reliance on current account balances and goods trade deficits as substitutes for the liquidity, official settlements, and basic balance measures the legislative history contemplated. ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/us-court-of-international-trade-invalidates-the-administrations)) Judge Stanceu dissented, arguing the statute does not freeze economic measurement methodologies in time and that the president reasonably relied on Bureau of Economic Analysis data showing a severe current account deficit. The CIT entered a permanent injunction only for the named plaintiffs — importers Burlap & Barrel and Basic Fun, and the state of Washington — while declining to enjoin collection on a nationwide basis, meaning the 10% duty remains in effect for all other importers. The government filed its appeal on May 8 and is expected to seek a stay pending appeal at the Federal Circuit; USTR Jamieson Greer expressed confidence in reversal, though notably he had expressed similar confidence before the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February. ([FreshFruitPortal](https://www.freshfruitportal.com/news/2026/05/11/122-tariffs-appeal/))

The Section 122 ruling's broader significance lies in what it portends for the administration's tariff replacement strategy. The Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026, after their statutory 150-day period, and the president cannot extend them unilaterally — only Congress can enact legislation to do so, a prospect that appears politically unlikely given that both chambers have previously voted to disapprove of IEEPA tariffs. USTR has already signaled its intention to impose Section 301 tariffs as the primary long-term replacement mechanism, with two active investigations underway concerning forced labor and manufacturing excess capacity that are expected to conclude in time for new tariffs before the July 24 expiration. ([Baker Donelson](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/trade-policy-shifts-ieepa-tariffs-end-section-122-begins-and-sections-301-and-232-activity-grows)) Unlike Section 122, Section 301 imposes no comparable limits on the duration or magnitude of tariffs, and stakeholders should prepare for the possibility of higher, more targeted duties that may remain in place for an extended period — though the intense judicial scrutiny applied to both the IEEPA and Section 122 tariffs suggests USTR will need to proceed carefully to ensure all procedural requirements are satisfied.

The consumer pass-through litigation wave continued to build this week with Nike becoming the latest major brand to face a class action, sued on May 8 in federal court in Portland, Oregon, by consumers alleging the company raised footwear prices by $5 to $10 and apparel prices by $2 to $10 to offset approximately $1 billion in IEEPA tariff costs, and now stands to recover those costs twice — once from consumers through price increases and again from the federal government through CAPE refunds. ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/nike-faces-class-action-lawsuit-accusing-pocketing-tariff-refunds-while-charging-consumers-more)) The Nike suit joins a growing docket that already includes Costco, Lululemon, and Fabletics, with at least a dozen major retailers now facing putative class actions alleging unjust enrichment, consumer fraud, and unfair business practices. For importers actively filing CAPE declarations, these suits create a documentation imperative that extends well beyond the refund process itself: companies must now preserve pricing decision records from the tariff period, internal communications regarding cost pass-through strategies, and any customer-facing disclosures about tariff-driven price increases, as this documentation will be relevant both to the refund claims and to the defense of consumer litigation.

The Trump-Xi summit in Beijing on May 14-15 adds a geopolitical overlay to the tariff landscape, with trade deal announcements expected to include a Chinese commitment to purchase 500 Boeing aircraft and sustained agricultural imports, alongside a proposed "Board of Trade" joint body to oversee implementation. ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/05/10/china-trump-xi-meeting-trade-deal-iran-war-midterm-elections/)) While the summit's direct impact on IEEPA refund processing is limited, any movement on the broader US-China trade architecture could materially affect the scope and country-specific targeting of the forthcoming Section 301 tariffs, particularly given that USTR Greer has publicly framed the Section 122 litigation as being about "importing from China." Importers with significant China-sourced supply chains should monitor summit outcomes closely for signals about whether Section 301 duties will disproportionately target Chinese goods or apply more broadly.

## Recommended Actions

Companies that have not yet filed CAPE declarations should treat the May 11 refund milestone as confirmation that the system is operational and prioritize submission immediately, ensuring ACE portal access is current and ACH bank account information is entered in the Refund tab — CBP has confirmed that refunds will be held indefinitely for importers who have not completed this setup. Importers whose entries were subject to the Section 122 surcharge and who were not named plaintiffs in the CIT litigation should organize and preserve all entry documentation, track deadlines for post-summary corrections under 19 U.S.C. §1520(c) and protests under 19 U.S.C. §1514, and closely monitor the Federal Circuit appeal for any broadening of relief, as the CIT ruling signals that refund opportunities may eventually extend beyond the named plaintiffs. Companies facing or anticipating consumer class action exposure should immediately implement document preservation protocols covering all pricing decisions, tariff pass-through analyses, customer communications, and internal memoranda from the IEEPA tariff period (approximately February 2025 through February 2026), and should consult with litigation counsel about whether voluntary disclosure of refund-related information to customers may help mitigate unjust enrichment claims. Given the approaching July 24 Section 122 expiration and the expected transition to Section 301 tariffs, importers should also begin evaluating their supply chain exposure to potential Section 301 duties — particularly on goods sourced from countries under active USTR investigation — and consider whether tariff engineering, country-of-origin diversification, or Foreign Trade Zone strategies warrant accelerated implementation.
