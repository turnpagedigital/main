# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Friday, June 19, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The dominant development this week is the DOJ/Federal Circuit appellate fight over whether the government can permanently retain an estimated $30 billion or more in finally liquidated IEEPA duties — notwithstanding the Supreme Court's February 20, 2026 ruling that those duties were unlawful from inception.** That argument, surfaced in government filings at both the CIT and the Federal Circuit in the weeks following *Learning Resources*, is now the central threat to the refund recovery thesis. Simultaneously, CAPE Phase 2 is confirmed for a June 29 launch, and the Section 122 surcharge is 35 days from its hard statutory expiration. Three clocks are running concurrently — the appellate clock on IEEPA refunds, the CAPE operational clock, and the Section 122 sunset clock — and the outcome on each will materially reset refund math.

---

### 1. The Government's Federal Circuit Appeal: $30 Billion at Stake on Finally Liquidated Entries


The Foley & Lardner client alert, first published June 11, 2026 and updated within the last 24 hours, identifies the sharpest threat in the post-*Learning Resources* landscape: the U.S. Government is arguing in its Federal Circuit appeal that it can retain $30 billion or more in unlawfully collected IEEPA tariffs on "finally liquidated" entries.
 
The government's position is that the CIT — specifically Judge Eaton's universal refund orders — cannot require the return of IEEPA tariff payments on entries that were finally liquidated more than 90 days before a qualifying court filing.
 The statutory anchor for that defense is 19 U.S.C. § 1514, which renders liquidation decisions final and conclusive if not timely protested. The government appears to be arguing that the universality of Judge Eaton's order — extending refund relief to non-plaintiff importers who did not file 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i) protective actions — cannot override finality under § 1514 for those entries.


This position creates significant uncertainty for importers who have not filed protective CIT actions: "finally liquidated" entries are the most immediate concern, but other categories outside CAPE Phase 1, including reconciliation entries and entries involving antidumping or countervailing duty (AD/CVD) issues, may also face delayed, uncertain, or disputed refund treatment depending on how CAPE Phases 2 and 3 develop and the full arguments DOJ chooses to raise on appeal.


The practical consequence: any importer whose entries liquidated more than 90 days ago, who has not filed a § 1581(i) action, and who is relying solely on CAPE Phase 1 administrative processing is now exposed to the argument that its refund claim is time-barred at the agency level and unenforceable as against the government's appellate position. This is not a speculative risk — it is the government's filed litigation posture.

---

### 2. CAPE Pipeline — Phase 2 Launches June 29; Phase 3 Access Remains Gatekept


At the June 9 CIT hearing, a CBP representative testified that CAPE Phase 3 entries — a category that includes finally liquidated entries — will start being processed for refunds, but that the government maintains those entries can only be processed for importers that have filed § 1581(i) actions before the CIT. The same representative confirmed that CAPE Phase 2 entries — covering reconciliation entries and entries involving AD/CVD issues — will launch on June 29.



CBP is gauging its readiness to handle an estimated 2.8 million additional refund requests ahead of the June 29 launch, with a projected $28.7 billion in refunds expected to be processed in Phase 2.
 That figure is material: if the Federal Circuit accepts the government's § 1514 finality argument and stays Phase 3 or restricts Phase 2 scope to plaintiffs-only, a significant fraction of the $28.7 billion in projected Phase 2 refunds could be placed in jeopardy or require separate litigation to recover.


In practice, refunds may require submission of CAPE declarations through the importer's ACE account to trigger processing. Liquidated entries are considered final and cannot be adjusted through standard liquidation procedures; recovering duties on those entries requires additional steps, such as filing protests under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 or participating in additional CAPE phases that have not been clearly defined yet.

### 3. Terry Precision Cycling Class Certification Motion — Response Due June 25


On June 4, Terry Precision Cycling, a plaintiff in another § 1581(i) case, moved in *V.O.S. Selections* (Case No. 25-1812) to certify a class of importers with IEEPA tariff refund claims not currently eligible for processing through CAPE, taking advantage of an exception to the universal injunction rule that allows for class actions with universal application to a class of plaintiffs. The government's response is due June 25, 2026.


This motion is a direct counter to the government's argument that non-plaintiff importers are excluded from the refund pipeline. If Judge Eaton certifies the class, it would provide a procedural mechanism to aggregate the recovery claims of importers who did not independently file § 1581(i) actions — the precise population the government is seeking to exclude from the $30 billion finally-liquidated refund pool. The June 25 government brief will signal whether DOJ contests class certification on jurisdictional grounds (likely) or on the merits of the class definition. Watch for that filing; it may be the most consequential short-term event in the refund docket.

### 4. Section 122 — Hard Expiration July 24; Federal Circuit Stay Intact; Section 301 Replacement in Motion


Current Section 122 tariffs are limited to a 150-day window and are set to expire on July 24, 2026, and cannot be immediately reimposed under the same statutory provision.
 The underlying CIT ruling — *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* — remains on appeal.


On May 7, 2026, the CIT ruled 2-1 that the 10% surcharge tariffs under Section 122 are unlawful. However, unlike the IEEPA ruling, which made refunds available to all impacted importers, the CIT's decision granted relief only to the plaintiffs involved in the case.



On May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay suspending the CIT's judgment and injunction while it considers the government's motion for a longer-lasting stay pending appeal. In seeking the stay, the government argued that enforcing the injunction would disrupt collection operations and divert CBP resources from the ongoing IEEPA refund process.
 
The Federal Circuit noted that the government is likely to succeed on appeal, meaning CBP will continue to collect the Section 122 global surcharge — which reportedly reaches the statutory maximum of 15% — while the appeal proceeds.



Section 122 empowers the President to impose temporary import surcharges of up to 15% ad valorem for no more than 150 days. The Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026 in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely.



The U.S. Trade Representative is conducting two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations, which are expected to conclude before the Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026.
 The practical implication: the Section 122 expiration is a revenue cliff for the administration, not a cliff for importers seeking refunds — but the replacement Section 301 regime, if finalized before July 24, will immediately reset landed cost assumptions and could make the Section 122 refund calculation moot for prospective entries.


On June 15, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court denied a petition to hear an appeal challenging the legality of USTR's expansion of Section 301 tariffs on China (specifically Lists 3 and 4A). By declining to hear the case, the Court effectively cemented the lower court's ruling that those tariffs are valid, thereby exhausting all appeals.
 This denial removes any Section 301 China tariff challenge from the appellate horizon and strengthens the administration's hand as it builds the post-Section 122 tariff architecture.

### 5. The Foundational Record — *Learning Resources* / *V.O.S. Selections* (Background)

The entire refund pipeline rests on the February 20, 2026 Supreme Court decision. 
In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250), the Court held 6-3 that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, invalidating both the "Reciprocal Tariffs" first imposed in April 2025 on "Liberation Day" and the "Trafficking and Immigration Tariffs" relating to fentanyl. The decision was authored by Chief Justice Roberts and joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson.



The same day, President Trump issued an Executive Order rescinding IEEPA tariffs and directing agency heads to "terminate the collection" of those tariffs "as soon as practicable."
 
Within hours of the ruling, the President signed a proclamation under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 imposing a 10% "temporary import surcharge" on products from all countries, effective February 24, 2026, for 150 days — a provision never previously invoked, capped at 15% for a maximum of 150 days unless Congress votes to extend.



With the Federal Circuit's mandate issued on remand, jurisdiction returned to the CIT where more than 2,000 similar tariff lawsuits had already been filed. Those cases had been paused pending the Supreme Court's decision. Key issues the CIT is now addressing include the mechanics and timing of refunds, treatment of liquidated versus unliquidated entries, administrative implementation by CBP, and potential offsets such as credits in lieu of cash refunds.



The ruling invalidated billions in tariffs, potentially unlocking $175 billion in total refunds.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and refund claimants should treat June 25 — the date the government's response to Terry Precision Cycling's class certification motion is due at the CIT — as the next critical read on DOJ's full appellate theory regarding non-plaintiff importers. Any company that has not yet filed a protective § 1581(i) action should consult trade counsel immediately, because the government's filed position is that finally liquidated entries — potentially $30 billion or more in IEEPA duties — are only recoverable by importers who independently appeared before the CIT; the CAPE administrative process, for that category, may provide no relief if the Federal Circuit accepts DOJ's finality argument under 19 U.S.C. § 1514. For CAPE-eligible Phase 1 entrants, confirm that CAPE declarations have been submitted through ACE and that the importer of record on the entry matches the entity holding the refund claim. For reconciliation entries and AD/CVD-affected entries, flag June 29 as the Phase 2 launch date but do not assume processing will be automatic — the government has reserved the right to dispute refund eligibility for entries in this category depending on appeal outcomes. On the Section 122 front: the 15% surcharge continues to be collected despite the May 7 CIT ruling, by virtue of the Federal Circuit's May 12 administrative stay; model landed costs against the July 24 hard expiration and against a Section 301 replacement regime that could activate simultaneously, since USTR's 76-determination investigation is tracking to conclude before that date. The June 15 Supreme Court denial of the Section 301 Lists 3 and 4A challenge confirms that the administration's Section 301 toolbox is litigation-proof at the appellate level.
