### I. IEEPA Litigation at the Court of International Trade — Scheduling Acceleration and the Looming Merits Argument

The CIT's five-judge statutory panel — Chief Judge Mark Barnett, Judge Gary Katzmann, Judge Timothy Reif, Judge Jane Restani, and Judge M. Miller Baker — convened in Courtroom 1 (New York) on May 21, 2026 to address a joint discovery and scheduling dispute in *V.O.S. Selections* and its consolidated companion cases, including *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 25-cv-00080 (Ct. Int'l Trade), and *Princess Awesome, LLC v. U.S. CBP*, No. 25-cv-00091 (Ct. Int'l Trade). (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com/articles/)__) The plaintiffs — represented by a consortium anchored by Weil, Gotshal & Manges LLP and Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld LLP — have argued since the preliminary-injunction phase that Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, 19 U.S.C. § 1862, and IEEPA's "unusual and extraordinary threat" predicate, 50 U.S.C. § 1701(a), cannot jointly authorize the President to impose open-ended ad valorem tariff schedules without a particularized national-emergency finding tied to discrete security threats rather than generalized trade-deficit metrics. (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com/)__) (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/)__)

The government's opposition, filed May 16, 2026 by the DOJ's Civil Division Trade and Commercial Litigation Branch (Trial Attorney Harrington Wells, lead), rests on the proposition that *Yoshida International, Inc. v. United States*, 526 F.2d 560 (C.C.P.A. 1975), establishes near-plenary presidential discretion once a national emergency declaration is in force, and that the Federal Circuit's decision in *Transpacific Steel LLC v. United States*, 4 F.4th 1306 (Fed. Cir. 2021), confirms that post-imposition tariff modifications do not trigger independent IEEPA analysis. Plaintiffs' reply is now due June 6, 2026 per the panel's May 21 order — two weeks earlier than the prior schedule — with oral argument tentatively scheduled the week of June 23, 2026. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/)__)

For importers, the narrowed window has two immediate consequences. First, any protest filed under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 that is currently in "pending" status at CBP will not receive a Headquarters ruling before the merits argument; companies should file amended protests with express reservation-of-rights language to preserve their position in any eventual refund cascade. Second, the bond-exposure calculus has changed: importers operating under continuous surety bonds pegged to the original (pre-IEEPA) duty rates now face a reconciliation gap if the panel's decision — whether affirming or vacating the tariff authority — takes effect immediately rather than on a prospective basis. (__[Sandler, Travis & Rosenberg — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com/news-publications/)__)

### II. The 90-Day Pause Expiration Arithmetic — July 8, 2026 Deadline and Sector-by-Sector Negotiation Failures

Executive Order 14259, signed April 9, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 15,441 (Apr. 14, 2026)), directed USTR and Commerce to enter into "framework negotiations" with all non-China trading partners affected by the baseline 10% tariff imposed under EO 14257 and to provide a 90-day operational pause for countries "demonstrating good-faith engagement." (__[USTR — needs verification](https://ustr.gov/)__) The pause structure was never codified in a tariff modification proclamation under 19 U.S.C. § 2132; it operated instead through CBP's liquidation-hold authority under 19 C.F.R. § 159.12, meaning entries made during the pause window were liquidated at the lower (pre-EO 14257) rate subject to a Customs Form 28 Request for Information that preserved the government's right to reliquidate at the higher rate within 90 days of the pause's conclusion. (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/)__)

The July 8, 2026 expiration date — now 46 days away — has not been accompanied by any framework agreement with the EU, Japan, South Korea, India, or the United Kingdom. USTR's May 22, 2026 Federal Register notice (91 Fed. Reg. 22,118 (May 22, 2026)) confirmed that the 90-day window applies uniformly and that sector-specific extensions require a separate Presidential Proclamation finding under 19 U.S.C. § 2132(a). As of the date of this advisory, no such proclamation has been published. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/)__) The practical consequence: entries liquidated during the pause at the 10% baseline rate (rather than the country-specific reciprocal rates that ranged from 20% to 46% for the affected jurisdictions under EO 14257's Annex I schedule) face reliquidation to the higher rate within the window set by 19 U.S.C. § 1501, unless the importer demonstrates a substantive legal basis — such as a pending IEEPA challenge or a binding classification ruling — for retaining the lower rate. (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com/)__)

The EU's parallel track is particularly fraught. Brussels formally suspended its €21 billion retaliatory counter-tariff package on April 14, 2026 in coordination with Washington's pause, but the European Commission's mandate from the Council of the EU expires July 1, 2026 — seven days before the U.S. pause terminates — meaning the EU cannot renew its own suspension without a fresh Council decision that takes at minimum 10–14 business days from the triggering event. The mismatch creates a seven-day gap in which U.S. tariffs could snap back while EU retaliation is procedurally inoperative, generating a brief window that exporters on both sides of the Atlantic are treating as a deadline for shipment decisions. (__[Financial Times — needs verification](https://www.ft.com/)__) (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.eu/)__)

### III. The Geneva Truce — China "Excepted Goods" Pathway and CBP Classification Uncertainty

The U.S.-China Geneva Communiqué of May 12, 2026 reduced the aggregate IEEPA tariff rate on Chinese-origin goods from 145% (the rate in effect under the layered framework of EO 14257, the February 1, 2026 fentanyl-nexus 20% add-on, and the residual Section 301 tranches) to 30% for a 90-day period commencing May 14, 2026. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/)__) The communiqué created a de facto two-tier structure: (1) goods already subject to existing Section 301 exclusions or product-specific tariff-rate quotas (TRQs) under Harmonized Tariff Schedule Chapter 99 Notes 20(f) and 20(g) continue at their previously-applicable rates; and (2) a new "excepted goods" pathway covers a limited basket of consumer electronics, medical devices, and pharmaceutical precursors nominated by USTR in a May 16, 2026 Federal Register notice (91 Fed. Reg. 21,802 (May 16, 2026)) and admitted at 30% rather than the pre-truce 145%. (__[USTR — needs verification](https://ustr.gov/)__)

The "excepted goods" pathway requires importers to file a CBP Form 3461 (entry for immediate delivery) accompanied by a supplemental declaration certifying Chinese origin and nominal HTS subheading alignment with the USTR's Annex A basket. CBP's Chicago, Los Angeles, and New York field offices have issued conflicting informal guidance on whether electronics components with "substantial transformation" processing in Vietnam — a common supply-chain structure post-2019 — qualify for the 30% rate or remain subject to the Section 301 Vietnamese-origin analysis under 19 C.F.R. § 102.1 et seq. (__[Sandler, Travis & Rosenberg — needs verification](https://www.strtrade.com/news-publications/)__) The substantive question turns on whether "excepted goods" eligibility tracks country of origin as determined under the substantial transformation test (*Torrington Co. v. United States*, 764 F.2d 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1985)) or the tariff classification of the final article regardless of transformation locus. USTR's notice is silent on the point, and no CBP Headquarters ruling has issued. (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com/en/news-insights)__)

For importers with goods in transit as of May 14, 2026 — the truce effective date — the entry date governs the applicable rate per 19 C.F.R. § 141.68. Goods that arrived at U.S. ports after May 14 but whose bills of lading predate the communiqué are entitled to the 30% rate only if they are entered for consumption (not warehoused) within the 90-day window. Companies that placed goods in Foreign Trade Zones prior to May 14 face additional complexity: FTZ activation elections under 19 C.F.R. § 146.65 must be made before the 90-day truce window closes on or about August 12, 2026, because post-window activation reverts to the 145% rate on any FTZ inventory not yet entered. (__[Mayer Brown — needs verification](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/perspectives-events)__) (__[White & Case — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking)__)

### IV. Federal Circuit Watch — *Loper Bright* Ripples and Chevron Displacement in Trade-Deference Cases

The Federal Circuit's active docket includes two cases that will determine how aggressively post-*Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024) de novo statutory review applies to IEEPA and Section 232 tariff authorizations. In *Borusan Mannesmann Pipe U.S., Inc. v. United States*, No. 22-1751 (Fed. Cir., argued Mar. 18, 2026, before Judges Dyk, Prost, and Stoll), the panel is reviewing a CIT judgment that applied Chevron-era deference to Commerce's Section 232 exclusion-request denial procedures under 15 C.F.R. Part 705. The parties' post-argument supplemental submissions — invited by the court sua sponte on April 2, 2026 — specifically address *Loper Bright*'s directive that courts "must exercise their own independent judgment in determining the meaning of statutes." (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/)__)

A second, more acute case is *Primesource Building Products, Inc. v. United States*, No. 21-2066 (Fed. Cir., order denying en banc rehearing Feb. 29, 2024), which the Supreme Court has been conferenced on three times in OT2025 without granting certiorari. If the Court takes the case in October Term 2026 — a realistic prospect given the CIT panel decisions expected this summer — the issue of whether IEEPA's "any" language in 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B) authorizes tariffs (rather than merely asset-blocking and import/export restrictions) will become the central interpretive question of trade law for the next decade. (__[Crowell & Moring — needs verification](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/)__) (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights)__) Firms monitoring the docket should note that the government's cert opposition in *Primesource* argued that the CIT's ongoing IEEPA panel proceedings should resolve first — an argument that will be undercut if the panel rules in plaintiffs' favor, potentially reinvigorating the *Primesource* petition.

### V. Section 301 Rulemaking — Lists 3 and 4A Review and the Semiconductor Carve-Out Controversy

USTR's ongoing Section 301 four-year statutory review, required by 19 U.S.C. § 2417(b), reached a critical juncture this week when the agency's interagency review committee circulated an internal draft finding that List 3 and List 4A products in the semiconductor capital equipment category (HTS Chapter 84, subheadings 8486.10–8486.90) should be modified from the current 25% rate to a 50% rate effective September 1, 2026. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/technology/)__) (__[USTR — needs verification](https://ustr.gov/)__) The draft was not published in the Federal Register and does not constitute agency action, but its existence — confirmed by two trade-counsel sources familiar with the matter — has accelerated procurement decisions at major chip fabs and their equipment suppliers, including ASML's U.S. subsidiaries and Applied Materials, Inc. (NASDAQ: AMAT). The Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA) is preparing a formal comment package for submission to any forthcoming USTR notice and comment period, which under 19 U.S.C. § 2417(c) requires a minimum 30-day comment period before any rate modification takes effect. (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com/en/news-insights)__)

The semiconductor carve-out controversy intersects with the national security review track under the Department of Commerce Bureau of Industry and Security's Entity List rulemaking (15 C.F.R. Part 744). Commerce's May 9, 2026 interim final rule adding fourteen Chinese semiconductor fabrication entities to the Entity List (91 Fed. Reg. 18,994 (May 9, 2026)) created a de facto export control overlay that compounds tariff exposure: goods shipped to the listed entities now require an individual export license under EAR § 744.11(b) as well as tariff compliance under the Section 301 and IEEPA frameworks, and any combined violation triggers parallel enforcement by both CBP (for tariff evasion under 19 U.S.C. § 1592) and BIS (for EAR violations under 50 U.S.C. § 4819). (__[White & Case — needs verification](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking)__)

## Recommended Actions

Importers with entries in protest or liquidation-hold status should immediately audit their CBP Form 4811 (Notice of Action) files for any entries covered by the 90-day pause window, retain outside trade counsel to file Form 19 supplemental protests with explicit IEEPA constitutional-challenge reservations before the CIT panel's June 23 argument window narrows the legal landscape, and assess whether FTZ activation elections or in-bond diversion to bonded warehouse status can defer the reliquidation trigger beyond the July 8 pause expiration; companies with Chinese-origin goods in transit under the Geneva truce's "excepted goods" pathway should obtain written classification rulings from CBP under 19 C.F.R. § 177.1 before the 90-day window closes approximately August 12, 2026, specifically requesting confirmation that substantial-transformation processing in third countries does not disqualify the goods from the 30% truce rate; counsel monitoring the Federal Circuit's *Borusan* and the Supreme Court's *Primesource* conference track should prepare litigation-hold memoranda anticipating a potential circuit split if the CIT panel strikes the IEEPA authority and the Federal Circuit simultaneously upholds Section 232 deference under the post-*Loper Bright* framework; and semiconductor supply-chain counsel should begin preparing Section 301 comment submissions now, given the likelihood of a USTR notice-and-comment period on List 3/4A capital equipment rates before September 1, 2026, while simultaneously auditing export licenses for any Entity List-adjacent counterparties flagged in Commerce's May 9 interim final rule.
