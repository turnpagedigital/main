## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: The Consumer Passthrough Litigation Wave Reaches Lululemon — And the Clock on Section 122 Is Three Weeks Out

Two developments anchor today's briefing. First, a second consumer passthrough class action against Lululemon dropped this week, filed June 30 in the Western District of Washington — this one by Hagens Berman and co-counsel The Miller Law Firm — compounding the earlier action already pending before Judge Stephen J. Murphy III in the Eastern District of Michigan. 
Hagens Berman alleges that Lululemon collected hundreds of millions of dollars from customers through price increases tied to since-invalidated IEEPA tariffs, with the new lawsuit filed June 30, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington.
 Second, the hard statutory clock on the Section 122 surcharge — 19 U.S.C. § 2132 — reaches its 150-day ceiling in twenty-one days. 
The Section 122 duties are scheduled to expire on July 24, 2026; following the Supreme Court's invalidation of IEEPA tariffs, the administration moved to replace them with the Section 122 framework, which officially took effect February 24, 2026.
 Neither Congress nor the President can extend it unilaterally. Those two threads — expanding retailer liability exposure and a binary tariff cliff — define the risk landscape through month's end.

### I. Lululemon Consumer Passthrough Actions: Two Circuits, One Theory, Zero Commitment to Refund


Plaintiffs who sued Lululemon on March 27 in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan are claiming unjust enrichment — that the company unfairly received a benefit at consumers' expense.
 
The judge assigned to that earlier action is Stephen J. Murphy III.
 The case caption is *Neuman v. Lululemon USA Inc.*, 
E.D. Mich., No. 2:26-cv-11029, complaint filed 3/27/26.


The June 30 Hagens Berman filing adds a second, independent docket in the Western District of Washington. 
Lululemon imports a significant share of its products from Vietnam, Cambodia, China, Indonesia, and Canada — countries subject to the tariffs — and public reporting estimated the tariffs would reduce the company's gross profit by approximately $240 million.
 
The lawsuit states that both Lululemon's CFO and CEO publicly stated that the company planned to raise prices in response to the tariffs and did so nationwide.
 The core legal theory in both actions is the same: 
tariffs are paid by the importer of record, the party legally responsible for the goods at the time they enter the United States; regardless of who ultimately bears the economic burden, federal law grants the right to recover unlawfully collected tariffs exclusively to the importer of record.
 Lululemon, in its own CIT refund action, now stands to recover what plaintiffs allege is money consumers already paid — hence the "double recovery" theory. 
After the Supreme Court invalidated the IEEPA tariffs, Lululemon filed suit in the U.S. Court of International Trade to recover the tariffs it had paid, and has made no commitment to return any portion of those funds to the consumers who ultimately bore the costs.



The trade court has ordered the government to refund importers, but CBP has not yet returned money to all claimants; refund processing remains ongoing.
 This structural gap — only the importer of record can recover from the government, while downstream consumers have no direct federal avenue for redress — is what plaintiffs' counsel have identified as the pressure point. 
Lululemon joins FedEx, Costco, and several others as targets of proposed class actions calling for tariff refunds for consumers.
 The WDWA action is the more recent filing on record as of July 1, 2026 and is the one Law360 (Riley, July 1, 2026) flagged as the newest entry in this litigation wave.

These cases do not affect the CAPE refund pipeline directly: Lululemon's CIT refund action proceeds on its own track, and the consumer-side litigation runs entirely in district court on state-law unjust enrichment theories. But if a court certifies a class and imposes a constructive trust or disgorgement remedy on the importer's CIT recovery, the net amount an importer-of-record actually retains from its CAPE refund could be materially reduced. GCs of any importer-retailer that publicly attributed price increases to IEEPA tariffs and has filed — or intends to file — a CIT refund suit should assess its consumer litigation exposure now, before CAPE disbursements begin materializing.

### II. CAPE Pipeline: $40B+ Disbursed, Phase 2 Live, Phase 3 Target Late July — But the Finally-Liquidated Fight Is Not Over


According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 
CBP launched Phase 1 of CAPE on April 20, 2026; Phase 1 is limited in scope and applies only to certain unliquidated entries and certain entries liquidated within 80 days of the CAPE submission date.


As of the June 9 hearing before CIT Judge Richard K. Eaton: 
CBP's Executive Assistant Commissioner for Trade Susan Thomas reported that Phase 1 has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected and that approximately $23 billion had been approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.
 The government separately represented that 
by the end of June 2026, more than $40 billion in refunds should actually have been disbursed.
 Phase 2 — reconciliation-flagged entries — 
became effective June 29, 2026; it accepts entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, 06) for which the reconciliation entry (entry type 09) has not been filed, limited to unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation.
 
EAC Thomas estimates Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.


Phase 3 — the finally-liquidated tranche — is 
targeted for late July 2026 and will cover finally liquidated entries, those liquidated more than 80 days before the refund period; critically, the government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT.
 
Under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE; the government's appeal has divided the importer universe into two groups — those who filed at the CIT and those who have not — and for importers in the latter category, the risk is no longer theoretical.


The DOJ is now before the Federal Circuit on this precise question. 
The DOJ argues the CIT has jurisdiction to issue refund orders on finalized entries only for importers that have filed lawsuits against the IEEPA tariffs, and the appeal is before the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.
 Judge Eaton denied the government's motion to limit reliquidation to plaintiff-importers only, 
and the government filed an appeal on June 2, 2026.
 That Federal Circuit appeal will determine whether the approximately $11.4 billion in finally-liquidated, non-plaintiff-importer entries ever see refunds at all. 
The finally liquidated entries account for $11.4 billion, or 6.9%, of payments made for tariffs under IEEPA.


A practical execution issue compounds the legal one: 
CBP has reported that thousands of otherwise payable CAPE refunds remain delayed because the importer or broker has not established a refund-enabled bank account configuration with CBP.
 Importers who have cleared CAPE declaration validation but have not configured ACH refund settings in ACE are leaving verified refunds sitting in the queue.

### III. Section 122 Countdown: July 24 Hard Expiration, Federal Circuit Stay in Place, Section 301 Racing to Replace

The Section 122 surcharge — Proclamation 11012, 91 Fed. Reg. 9339 (Feb. 25, 2026), imposing a 10% ad valorem surcharge under 19 U.S.C. § 2132 — expires by statute at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026. 
The President cannot unilaterally renew or extend the surcharge past that date; the 150-day limit is statutory, only Congress can extend Section 122 tariffs beyond that date, and if Congress does not act and the tariffs are not replaced by another authority, they expire automatically on July 24, 2026.


On the litigation track: 
On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge CIT panel in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122, dealing another blow to the administration's trade agenda after the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*.
 
The remedy is party-specific, not universal; the permanent injunction extends only to the State of Washington (through the University of Washington), Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.
 The CIT's case reference is Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606. 
Chief Judge Barnett and Judge Kelly granted summary judgment for the importer plaintiffs, holding that the duties imposed through Proclamation 11012 exceeded the President's statutory authority.



On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motion to stay the CIT's injunction; this allows the government to continue collecting the 10% Section 122 surcharge from all importers — including the three named plaintiffs who had previously won relief — throughout the duration of the appeals process.
 Three of the four stay factors favored the government; the fourth was neutral. The practical result: every non-plaintiff importer continues to pay the 10% surcharge through July 24, and the three plaintiff-importers who briefly held injunctive relief are now paying it again too.


The U.S. Trade Representative is conducting two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations, which are expected to conclude before the Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24, 2026.
 
The administration initiated wide-ranging Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974, which authorizes tariffs on goods from countries engaged in unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory trade practices, and reportedly intends for these investigations to conclude this summer before the expiration of the Section 122 tariffs.
 For importers, the operative question is whether a Section 301 determination issued in the final days before July 24 will achieve seamless coverage or leave an exposure gap. The Section 301 investigations — unlike Section 122 — carry no statutory time ceiling, and rates under Section 301 can substantially exceed 10%.

Critically: 
Section 122 is not refundable through CAPE — it is a completely separate authority from IEEPA, and it expires July 24, 2026.
 Any importer tracking duty recovery should maintain a clean separation between IEEPA-coded entries (HTS 9903.01.xx and 9903.02.xx, February 2025 – early February 2026, eligible for CAPE) and Section 122-coded entries (HTS 9903.03.xx, from February 24, 2026 forward, not eligible for CAPE and with no announced refund mechanism at this time). 
There is no announced refund mechanism for Section 122 duties; entries from late February 2026 onward showing 9903.03.xx codes cannot be recovered through CAPE.
 If the Section 122 tariff is ultimately held unlawful by the Federal Circuit, a second refund proceeding — separate from CAPE — would be required to recover those amounts.

### IV. Foundational Architecture: *Learning Resources / V.O.S. Selections* — The Ruling That Governs Everything

For context that informs all downstream refund and litigation questions: 
The Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, in a 6-3 judgment affirmed by Chief Justice Roberts on February 20, 2026.
 
Chief Justice Roberts announced the judgment joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson.
 
Justice Kavanaugh wrote the principal dissent, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito.
 The two consolidated dockets are *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 24-1287, and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, No. 25-250, 607 U.S. ___ (2026). 
The case returned to the Court of International Trade, which faces the formidable task of overseeing what could be the largest government refund obligation in American history: an estimated $175 billion in IEEPA tariff collections.



Judge Richard K. Eaton of the CIT ruled on March 4, 2026 that all importers that paid tariffs under IEEPA were due refunds including interest from CBP, and would be ruling on all further actions related to these refunds.
 That universal refund order is the legal foundation on which CAPE rests — and is precisely what the government's Federal Circuit appeal now challenges as applied to finally-liquidated, non-plaintiff entries.

## Recommended Actions

**CAPE/IEEPA:** Any importer of record that paid IEEPA duties (HTS 9903.01.xx or 9903.02.xx, February 2025 through early February 2026) and has not yet filed a CAPE Declaration in ACE should do so immediately — Phase 1 is open, and the 60-to-90-day disbursement window means early filers are already receiving cash; confirm ACH bank account configuration in ACE before submitting, as thousands of otherwise-approved refunds are stalled for this reason alone. Importers with finally-liquidated entries who have not filed at the CIT should consult trade counsel on whether to file a protective CIT action before the Federal Circuit issues its ruling on the DOJ appeal: the government's current position treats non-plaintiff finally-liquidated entries as categorically ineligible for Phase 3, and the risk of permanent exclusion from $11.4 billion in final-tranche refunds is real. **Section 122:** Model your effective duty rate as of July 24 across both scenarios — hard expiration (10% Section 122 drops, leaving only Section 232 and Section 301 layers) and replacement via new Section 301 determination (potentially higher rates, product-specific, with no gap period); the administration has telegraphed it intends Section 301 coverage to be in place at expiration, but the 76 pending determinations create product-level uncertainty that supply chain and pricing teams need to stress-test now. **Consumer passthrough litigation:** Any importer-retailer that (a) has filed or intends to file a CIT refund action and (b) publicly attributed retail price increases to IEEPA tariffs should conduct a privileged review of its litigation exposure to unjust enrichment claims in the district court consumer class actions — the Lululemon docket split across E.D. Mich. and W.D. Wash. illustrates that a single importer can face parallel consumer actions in multiple circuits, and the absence of any announced consumer refund commitment is itself being pled as an affirmative element of injury.
