# ⚖️ Tariff Refunds | Wednesday, August 05, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Two developments moved the tape yesterday and this morning. First, CBP disclosed in a CIT status filing Tuesday that cumulative IEEPA refunds have crossed $100 billion — roughly 60% of the ~$166 billion collected — a meaningful throughput milestone that nevertheless leaves the hardest categories of claims still in queue and the DOJ's Federal Circuit appeal of the underlying refund order fully live. Second, 25 Democratic-led states filed a new CIT complaint Monday challenging the Section 301 forced-labor tariffs that went live on July 24, adding a third front to litigation that is already testing the outer limits of executive tariff authority. Both developments have direct implications for the refund math and for the new-tariff exposure that importers are now carrying.

---

### 1. CBP Files $100B Disbursement Report in *Atmus Filtration* — CIT, Judge Eaton


The $100 billion figure was reported in a CIT filing Tuesday; it represents approximately 60% of the roughly $166 billion the government says it collected from importers under the IEEPA tariff regime.
 The disclosure is the latest in the weekly CAPE status-update sequence that CIT Senior Judge Richard Eaton — the presiding judge in *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States* — has required CBP to maintain since March 2026.

The headline number requires context. 
About $100 billion, equal to roughly 60% of the $165 billion raised, has already been repaid, while over $128 billion has been accepted for processing.
 Those two figures — $100 billion disbursed versus $128 billion accepted — point to a roughly $28 billion gap between claims in the pipeline and checks actually sent. 
As of June 29, CBP had authorized $104.29 billion in refunds and paid out $71.06 billion including interest
 — which means the pace of actual disbursements has accelerated materially in the five weeks since that snapshot. The acceleration reflects CAPE Phase 2 processing of reconciliation-flagged entries and is consistent with CBP's earlier projections.

**What remains at risk.** 
Refunds could eventually climb to approximately $130 billion of the $166 billion in IEEPA duties paid by importers (before interest).
 The ceiling reflects the structural problem that has shadowed this process since the beginning: a category of entries that were fully liquidated more than 80 days before the refund clock started. 
On June 2, 2026, the DOJ filed a Notice of Appeal to the Federal Circuit challenging the CIT's Refund Order; specifically, DOJ contests the CIT's authority to require CBP to issue IEEPA refunds on entries that are both liquidated and past the 80-day window in which CBP can reprocess them.
 
CBP's filings indicate it could begin processing finally-liquidated entries — covering about $11.4 billion, or approximately 7% of total IEEPA tariff revenue — in a forthcoming Phase 3 of CAPE.
 That $11.4 billion tranche is the category DOJ is contesting on appeal; importers holding claims in that bucket face the highest litigation risk of any segment.

**Importer-of-record limitation.** 
Refunds mainly benefit large companies, as only the importer of record can claim through the CBP system, leaving many small businesses unable to recoup tariff costs passed up the supply chain.
 That structural constraint does not change with the $100 billion milestone and continues to affect recovery strategy for any claimant who was not the party that made entry.

**Interest accrual.** The $100 billion figure includes principal plus statutory interest. The interest component — confirmed in prior CBP testimony before Judge Eaton — accumulates at the rate prescribed by 19 U.S.C. § 1505(b) from the date of original payment, compounding the government's net refund obligation and the claimant's economic incentive to stay in the CAPE queue rather than pursuing individual protest litigation under 19 U.S.C. § 1514.

### 2. 25-State Coalition Files Section 301 Forced-Labor Challenge — CIT, New Complaint


On Monday, a group of 25 Democratic-led states sued the Trump administration in the U.S. Court of International Trade, arguing that the president's latest round of tariffs on goods from 60 trading partners exceeds his legal authority to tax imports.
 The complaint is filed through the office of Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield; 
the states that sued, including Oregon and New York, all have Democratic attorneys general or governors.


**The tariffs under challenge.** 
On July 24, 2026, the U.S. imposed Section 301 tariffs of 10% or 12.5% ad valorem on goods from 60 trading partners that fail to prohibit or enforce bans on imports made with forced labor; President Trump issued a memorandum on July 23 directing USTR to impose the tariffs, which took effect on July 24 following USTR investigations concluding that each of the 60 partners either failed to impose, or failed to effectively enforce, a forced-labor import prohibition.
 
The Section 122 tariffs expired at midnight on July 23, 2026, and the Section 301 forced-labor tariffs entered into force at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24 — the Trump administration's stated objective being to maintain a consistent tariff structure and avoid a "tariff holiday."


**Rate structure.** 
The Section 301 forced-labor tariffs are 10% for countries that USTR has determined have prohibitions in place on imports made with forced labor, or 12.5% for countries that have no such prohibitions.
 
An Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (ART) is a bilateral trade agreement negotiated by USTR under which a trading partner commits to adopting and enforcing a forced-labor import prohibition in exchange for a lower tariff rate; 17 trading partners qualify for the 10% rate on those grounds.


**States' theory of the case.** 
The states argue that the administration used Section 301 of the Trade Act to replace sweeping tariffs already rejected by the Supreme Court and the CIT, and ask the court to block and overturn the tariffs and refund duties paid by the states.
 
Unlike earlier tariffs that relied on emergency powers, the administration based the newest policy on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.
 The states' theory tracks a broader pattern: 
Trump ordered temporary 10% across-the-board tariffs under Section 122 purportedly to address a balance of payments problem, and the CIT found that the United States did not have a balance of payments problem as specified under the law — those tariffs expired on July 24.
 The states are essentially arguing that the administration is playing a whack-a-mole strategy, substituting one legally deficient authority for another each time a court rules against it.

**Legal vulnerabilities in Section 301 route.** The APA exposure here is material and distinct from the IEEPA litigation. 
Section 301 grants authority not to the President but to an agency — USTR — and the Federal Circuit has held that Section 301 actions are subject to judicial review under the APA, in contrast to presidential actions which are not subject to APA requirements.
 That framing opens the door to arbitrary-and-capricious challenges, procedural-notice challenges, and scope challenges that were not available in the IEEPA litigation. 
USTR's actionability determination was issued June 2, 2026, under 19 U.S.C. § 2411(b)(1), at 91 Fed. Reg. 34272.


**Existing small-business challenge.** 
The states' lawsuit filed in the CIT follows previous challenges by small U.S. businesses, which sued to block the tariffs on the day they went into effect last month.
 The CIT is therefore already managing at least two separate Section 301 challenges — the small-business action filed in late July and Monday's state-coalition complaint. No docket number for the state-coalition complaint has been confirmed in the sources reviewed for this briefing; that will surface in the next CIT daily docket.

**Refund math implication.** If the Section 301 tariffs are enjoined or vacated, a new refund obligation could crystallize — potentially requiring CBP to stand up a second CAPE-like infrastructure at a moment when it is still resolving Phase 3 of the original IEEPA pipeline. That compounding operational risk is not theoretical; 
the administration is trying to rebuild Trump's tariff programme through separate legal authority, and the new duties are already facing at least three legal challenges.
 Every injunction motion filed against the Section 301 regime will also carry an implicit argument that CBP's refund-processing capacity is already strained — a point the states' counsel will likely press.
