# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Tuesday, June 02, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's controlling delta:** The Trump administration has announced it will appeal the Court of International Trade's sweeping May 28, 2025 injunction in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 25-00066 (Ct. Int'l Trade), and its progeny — a procedural move that, if successful at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, would suspend or reverse the CIT's directive permitting *all* importers that paid IEEPA-based tariffs to seek refunds through a government-wide reliquidation mechanism. Simultaneously, the administration has proposed a 25% tariff on Brazilian goods under Section 301 and IEEPA authorities, a move that broadens the geographic and statutory footprint of the administration's aggressive tariff posture well beyond China and the initial "Liberation Day" tranche. Together, these developments reopen foundational questions about the scope of executive tariff authority, the procedural mechanics of refund recovery, and the practical enforceability of Article III court orders against executive branch customs operations.

---

### I. The Federal Circuit Appeal: Procedural Posture and Stakes for Refund Claimants

The administration's decision to seek Federal Circuit review of the CIT's refund order carries immediate, high-stakes practical consequences for the tens of thousands of importers that paid IEEPA-based Section 2(c) tariffs levied under Executive Orders 14257 (April 2, 2025, "Liberation Day") and its successor EOs. The CIT — sitting as a three-judge panel comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett, Judge Claire Kelly, and Judge Gary Katzmann — entered an injunction in late May 2025 finding that the President lacked statutory authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708, to impose *ad valorem* tariffs of the breadth and duration at issue, holding that IEEPA's "deal with" language in § 1702(a)(1)(B) does not confer open-ended revenue-raising tariff authority. That ruling in *V.O.S. Selections* was quickly consolidated for remedy purposes with *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, No. 25-00086 (Ct. Int'l Trade), and *Simplified Designs Inc. v. United States*, No. 25-00102 (Ct. Int'l Trade), among others (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__).

The government's notice of appeal does not automatically stay the CIT injunction under USCIT Rule 62(c), but the DOJ is expected to move concurrently for an emergency stay pending appeal, likely arguing: (1) the Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over customs and trade appeals under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(5) and should have de novo constitutional analysis authority; (2) the administration's statutory interpretation of IEEPA — as confirmed by historical practice in *Yoshida International v. United States*, 526 F.2d 560 (C.C.P.A. 1975) — is at minimum a close question warranting stay; and (3) vacatur of the tariff regime creates irreversible harm to national security discretion (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__). Counsel for petitioner-importers are expected to argue that the CIT's equitable relief is not subject to stay where customs duties already collected constitute completed government action and where the harm to importers from continued collection is concrete and quantifiable.

The financial magnitude is staggering. IEEPA tariffs collected between April 9, 2025 (the 90-day pause baseline for non-China countries) and the date of any final appellate disposition are estimated at **$200–$400 billion** in cumulative duty receipts, depending on collection period, with the China-specific 145% tranche (subsequently reduced to 30% under the May 12, 2025 Geneva agreement) representing the largest single-country component. Customs and Border Protection's automated Commercial Invoice system does not have a standing reliquidation queue of this scale; CBP spokespersons have flagged that processing refund claims across entry lines numbering in the millions will require regulatory action through 19 U.S.C. § 1520 and possibly a Congressional supplemental appropriation (__[Washington Post — needs verification](https://www.washingtonpost.com)__).

**Critical watchpoint for refund claimants:** A Federal Circuit stay, if granted, would not retroactively validate tariff collections but would suspend CBP's obligation to process reliquidation requests during the pendency of the appeal. Importers whose 180-day protest windows under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a) are approaching should file protective protests immediately, preserving their refund rights regardless of the appellate stay outcome. The statute does not toll the protest period for pending litigation.

### II. IEEPA Constitutional Architecture: What the Federal Circuit Will Actually Decide

The government's core brief before the Federal Circuit — likely due 45 days after the formal docketing of the appeal, placing the opening brief in mid-to-late July 2026 — will need to address three distinct constitutional and statutory arguments the CIT credited:

**First**, the *major questions doctrine* as applied in *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022). The CIT held that imposing a multi-hundred-billion-dollar tariff regime with economy-wide pricing effects is precisely the kind of "question of deep economic and political significance" for which clear congressional authorization is required, and that IEEPA's "regulate" and "deal with" language does not supply that clear statement (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__). The government will counter that IEEPA was enacted *specifically* to replace the Trading with the Enemy Act's tariff authority (Public Law 95-223, 91 Stat. 1625 (1977)) and that the legislative history of § 1702 explicitly contemplated tariff use.

**Second**, the non-delegation challenge. The CIT noted in dicta that even if IEEPA's text permits tariffs, the absence of any intelligible principle limiting the President's rate-setting discretion raises serious non-delegation concerns under *Gundy v. United States*, 588 U.S. 128 (2019), and the plurality concurrence by Justice Gorsuch. At the Federal Circuit, any non-delegation holding would require overturning *Yoshida* — a result the circuit has historically been reluctant to reach.

**Third**, the Presentment Clause / appropriations nexus: challengers argue that collecting revenue through executive proclamation without an annual appropriations authorization violates the constitutional revenue origination requirement (Art. I, § 7). The Federal Circuit is likely to decline to reach this third ground if it can resolve the case on statutory construction alone.

Federal Circuit argument is unlikely before November 2026 at the earliest, making the stay question the operationally critical battleground for the next 90 days (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__).

### III. Brazil: Section 301 and IEEPA Tariff Proposal — Mechanics and Timeline

In a separate action, the U.S. Trade Representative's office and the Department of Commerce jointly proposed a **25% tariff** on Brazilian goods, citing unfair trade practices including Brazil's digital services tax framework, its domestic content requirements in the oil-and-gas sector, and alleged dumping practices in steel and poultry. The proposal invokes both Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2411) — which requires a USTR investigation and, absent negotiated resolution, authorizes retaliatory duties — and IEEPA Section 1702 as an independent, faster-moving authority (__[South China Morning Post — needs verification](https://www.scmp.com)__; __[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__).

The Section 301 track formally: USTR would need to publish a Federal Register notice opening an investigation (estimated 30–45 days for a formal Section 301(b)(1) initiation), conduct a public comment period of not less than 30 days under 19 U.S.C. § 2412(b)(1), and afford Brazil an opportunity for consultations under USTR's standard bilateral engagement protocol. The full Section 301 timeline typically runs **6–12 months** before final action. However, the administration has previewed that it will use the IEEPA channel — the same authority now under Federal Circuit review — to move faster, potentially imposing a provisional 25% duty within weeks via presidential proclamation.

**Brazil's trade exposure** is material: U.S. imports from Brazil totaled approximately **$38.7 billion** in 2025, with top commodity categories including crude oil and petroleum products ($9.2B), iron ore and steel semi-manufactures ($4.1B), aircraft and aerospace components ($3.3B, largely Embraer deliveries), and agricultural commodities including coffee and orange juice concentrate ($2.8B). A blanket 25% tariff on this trade flow would impose approximately **$9.7 billion** in annualized additional duty burden on U.S. importers (__[South China Morning Post — needs verification](https://www.scmp.com)__).

From a WTO standpoint, Brazil is expected to request consultations under DSU Article 4 within 60 days and may seek a Panel under Article 6 — though WTO Appellate Body dysfunction (the Body remains non-operational since December 2019 due to U.S. blocking of appellate appointments) means any final WTO ruling is years away and practically unenforceable against the U.S. in the near term. Brazil may instead prioritize bilateral negotiation leverage given its Mercosur-EU trade deal, finalized in December 2024 and currently in European Parliament ratification, which creates countervailing diplomatic pressure from Brussels.

### IV. The China Track: Geneva Truce Durability and the 30% Baseline

The U.S.-China tariff truce reached in Geneva on May 12, 2025 — reducing the U.S. composite China tariff rate from 145% to a 30% baseline (comprising the 20% fentanyl IEEPA tranche plus a residual 10% reciprocal tranche) — remains nominally operative, but with the Federal Circuit appeal now in play, the durability of the truce is being scrutinized on two axes. **First**, the CIT's constitutional ruling, if affirmed, would invalidate the legal basis for the remaining 30% IEEPA tariff on Chinese goods as well, arguably stripping the executive branch of its primary near-term trade leverage over Beijing. **Second**, China's Ministry of Commerce has indicated that the PRC regards the 90-day consultation window (which runs to approximately August 10, 2026) as contingent on U.S. good-faith conduct — a standard that Chinese officials have suggested the appellate strategy may not satisfy (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The Section 301 China tariffs (Tranches 1–4, totaling approximately **$370 billion** in covered trade at rates of 7.5–25%) are on a separate legal track and are *not* implicated by the IEEPA CIT ruling. They were imposed under 19 U.S.C. § 2411 with full notice-and-comment procedures and have been upheld by the Federal Circuit and CIT on multiple occasions, most recently in *HMTX Industries LLC v. United States*, No. 20-00177 (Ct. Int'l Trade 2023). Any claimant analysis distinguishing IEEPA-origin tariffs (refund-eligible under the CIT ruling) from Section 301-origin tariffs (not currently subject to any pending refund order) is critical — importers conflating the two categories risk misallocating protest resources.

### V. Rewind Tariffs Vertical — Operational Implications

For the Rewind Tariffs practice at Turnpage Digital Markets LLC, the Federal Circuit appeal announcement crystallizes the triage framework:

**Priority Class A — file protective protests now:** Importers with IEEPA-tariff entries within 180 days of the relevant liquidation date under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a)(1) must file protests immediately with CBP Port Directors. The protest window does not pause for appellate litigation. Standard protest form CF-19 must identify the specific entry lines, the IEEPA EO authority under which duty was collected, and the legal basis for refund (CIT injunctive relief / constitutional invalidity).

**Priority Class B — monitor the stay motion:** If the DOJ moves for an emergency stay at the Federal Circuit — expected within 10–15 business days of formal docketing — the standard for stay pending appeal under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009) requires showing: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm absent stay; (3) balance of equities; and (4) public interest. The government is likely to argue all four factors; claimant counsel should prepare to file amicus or intervenor briefs addressing the inadequacy of the government's irreparable-harm showing given that monetary claims admit of complete remedy.

**Priority Class C — distinguish Brazil exposure:** Importers in sectors with Brazil supply chains (steel, petroleum, agricultural inputs, aerospace components) should model the 25% tariff scenario now. If the IEEPA track is used, duties could begin accruing within 30–60 days of a presidential proclamation, with no Section 301 comment period as a procedural buffer.

## Recommended Actions

Importers and trade counsel should take the following concrete steps in the next 10 business days: **(1)** Conduct a full entry-line audit segregating IEEPA-tariff entries (EO 14257 and successor EOs) from Section 301-tariff entries and sort by CBP liquidation date, prioritizing any entry liquidated on or after December 5, 2025 (i.e., within 180 days of June 2, 2026) for immediate CF-19 protest filing at the relevant CBP port of entry under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(a)(1); **(2)** calendar the Federal Circuit stay motion briefing — anticipated government motion within 10–15 business days of docketing, opposition due 7 days thereafter under FRAP Rule 18 — and prepare to file amicus arguments opposing any stay that would freeze CBP's reliquidation obligation; **(3)** run a Brazil supply-chain exposure analysis across Harmonized Tariff Schedule chapters 27 (petroleum), 72–73 (steel), 88 (aircraft), and 09/20 (agricultural) against a 25% tariff scenario, quantifying annualized additional duty burden and identifying any first-sale valuation or Foreign Trade Zone strategies that could reduce the dutiable value; **(4)** brief senior management that the constitutional invalidity of IEEPA tariffs, while strongly supported by the CIT panel ruling, is not final and binding until Federal Circuit affirmance or Supreme Court resolution — budget planning should model both a full-refund scenario and a stay-pending-appeal scenario where refund cash flows are deferred 18–24 months; and **(5)** for any importer that has entered into supplier contracts with tariff pass-through clauses referencing the IEEPA EOs specifically, engage contract counsel now to assess whether a stay-induced continuation of tariff collections triggers contractual escalation rights or force majeure provisions.
