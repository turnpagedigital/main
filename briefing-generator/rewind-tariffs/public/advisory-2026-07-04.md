## Analysis & Developments

### The Clock in the Room: Twenty Days to July 24

Today's single most consequential date is not in a docket — it is statutory. 
The Section 122 surcharge is set to expire on July 24, 2026.
 That is twenty days out. Unless Congress acts affirmatively to extend it, the 10% global surcharge imposed under Presidential Proclamation 11012 terminates by operation of 19 U.S.C. § 1322, which caps § 122 surcharges at 150 days absent legislative extension. 
The administration reportedly intends Section 301 investigations to conclude before that expiration.
 If those investigations ripen on time, the administration will attempt to migrate the § 122 revenue base onto § 301 authority — permanent, uncapped, and far harder to challenge. That migration, not the July 24 sunset itself, is the macro trade risk for importers in Q3 2026.

On the appellate posture for § 122: 
on June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the federal government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805, consolidated appeals from two CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of tariffs under Proclamation 11012 and § 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — meaning CBP may continue to collect the § 122 surcharge from the named injunction plaintiffs (the State of Washington, Burlap & Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc.) pending further order.
 
The Federal Circuit found the government made a sufficient showing of likely success on the merits but expressly declined to adopt any final interpretation of "balance-of-payments deficit," finding the legislative history cited by the government and Judge Stanceu's dissent sufficient to call into question the CIT majority's three-measure framework.
 
The government's initial brief is due to the Federal Circuit in July, making it unlikely that this litigation is resolved prior to the July 24, 2026 expiration.
 That timing is dispositive: the § 122 tariff will almost certainly lapse on schedule regardless of the appellate outcome, rendering the merits panel ruling largely prospective rather than relief-generating.

### The CIT's Section 122 Ruling — What the May 7 Decision Actually Held


On May 7, 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the CIT, in *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States*, invalidated the Trump administration's 10% tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, dealing another blow to the administration's trade agenda after the IEEPA tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*.
 
The 2–1 CIT majority comprising Chief Judge Mark Barnett and Judge Claire Kelly held that the administration failed to demonstrate the existence of a balance-of-payments deficit as understood in 1974.
 
Specifically, the CIT held that Proclamation 11012's identification of a trade deficit, current account deficit, and net negative U.S. international investment position was not contemplated by the statute — the legislative history indicating that a "balance-of-payments deficit" can only be measured by liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance.


Critically, 
the permanent injunction extends only to three importer plaintiffs found to have standing — the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. All other importers remain subject to § 122 duties unless they bring their own action, the administration withdraws the tariffs, the tariffs expire, or a court orders broader relief.
 
The DOJ appealed and on May 12, 2026, the Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay of the CIT's ruling while it considered the government's motion; on May 20, 2026, the CIT separately denied the government's motion for a stay pending appeal — but the Federal Circuit's administrative stay remained in place.
 The June 11 stay pending appeal now supersedes both.

The dissent by Judge Stanceu is worth tracking as the government's merits argument. 
The appeal may focus on Judge Stanceu's dissent; on the merits, Judge Stanceu argued that § 122 does not limit "balance-of-payments deficits" to the three historical measures — liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance — identified by the majority.

### The IEEPA Refund Machinery: CAPE Phases 1–3 and the Finally-Liquidated Fault Line

The operative refund mechanism — CBP's Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system, operating in *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, CIT No. 25-00066, before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton — is the dominant operational question for recovery counsel today.


According to declarations filed with the CIT, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 
CAPE Phase 1 became operational on April 20, 2026, and covers most unliquidated entries and certain recently liquidated entries.
 
Phase 1 has accepted claims covering approximately $90 billion of the total $166 billion in IEEPA tariffs collected, with approximately $23 billion approved and transmitted to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for refund.
 
To underscore the scale: in all of the last fiscal year, CBP processed only 338,000 entries with tariff refunds of any type; in just the first six weeks of CAPE Phase 1, CBP has processed IEEPA refunds on nearly 8.5 million entries.


Phase 2 is now live. 
Effective June 29, 2026, CAPE accepts entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, and 06) for which the reconciliation entry (type 09) has not been filed, consistent with Phase 1's 80-day liquidation limit.
 
CBP estimates Phase 2 will cover approximately 2.8 million additional entries with potential refunds of approximately $28.7 billion, bringing combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 coverage to roughly $130 billion of the $166 billion total.


Phase 3 — covering finally liquidated entries — is the fault line. 
CAPE Phase 3 will be ready by end of July 2026; CBP Executive Assistant Commissioner Susan Thomas confirmed that timeline at the June 9, 2026 hearing before Senior Judge Eaton.
 
However, the federal government continues to contest the legal basis for refunds on finally liquidated entries, meaning it remains unclear whether refunds will actually flow once the technical capability is in place.
 The government's position, articulated in DOJ's June 4 court filing and now on appeal at the Federal Circuit (Case No. 26-1898, filed June 2, 2026, consolidated with the *Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States*, CIT No. 25-00595 appeal): 
"Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final" — meaning an importer must file suit at the CIT and obtain an importer-specific order.



The government has stated that Phase 3 refunds will be processed only for importers who have filed lawsuits at the CIT — approximately 4,000 plaintiff importers who have filed refund lawsuits are expected to have all of their IEEPA tariffs refunded through CAPE Phases 1, 2, and 3 combined, regardless of liquidation status.
 
Under the government's current position, importers who have not filed suit will not receive Phase 3 refunds through CAPE — dividing the importer universe into two groups, and for those who have not filed, the risk is no longer theoretical.


Warehouse entry practice note: 
effective July 7, 2026 — three days ago — warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on a CAPE Declaration; any submitted on or after that date is rejected with the "ENTRY TYPE NOT ALLOWED" error.
 Importers with warehouse withdrawals (Entry Types 31, 32, 34, 38) are not affected and continue to qualify. 
CBP advises that valid IEEPA refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration, unless a compliance concern requires further review.

### Background: The Supreme Court Decision That Started the Refund Reckoning

The structural predicate for all refund activity remains the February 20, 2026 ruling. 
On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court issued its landmark decision in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (No. 24-1287) and *Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.* (No. 25-250), holding 6–3 that IEEPA does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs.
 
Chief Justice Roberts announced the judgment and delivered the opinion of the Court.
 
The majority was joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson.
 
Justice Kavanaugh authored the principal dissent, joined by Thomas and Alito, arguing that "regulate … importation" plainly encompasses tariff-setting.



The judgment in No. 24-1287 was vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction; the judgment in No. 25-250 (affirming the Federal Circuit's en banc ruling at 149 F.4th 1312) was affirmed.
 
The ruling invalidates both categories of IEEPA-based tariffs: the targeted "fentanyl" tariffs on imports from Canada, Mexico, and China, as well as the broader "reciprocal" tariffs affecting dozens of countries worldwide.
 
The case returned to the CIT, which faces the task of overseeing what could be the largest government refund obligation in American history: an estimated $175 billion in IEEPA tariff collections.



Section 122 tariffs are time-limited to 150 days; the president made clear he intended to use that 150-day period to launch Section 301 investigations under the Trade Act of 1974.
 
The Court's decision did not disturb other existing tariffs not relying upon IEEPA, including tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.

### Section 301 as the Administration's Successor Authority


The administration is building its next line of defense: two Section 301 investigations spanning 76 separate tariff determinations, expected to conclude before the § 122 surcharge expires on July 24, 2026. Unlike IEEPA or § 122, § 301 tariffs carry no expiration date — and if they materialize at the scale the administration intends, they could approximate the breadth of the original IEEPA tariffs, on far more durable legal ground.
 Recovery counsel should note the asymmetry: IEEPA refunds are flowing, § 122 refunds are contingent on appellate outcomes that may never fully crystallize before sunset, and § 301 tariffs — if promulgated — carry no refund pathway absent a new legal challenge with very different statutory terrain.

## Recommended Actions

Any importer that paid IEEPA tariffs and has not yet filed a CAPE Declaration should do so immediately for all unliquidated and recently liquidated entries — Phase 1 and the newly live Phase 2 (reconciliation entries, effective June 29, 2026) are the path of least resistance, and 
CBP advises that valid refunds are generally issued within 60–90 days following acceptance of the CAPE Declaration.
 For importers holding finally liquidated entries — the approximately $36 billion outside CAPE Phases 1 and 2 — the government's current appellate position in *Euro-Notions* (Fed. Cir. No. 26-1898) is that only named CIT plaintiffs receive Phase 3 refunds; importers who have not yet filed at the CIT should consult counsel immediately to evaluate whether a complaint can be filed before the DOJ wins that argument or before the government moves to cap the docket. On § 122, any importer that has paid the 10% surcharge since February 24, 2026, and wishes to preserve refund rights in the event the CIT's May 7 ruling is ultimately affirmed should evaluate filing a CIT complaint or administrative protest now, not after the July 24 sunset — 
importers that have paid § 122 tariffs but have not initiated litigation or taken other steps should evaluate whether to file suit at the CIT, submit protests to CBP, or pursue other claim-preservation strategies; an IEEPA-only filing may not provide a procedural basis to recover § 122 tariffs if the CIT's ruling is ultimately affirmed.
 Finally, model the § 301 replacement scenario now: if the administration's 76-country investigation produces tariffs before July 24, the § 122 sunset produces no relief and IEEPA refunds remain the only near-term recovery available.
