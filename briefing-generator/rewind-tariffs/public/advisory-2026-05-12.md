# IEEPA TARIFF ADVISORY | May 12, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Today marks a significant inflection point across multiple fronts of U.S. trade policy. The United States and China announced a 90-day tariff truce following two days of negotiations in Geneva, reducing U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods from 145% to 30% and Chinese tariffs on American goods from 125% to 10%, with both sides agreeing to introduce no new tariffs during the cooling-off period. China has additionally suspended its rare earth export restrictions, regulatory investigations of selected U.S. firms, and use of its "unreliable entity list" — non-tariff retaliatory measures that had begun to bite across semiconductor and defense supply chains. The reduction of 115 percentage points on the U.S. side represents the largest single tariff de-escalation since the trade war began, though the 30% floor remains historically elevated and the 90-day window expires approximately August 10, leaving the structural trade relationship unresolved. ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/expert-brief/trumps-china-truce-tariffs-comes-cost-us-credibility)) ([Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/econographics/as-the-trump-xi-summit-draws-closer-trade-uncertainty-still-looms-large/))

Meanwhile, the administration's tariff replacement architecture suffered a judicial setback last week when the Court of International Trade, in a 2-1 decision in the consolidated Oregon v. Trump and Burlap & Barrel, Inc. v. Trump cases, held that the 10% global tariff imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 exceeds statutory authority, reasoning that the nation's goods trade deficit does not constitute the "large and serious balance-of-payments deficits" Congress contemplated when it enacted the provision. The administration filed its appeal on May 8, and is expected to seek an expedited stay from the Federal Circuit pending resolution — a critical procedural step given that the Section 122 tariffs are independently set to expire on July 24 absent congressional action to extend beyond the 150-day statutory window. Crucially, the CIT declined to issue a nationwide injunction, entering relief only for the named plaintiffs (Burlap & Barrel, Basic Fun!, and the State of Washington), which means CBP will continue collecting Section 122 duties from the vast majority of importers while the appeal proceeds. ([Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/more-tariff-refunds-not-so-fast-court-of-international-trade-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs-but-relief-is-limited-to-plaintiffs/)) ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/us-court-of-international-trade-invalidates-the-administrations)) ([FreshFruitPortal](https://www.freshfruitportal.com/news/2026/05/11/122-tariffs-appeal/))

On the IEEPA refund front, the first ACH disbursements from the CAPE system were expected to begin arriving in importer accounts as of yesterday, May 11, marking the transition from declaration acceptance to actual cash recovery. As of the most recent CBP data, approximately 75,300 CAPE declarations have been submitted covering more than 11.2 million individual entries, of which 1.74 million entries had cleared all validations — roughly 21% of submissions accepted, with 3% already in the refund processing stage. The $166 billion total liability continues to accrue approximately $650 million in interest per month, creating substantial fiscal pressure to accelerate processing. Portal congestion and submission errors have been reported among first-time filers, but the system has remained operationally stable under initial demand. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-tariff-refunds-ieepa-timeline-may-2026/)) ([Baker Tilly](https://www.bakertilly.com/insights/ieepa-refund-cape-portal))

The Section 301 replacement strategy continues to advance on a parallel track. USTR initiated its second statutory four-year review of Section 301 tariffs on Chinese imports on May 6, opening comment windows through July 5 for the 2018 List 1 action and August 22 for the List 2 action, while the new Section 301 investigations launched in March targeting 16 economies for manufacturing overcapacity and 60-plus economies for forced labor enforcement failures completed their public hearing phase last week. Post-hearing rebuttal comments on the industrial excess capacity investigation are due approximately May 15, and the forced labor investigation rebuttals closed shortly before. These proceedings represent the administration's primary mechanism for establishing a durable, legally defensible tariff regime to replace both the invalidated IEEPA tariffs and the time-limited Section 122 surcharge, with the implicit deadline being the July 24 expiration of Section 122 authority. ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/ustr-launches-awaited-section-301-investigations)) ([Thompson Hine SmarTrade](https://www.thompsonhinesmartrade.com/2026/05/ustr-initiates-second-four-year-review-of-section-301-tariff-actions-on-imports-of-certain-chinese-products/))

The convergence of these developments creates a uniquely complex compliance environment. Importers now face simultaneous obligations across multiple tariff regimes — filing CAPE declarations for IEEPA refunds, evaluating whether to file protests or CIT actions to preserve Section 122 refund rights in light of the narrow injunction, monitoring the Geneva truce's impact on China-specific duties, and preparing for potential Section 301 tariff actions that could land before or shortly after the Section 122 expiration. The AdvaMed industry webinar held yesterday underscored the particular burden on medical device and technology importers, whose supply chains span multiple affected jurisdictions and tariff authorities. ([AdvaMed](https://www.advamed.org/2026/05/11/ieepa-tariff-refunds-webinar-summary/)) ([Forvis Mazars](https://www.forvismazars.us/forsights/2026/05/ace-reports-now-available-support-for-ieepa-tariff-refunds))

## Recommended Actions

Companies that have not yet submitted CAPE declarations for IEEPA refunds should treat the matter as urgent given that the first disbursements are now flowing and the interest clock continues to run at approximately $650 million per month across all eligible importers; those experiencing submission errors should consult their customs broker or contact CBP's IEEPA helpline rather than attempting repeated filings that could further congest the system. Importers paying Section 122 duties should evaluate whether to file protective protests under 19 U.S.C. §1514 or initiate individual CIT actions under 28 U.S.C. §1581(i) to preserve refund rights, given that the CIT's narrow injunction leaves the overwhelming majority of importers without automatic relief even as the underlying legal authority has been found unlawful. Companies with significant China-sourced imports should immediately model the impact of the 30% tariff rate under the Geneva truce against their current landed cost assumptions, while recognizing that the 90-day window creates planning uncertainty that argues for hedging rather than permanent supply chain restructuring. Compliance teams should calendar the Section 301 rebuttal comment deadline of approximately May 15 for the industrial excess capacity investigation and the July 5 and August 22 comment deadlines for the four-year review, as participation in these proceedings may influence the scope and rate of successor tariffs that will define the post-Section 122 trade landscape.
