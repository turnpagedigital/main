# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Wednesday, July 15, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The refund pipeline is now a macro event — and the Section 122 clock hits zero in nine days.**

Treasury's Monthly Treasury Statement released Monday confirmed what the refund posture has been signaling for weeks: 
the June federal budget deficit reached $120 billion, a sharp reversal from the $27 billion surplus in June 2025, with Treasury reporting gross customs duty collections of $23.6 billion against refunds of $49.2 billion — a net customs outflow of $25.6 billion for the month.
 
The refunds began flowing in May after the Supreme Court's February IEEPA ruling, and June refunds were more than double the $22 billion returned in May.
 
Combined, May and June payouts account for roughly $71 billion — approximately 42% of the $166 billion in IEEPA-based duties that CBP collected and that are owed back to importers.
 The fiscal math is now unambiguous: the refund program is not a rounding error; it is the largest single line-item shifting the federal budget. That matters to claimants because it establishes political and institutional pressure to manage, delay, or contest the remaining $95 billion in outstanding liability — a dynamic that shapes every tactical decision in the refund pipeline.

### CAPE Pipeline: Phases Live, Phase 3 and the Finally-Liquidated Fault Line


According to CBP and court filings, approximately 330,000 importers paid or deposited an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.
 Against that universe, the operational status as of last week is as follows.

**Phase 1** (launched April 20, 2026) and **Phase 2** (launched June 29, 2026 per CSMS #69035485) are running concurrently. 
As of May 26, CBP had received approximately 157,402 CAPE declarations, with roughly 70% passing initial file validation; approximately 15.9 million entries had passed entry validation, approximately $85 billion in refund claims had been accepted for processing, and approximately $20.6 billion had already been processed through the CAPE refund component.
 
CBP has since processed more than 16 million entries under Phase 1 and accepted more than $89 billion in potential and certified IEEPA refunds for processing, of which roughly $22 billion has moved to Treasury for disbursement.



CBP published CSMS #69035485 on June 23, 2026, confirming that Phase 2 would deploy in ACE on June 29, 2026.
 
Phase 2 covers entries flagged for reconciliation (entry types 01, 02, and 06) where a corresponding reconciliation entry (type 09) has not yet been filed, limited to unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation.


**Phase 3 — the finally-liquidated fault line** — is the critical open issue. 
CBP confirmed at a June 9, 2026 CIT hearing before Senior Judge Richard K. Eaton that Phase 3 will be ready by the end of July 2026; Susan Thomas, CBP's executive assistant commissioner for the trade office, delivered that update.
 
The case is *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066.
 Phase 3 covers finally liquidated entries — but technical readiness and legal authorization are separate questions. 
DOJ argues that CBP cannot issue refunds on finally liquidated entries without an individual lawsuit at the CIT; that question is currently before the Federal Circuit.
 
In its June 4 CIT filing, DOJ stated that "Congress has not given CBP the authority to pay refunds on entries that are liquidated and final," and that to obtain refunds for such entries "an importer must file suit in this Court, and the Court must enter an importer-specific order."
 Importers with a significant portion of their IEEPA exposure sitting in finally liquidated entries should treat the end-of-July Phase 3 technical launch as a conditional milestone, not a disbursement date — DOJ's appellate posture at the Federal Circuit must resolve before Treasury moves money on that tranche.


CBP has also imposed an entry-type cutoff: effective July 7, 2026, warehouse entries (Entry Types 21 and 22) are no longer accepted on a CAPE Declaration and are rejected with an "ENTRY TYPE NOT ALLOWED" error.
 
Warehouse withdrawals (Entry Types 31, 32, 34, 38) continue to be accepted.
 Importers who submitted warehouse entries before July 7 under the April 20–July 6 transition window but without corresponding withdrawal submissions need to file separate CAPE Declarations covering the withdrawals on which duties were actually paid.

### Section 122 — Nine Days to Statutory Sunset; Refund Odds Still Uncertain


The 10% global import surcharge imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. § 2132) expires by operation of law at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026 — 150 days after it took effect on February 24 — because Section 122 caps a balance-of-payments surcharge at 150 days unless Congress affirmatively extends it, and no extension legislation is pending.
 
The President cannot unilaterally renew or extend the surcharge.


The appellate posture is unfavorable to claimants seeking refunds. 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted a stay of the CIT's injunction, meaning CBP has continued collecting Section 122 duties on all importers through July 23.
 In granting the stay, the Federal Circuit signaled skepticism of the CIT majority's reasoning: 
the CAFC stated it did not "state a final conclusion as to the correctness of the CIT's judgment" but concluded the government had "made a sufficient showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits," leaving refund prospects uncertain pending affirmance.
 
The CAFC indicated that the CIT's interpretation — limiting "balance-of-payments deficit" to liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance — "may be incorrect," and that the legislative history "strongly call[s] into question" whether those were the only measures Congress intended.


The procedural timeline makes pre-expiration relief implausible. 
The government's initial brief at the CAFC is due in July, making resolution before the July 24 Section 122 expiration unlikely.
 The sunset extinguishes forward collection but does not automatically generate refunds. 
The surcharge's statutory expiration is distinct from the pending litigation over its legality; the July 24 sunset extinguishes the surcharge going forward but does not itself refund duties collected during the 150-day period, with the disposition of amounts already paid tied to the Federal Circuit appeal.


The key structural distinction for claimants: 
Section 122 is not refundable through CAPE — it is entirely separate from IEEPA authority, and no announced refund mechanism exists for Section 122 duties.
 If the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the CIT, importers will need individual CIT litigation to obtain Section 122 refunds — replicating the finally-liquidated IEEPA dispute in a new statutory lane.


The USTR is conducting two Section 301 investigations involving 76 separate potential tariff determinations, expected to conclude before the Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24.
 
Pharma Section 232 takes effect July 31, 2026 with tiered rates.
 The administration's stated strategy is to replace the Section 122 revenue base with Section 301 country-specific and sector-specific authorities before the sunset — meaning importers should model a tariff landscape after July 24 that is materially different in structure but not necessarily lower in aggregate duty burden.

### Brazil Section 301: July 15 Decision Day

Today is the USTR's self-imposed statutory deadline on the Brazil Section 301 action. 
The US faces a July 15, 2026 statutory deadline to act on a proposed tariff rate of up to 25% on more than 4,100 Brazilian products worth approximately $14.9 billion in exports.
 
USTR Jamieson Greer announced the proposed tariffs under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, stemming from issues including digital trade and illegal deforestation.


The public hearing ran July 6. 
Leading business groups from both countries — the National Confederation of Industry (CNI), Amcham Brazil, and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce — sent a joint letter urging negotiations, as Washington considered 25% duties on more than 4,100 Brazilian products citing "unfair" and discriminatory practices.
 
Washington has also floated an additional 12.5% duty on Brazilian imports as part of a global forced-labor probe covering 60 countries.


Brazil's negotiating posture is to seek an extension or deal rather than accept imposition. 
Brazil has contested the Section 301 findings, arguing that its average tariff on U.S. imports is 2.7%, which does not justify claims of disadvantaged market access.
 
Brazil has passed a law authorizing it to mirror a partner's tariffs, keeping countermeasure options available if talks fail.
 
The additional 12.5% forced-labor surcharge is viewed by the Brazilian government as designed not to be negotiated — its primary aim believed to be reinstating, under new legal grounds, the prior tariff struck down by the Supreme Court.


The outcome today — imposition, extension, or negotiated framework — has direct consequences for the refund posture. Any new tariff regime layered on top of Section 301 applies independently of the IEEPA refund pipeline; importers in Brazil-origin supply chains who received or are pending IEEPA refunds should not treat those refunds as a net offset against forward Section 301 exposure.

### V.O.S. Selections Federal Circuit Posture and Liquidated-Entry Appellate Risk

*V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States*, Court No. 25-00066, before Senior Judge Eaton, remains the lead docket governing CBP's refund obligations. 
On May 29, 2026, DOJ filed a notice of appeal and a motion to amend the CIT's order, taking the filings to the Federal Circuit.
 
The petition for a writ of mandamus argues that universal injunctions are unlawful under the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in *Trump v. [CASA]*.
 The government's strategy is to use the universal-injunction attack to eliminate the CIT's broad refund mandate — forcing each importer in the finally-liquidated category to file individual CIT actions under 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i). If that argument prevails at the Federal Circuit, the remaining $95 billion in unprocessed IEEPA refund exposure would effectively require 330,000 importers to individually litigate, a structural outcome that would delay or foreclose recovery for all but the most resourced claimants.


Importers should not assume that voluntarily processed CAPE refunds will be insulated from later government efforts to recoup or offset, as CBP has confirmed that IEEPA refunds returned through CAPE remain "available to offset amounts owed with respect to other duties."
 This is an underappreciated risk: even where a refund has been issued, CBP retains administrative authority to apply it against open duty obligations on other entries.

## Recommended Actions

For importers actively managing IEEPA refund recovery: validate immediately that all CAPE declarations submitted before July 7, 2026 for warehouse entries (Types 21 and 22) have been re-filed as warehouse withdrawal declarations, or they will not generate refunds; entries flagged for reconciliation under Phase 2 (deployed June 29 via CSMS #69035485) must clear the 80-day liquidation window — calendar that deadline now. For any entry exposure sitting in finally liquidated status, do not wait for Phase 3 to resolve: engage CIT counsel now to assess whether a § 1581(i) protective filing is warranted before DOJ's appellate challenge to the universal injunction ripens at the Federal Circuit. On Section 122 (19 U.S.C. § 2132): the surcharge expires July 24 by statute, but the refund path requires Federal Circuit affirmance of *Burlap and Barrel / Oregon v. United States*, Slip Op. 26-47, Nos. 26-01472 and 26-01606 — a result the CAFC stay order has made less certain; preserve your protest rights under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 for all Section 122 entries now rather than waiting for a definitive ruling. On Brazil/Section 301: if today's USTR action imposes or extends, a rate of up to 25% on 4,100-plus HTS lines is effective independently of any IEEPA refund credit — audit Brazil-origin supply chains against the proposed product list and model the combined Section 301 plus Section 232 duty stack post-July 24.
