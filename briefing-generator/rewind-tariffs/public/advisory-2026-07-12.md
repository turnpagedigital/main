## Analysis & Developments

### I. The Section 122 Clock: Twelve Days to Expiration

The most consequential near-term date in the entire IEEPA-refund landscape is now twelve days away. 
Proclamation No. 11012's 10% Section 122 surcharge is set to expire at 12:01 a.m. EST on July 24, 2026, unless suspended, modified, terminated earlier, or extended by an Act of Congress.
 That deadline is not a formality. 
The government's initial brief in the Federal Circuit appeal is due in July, making it virtually certain that the Section 122 litigation will not be resolved before the July 24 expiration — meaning CBP will continue to collect these duties on all importers through that date regardless of the CIT's May 7 ruling.


The posture heading into that expiration: the Federal Circuit has already acted to preserve the status quo. 
On June 11, 2026, the Federal Circuit granted the government's motions for a stay pending appeal in *State of Oregon v. Trump*, Nos. 2026-1804 and 2026-1805 — consolidated appeals from the CIT decisions that had enjoined enforcement of Proclamation No. 11012 — permitting CBP to continue collecting the 10% surcharge from the State of Washington, Burlap and Barrel, Inc., and Basic Fun, Inc. pending further order.
 The appellate court's stay analysis carried a notable signal: 
the Federal Circuit concluded the government had made a sufficient showing it is likely to succeed on the merits, stating that the CIT's interpretation — limiting "balance-of-payments deficit" to deficits measured by liquidity, official settlements, or basic balance — "may be incorrect," and that legislative history "strongly call[s] into question" whether those were the only methods Congress intended.


That language matters for refund-planning purposes. 
The Federal Circuit did not state a final conclusion on the correctness of the CIT's judgment, and the opportunity for Section 122 refunds remains uncertain pending affirmance of the CIT's decision and any possible Supreme Court appeal; if the Federal Circuit ultimately sustains the CIT's opinion, importers should be prepared to file complaints at the CIT under 19 U.S.C. § 1581(i) to seek refunds.


For GCs and CFOs modeling recovery scenarios: 
the Section 122 tariffs will expire on July 24, 2026 in any event, and the administration has an array of other tariff authorities on which it can rely regardless of the appellate outcome.
 The structural question — whether any Section 122 duties already collected are refundable — will not be answered by expiration alone. It requires either Federal Circuit affirmance of the CIT or independent CIT litigation under § 1581(i).

### II. The CAPE Refund Pipeline and the Finally-Liquidated Entry Problem

The IEEPA refund pipeline remains the more immediate recovery vehicle, but the government's Federal Circuit appeal continues to cast a shadow over the ~$30 billion in finally-liquidated entries. The foundational refund order — first entered in *Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States*, Case No. 26-1259, then transferred on April 7 to *Euro-Notions Florida v. United States*, Case No. 25-595 after Atmus withdrew — 
concerns "finally liquidated" entries, i.e., entries liquidated more than 90 days prior (80 days under CAPE's ten-day buffer), which may account for an estimated $30 billion or more in unlawfully collected IEEPA tariff payments.



CBP responded to the CIT's refund order by developing the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system — a new ACE module allowing importers or their brokers to upload entry lists — but the CIT temporarily suspended its requirement of immediate refunds while CBP completes and deploys the process. As of mid-March 2026, CBP reported the claim-portal component approximately 73% complete, the mass processing component 25% complete, the review and liquidation/reliquidation component 80% complete, and the refund component 63% complete.



That development process creates material uncertainty for importers whose entries fall outside CAPE Phase 1 — most immediately, "finally liquidated" entries beyond 90 days (80 days under CAPE) from liquidation.
 
Other categories also at risk include reconciliation entries and entries involving antidumping or countervailing duty (AD/CVD) issues, which may face delayed, uncertain, or contested refund treatment depending on how CAPE Phase 2 plays out.


At the June 9 CIT hearing in *V.O.S. Selections*, 
a CBP representative testified that CAPE Phase 3 entries — including finally liquidated entries — will start being processed for refunds,
 but no timeline was provided. A parallel procedural development in *V.O.S. Selections* warrants attention: 
Terry Precision Cycling, a plaintiff in another Section 1581(i) action, moved to certify a class of importers with IEEPA refund claims not currently eligible for CAPE processing, taking advantage of the exception to the universal injunction rule that allows class actions with universal application.
 The government's response to that certification motion was due June 25; the court's ruling on it will determine whether the finally-liquidated entry cohort can access a class vehicle rather than individually litigating under § 1514 or § 1581(i).

### III. The Consumer Class Action Surge: A New Liability Overhang for Importers

A development that has materially shifted the IEEPA refund calculus over the past two weeks: 
since the Supreme Court invalidated IEEPA tariffs in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* in February 2026, plaintiffs' firms have filed more than 100 putative consumer class actions against businesses nationwide that passed tariff costs on to consumers and are now positioned to receive government refunds — alleging an improper "double recovery." No court has yet ruled on the merits of these claims, and defendants have raised significant threshold, procedural, and substantive defenses.


The Foley & Lardner alert published July 9, 2026 is the freshest survey of the field. The core structural problem for importer-defendants is statutory: 
only importers of record — not distributors, retailers, or consumers — have any right to recover tariff payments from the government.
 That asymmetry is what plaintiffs are exploiting. 
Because only importers of record have standing to seek refunds in the CIT, consumers are filing claims in state and federal courts under equitable theories.


The filing landscape is broad and accelerating. 
Beginning in early March 2026, plaintiffs' firms began filing consumer class actions against businesses that allegedly passed IEEPA tariff costs to consumers; as of mid-June, more than 80 putative class actions had been filed in courts across the country, spanning more than 20 federal judicial districts across more than 20 states, and new cases continue to be filed — targeting shipping and logistics companies, retailers, manufacturers, consumer brands, and automobile manufacturers and distributors.


An early bellwether illustrates the exposure magnitude. 
A proposed class action filed March 27, 2026 against Lululemon USA Inc. in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, Case No. 2:26-cv-11029, alleges the company passed approximately $240 million in IEEPA tariff costs onto consumers through higher prices while simultaneously seeking a full government refund — asserting claims for unjust enrichment and money had and received, and seeking certification of a nationwide class for the period February 1, 2025 through February 24, 2026.


Among the earliest-filed suits: 
*Stockov v. Costco Wholesale Corp.*, No. 1:26-cv-02734 (N.D. Ill. filed Mar. 11, 2026) and *Deburro v. Federal Express Corp. & FedEx Logistics, Inc.*, No. 2:26-cv-02240 (W.D. Tenn. filed Mar. 6, 2026),
 
along with *Rosado v. FedEx Corp.*, No. 1:26-cv-01861 (S.D.N.Y. filed Mar. 6, 2026).
 FedEx has become a heavily targeted defendant, with multiple overlapping putative classes across multiple districts, raising obvious consolidation questions under 28 U.S.C. § 1407.

The principal legal theories are: (1) unjust enrichment and money-had-and-received, premised on the "double recovery" theory; (2) consumer protection claims under state UDAP statutes, focused on how tariff-related charges were described and whether pass-through was disclosed; and (3) contract claims asserting that provisions allowing pass-through of "duties, taxes, or governmental charges" did not cover charges later deemed unlawful. 
These early complaints have vulnerabilities including a lack of deception, causation, and materiality; and even if plaintiffs overcome those hurdles, any monetary relief would be limited to the amount of the tariff-driven price increase per product, not the full purchase price.



This continuing uncertainty around how and when refunds will be paid matters for class action defendants: many consumer complaints are premised not only on refunds already received, but also on companies' eligibility to pursue refunds and their alleged duty to seek and share those refunds.

### IV. Section 301 Investigations and the July 20 USTR Deadline

The administration's longer-term tariff architecture rests on Section 301 investigations launched in March 2026. 
The USTR's completion deadline for the Section 301 investigations launched March 11 is July 20, 2026 — with proposed 12.5% duties on 46 countries including China, Vietnam, India, Thailand, Japan, and South Korea.
 That deadline falls four days before the Section 122 expiration. If USTR publishes proposed Section 301 duties on July 20 and the Section 122 surcharge lapses on July 24, the effective tariff rate on key trading partners will shift materially — resetting the refund math for importers who modeled their Section 122 exposure under the assumption of a replacement regime.


As of July 2026, China faces a 10% Section 122 plus 25–100% Section 301 effective rate of 35–110%; Canada and Mexico face 0% on USMCA-qualifying goods (approximately 89% of goods), otherwise 10% Section 122.
 Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum remain unaffected by any of the IEEPA or Section 122 litigation. 
The Learning Resources decision did not disturb tariffs imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 or Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, and courts have rejected prior attempts to limit the scope of those authorities.

### V. Statutory and Procedural Preservation: What Importers Must Do Before July 24

The Section 122 expiration on July 24 does not automatically create refund rights — and the failure to file timely § 1514 protests or § 1581(i) actions may forfeit them permanently. 
Because Section 122 tariffs are imposed by the President, CBP lacks discretion over whether to collect and assess the duties; and where CBP is not the decisionmaker, a liquidation is non-protestable. In the absence of a universal refund order, CBP is not likely to accept a post-summary correction on Section 122 issues, as it does not for IEEPA duty refunds. To obtain the relief granted to the three plaintiffs in *Oregon* and *Burlap and Barrel*, an importer at this point must file its own action at the CIT under § 1581(i).



If an appellate court ultimately invalidates the Section 122 tariffs, it is reasonable to expect CBP will launch a refund process under the CAPE mechanism; however, this is not assured, and CIT intervention — whether on remand in the Oregon and Burlap and Barrel cases or through separate CIT actions — may be required.
 The duty-drawback mechanism under 19 U.S.C. § 1313 remains available for importers who re-exported goods subject to Section 122 duties — a parallel recovery path that does not depend on the Oregon/Burlap appellate outcome.

## Recommended Actions

Importers with entries subject to the Section 122 10% surcharge (Proclamation No. 11012, effective February 24–July 24, 2026) should immediately inventory all unliquidated entries and calculate the aggregate duty exposure before the July 24 expiration, as the window to file a CIT § 1581(i) action to preserve refund rights based on the *Oregon v. United States* and *Burlap and Barrel, Inc. v. United States* (Slip Op. 26-47, Court Nos. 26-01472 & 26-01606) rulings is open now and will narrow once entries liquidate; separately, importers with IEEPA-duty entries not yet in CAPE Phase 1 — particularly finally-liquidated entries potentially comprising ~$30 billion in the aggregate — should evaluate whether to join the Terry Precision Cycling class certification motion in *V.O.S. Selections* or file independent § 1581(i) actions, preserving both the CAPE queue position and litigation optionality; companies that passed IEEPA tariff costs through to consumers and are pursuing CAPE refunds face a material secondary exposure in the now 100+ consumer class actions, and should immediately audit internal pricing records, assess whether arbitration clauses in consumer terms of service are enforceable against putative class members, and consider whether proactive price adjustments or customer disclosure programs reduce exposure to unjust enrichment theories before any refund is actually received.
