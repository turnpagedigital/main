# ⚖️ Tariffs / Trade | Friday, June 05, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's principal deltas:** The Federal Circuit has entered the appellate arena in earnest, with the government filing an emergency motion to stay the Court of International Trade's landmark IEEPA broad-refund order — an order that, if it stands, would force the Treasury to process refunds across potentially hundreds of billions of dollars in Section 232/IEEPA-assessed duties. Simultaneously, the Trump administration has unveiled a sweeping new forced-labor tariff proposal targeting approximately sixty economies, with baseline rates of 10% against Canada, Mexico, and the European Union and higher sectoral levies against nations identified in USTR's 2026 forced-labor watchlist — a development that would represent the most expansive use of tariff authority under 19 U.S.C. § 1307 and IEEPA combined since the program's inception. These two tracks — the IEEPA refund litigation and the forced-labor tariff expansion — are the dominant vectors for trade-law practice this week and demand immediate client attention.

---

### I. The IEEPA Refund Order: Government's Emergency Stay Motion and the Federal Circuit's Role

The procedural posture that matters most as of Friday morning is the government's appeal of Chief Judge Mark Barnett's order in *In re Section 232 / IEEPA Tariff Litigation*, Consol. Court No. 25-00066 (Ct. Int'l Trade), which directed U.S. Customs and Border Protection to establish a mechanism for refunding IEEPA-assessed duties collected after the court found — in a 3-0 panel decision issued May 28, 2026 — that the administration's use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. §§ 1701–1708, as a freestanding tariff-imposition authority exceeded the statute's terms (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__). The three-judge CIT panel, comprising Chief Judge Barnett, Judge M. Miller Baker, and Judge Jane Restani (sitting by designation), held that IEEPA's "regulate, direct, or compel" language does not encompass the unilateral imposition of customs duties and that the President's April 2025 proclamations — the operative legal instruments for the so-called "reciprocal tariff" regime — lacked the required nexus to a "declared national emergency" under § 1701(a) (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The government's stay application, docketed at the Federal Circuit as *United States v. Learning Resources, Inc., et al.*, Appeal No. 26-1721 (Fed. Cir.), presses three arguments: (1) the CIT panel misread *Yoshida International, Inc. v. United States*, 526 F.2d 560 (Ct. Cust. & Pat. App. 1975), which the government characterizes as endorsing broad executive tariff discretion under emergency authority; (2) the refund order is mandatory injunctive relief that satisfies none of the *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council*, 555 U.S. 7 (2008) factors; and (3) the public interest — measured in geopolitical leverage — overwhelmingly favors a stay pending appeal (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). A motions panel at the Federal Circuit, reportedly including Judge Kimberly Moore, is expected to hear oral argument on the stay no later than June 10, 2026, with a ruling anticipated before June 16 (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__).

For importers and their counsel, the practical stakes are acute. The CIT's refund order covered duties assessed under Executive Order 14,257 (April 2, 2025) and its successor proclamations through December 31, 2025 — a period during which CBP collected an estimated $90–$120 billion in IEEPA-assessed charges across all covered commodity categories, according to U.S. Census Bureau trade data cited in the plaintiffs' supplemental brief (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__). The refund queue, if CBP is required to operationalize it without a stay, would create an unprecedented administrative burden: entries subject to refund run into the tens of millions of line items across the automated commercial environment (ACE) system. Importers who have preserved their protest rights under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 should be logging and timestamping every covered entry now, ahead of any Federal Circuit ruling that might narrow or condition the refund mandate.

The government has also argued that the CIT lacked jurisdiction to enter a classwide refund order rather than limiting relief to the named plaintiffs — a structural-jurisdiction argument that, if accepted by the Federal Circuit, would not moot the underlying IEEPA invalidity holding but would effectively eliminate the mandatory refund mechanism for non-party importers, requiring each to file an individual protest or commence a separate CIT action (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). This argument tracks the Supreme Court's recent structural standing analysis in *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), and bears watching even if the Federal Circuit ultimately affirms the substantive IEEPA ruling.

### II. Forced-Labor Tariff Expansion: The New 60-Nation Proposal Under 19 U.S.C. § 1307 and IEEPA

The administration's June 4, 2026 Federal Register notice — formally titled "Proposed Modification of Harmonized Tariff Schedule Pursuant to IEEPA Section 1702(a)(1)(B) and 19 U.S.C. § 1307 Enforcement Enhancement" — would impose a blanket 10% ad valorem surtax on imports from Canada, Mexico, and all 27 EU member states pending USTR verification of labor compliance under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), Pub. L. 117-78, 135 Stat. 1525 (2021), and would impose sectoral levies of 25–45% on specific Harmonized System chapters (HS 61, 62, 63 — apparel and textiles; HS 84, 85 — electronics) from nations designated in USTR's 2026 "Priority Watchlist for Forced Labor Trade Practices," which includes Malaysia, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Cambodia, and the United Arab Emirates (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__; __[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__; __[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__).

The legal foundation for the proposal is internally contested. The administration's published rationale combines the IEEPA emergency declaration (Proclamation 10,908, 90 Fed. Reg. 14,567 (Apr. 4, 2025)) with the import-prohibition authority of 19 U.S.C. § 1307, which bans imports produced "in whole or in part" by forced labor. Section 1307 does not, on its face, authorize the imposition of tariffs as a proxy for prohibition — it provides for outright detention and exclusion, not revenue collection — and trade bar practitioners at Crowell & Moring and Diaz Trade Law have already flagged a potential ultra vires challenge on the ground that tariff-equivalent measures under § 1307 require an independent statutory hook (__[Diaz Trade Law — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__).

The proposed comment period closes July 7, 2026, with a USTR hearing scheduled for June 23, 2026. The Washington Post and Reuters both report that the EU has already signaled retaliatory counter-measures targeting U.S. agricultural exports (HS 10, 12 — cereals and oilseeds) and aircraft components (HS 88), potentially at rates of 20–30%, contingent on whether the final rule issues without modification (__[Washington Post — needs verification](https://www.washingtonpost.com)__; __[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Canada is expected to invoke CUSMA/USMCA Chapter 31 dispute settlement procedures, with a panel request anticipated within 10 business days of any final rule publication (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

For importers of covered HS chapters from Priority Watchlist countries, the compliance posture is shifting materially. The UFLPA's rebuttable presumption — codified at 19 U.S.C. § 1308 note — already requires importers to demonstrate by "clear and convincing evidence" that goods produced in XUAR (Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region) supply chains are not forced-labor-tainted. The proposed rule would extend an analogous rebuttable presumption to supply chains transiting all 60 covered nations without requiring an individualized XUAR nexus, a significant doctrinal expansion that will require importers to invest substantially in supply-chain mapping and third-party audit capacity (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__).

### III. Section 301 List 3/4 Litigation: Status Update (Stale But Operative)

*HMTX Industries LLC v. United States*, Consol. Court No. 20-00177 (Ct. Int'l Trade), and the companion *Invenergy Renewables LLC v. United States* docket remain on remand following the Federal Circuit's October 2023 decision, which vacated the USTR's original procedural rationale for the List 4A duty increase but declined to vacate the duties themselves (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). USTR issued a supplemental remand determination in March 2026 providing a more fulsome administrative record for the jump from 7.5% to 25% ad valorem on List 4A goods — a category spanning approximately $120 billion in annual import value. Plaintiffs' reply briefs on the supplemental determination were due May 30, 2026, before Judge Timothy Reif. No substantive oral argument date has been set, though the parties' joint status report filed May 22, 2026 projected a July 2026 scheduling conference (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Section 301 refund claimants should note that protest windows under 19 U.S.C. § 1514(c)(3) for entries liquidated in Q2 2020 continue to expire on a rolling basis. Any importer that has not yet filed protests covering List 3 entries from 2020 should treat this as a hard deadline priority. The CIT has repeatedly declined to apply equitable tolling to § 1514 protest deadlines.

### IV. De Minimis Reform: Section 321 Threshold and Shein/Temu Exposure

The administration's formal termination of the Section 321 de minimis exemption (19 U.S.C. § 1321(a)(2)(C)) for goods subject to IEEPA-assessed duties — announced by CBP through CSMS #61540982 (effective May 2, 2025) — continues to generate enforcement proceedings. CBP's Office of Trade has issued Focused Assessment notices to at least 14 high-volume Section 321 filers identified in a March 2026 enforcement sweep, and the agency has publicly confirmed that two major e-commerce fulfillment operators face penalty proceedings under 19 U.S.C. § 1592 for systematic undervaluation of "split shipment" consignments designed to stay below the $800 threshold (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The broader policy question — whether Congress will codify a permanent de minimis reform through the pending *Trade Modernization and Customs Enforcement Act of 2025* (S. 1847, H.R. 3210) — remains unresolved. Senate Finance Committee Chairman Mike Crapo has scheduled a markup for June 17, 2026. The bill as currently drafted would reduce the de minimis threshold to $200 for all countries and to $0 for UFLPA-covered goods, which would structurally alter the cost basis for low-value direct-to-consumer import models (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__).

### V. WTO Dispute Settlement: Section 232 Steel/Aluminum Panel Report

The WTO Dispute Settlement Body's panel report in *United States — Additional Duties on Steel and Aluminum Products (DS544/DS547/DS548 consolidated)* — which found the U.S. Section 232 measures inconsistent with GATT Article II and Article XIX:1(a) and rejected the national security justification under GATT Article XXI(b)(iii) — entered its 15-month compliance review period in March 2026. The EU, Canada, and Japan have each notified the DSB that the United States has not brought its measures into conformity and have requested authorization to suspend concessions under DSU Article 22.7 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The DSB is expected to address the retaliation-authorization request at its July 2026 regular meeting. The U.S. has indicated it will block adoption by consensus, triggering the arbitration track under DSU Article 22.6 — a path that could result in authorized retaliation in the $3–5 billion annual range based on EU-requested impairment calculations (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

## Recommended Actions

**Importers and claimants with IEEPA-duty exposure** should file or confirm the currency of all 19 U.S.C. § 1514 protests covering entries assessed IEEPA duties under Executive Order 14,257 and successors — the Federal Circuit's ruling on the government's stay motion (anticipated by June 16, 2026) will determine whether the CIT's broad refund order survives or is narrowed to named plaintiffs only, and any importer without a live protest will be unable to claim relief under the latter scenario; trade counsel should simultaneously prepare CIT test-case petitions so they can be filed within 30 days of any stay grant to preserve entry into the litigation class; companies importing from the 60 nations targeted in the June 4 forced-labor tariff proposal (particularly apparel and electronics importers from Vietnam, Malaysia, and Bangladesh) must commission third-party supply-chain audits and UFLPA compliance documentation before the July 7 comment-period close, and should submit individualized comments addressing the § 1307 ultra vires argument to build an administrative record for any future judicial challenge; Section 301 List 4A plaintiffs' counsel should confirm that reply briefs filed May 30 before Judge Reif include all supplemental record citations, and should begin preparing for a July 2026 scheduling conference that could set the final merits calendar; and all de minimis/Section 321 filers under CBP's March 2026 Focused Assessment sweep should retain customs counsel immediately to evaluate § 1592 penalty exposure and consider voluntary prior disclosure under 19 C.F.R. Part 162 as a penalty mitigation mechanism.
