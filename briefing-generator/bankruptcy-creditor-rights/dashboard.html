II. Harvest Sherwood Food Distributors — Confirmation Hearing Aug. 5 (N.D. Tex. No. 25-80109, Judge Jernigan)

The confirmation hearing in In re Harvest Sherwood Food Distributors, Inc., Bankr. N.D. Tex. No. 25-80109 (Judge Stacey G.C. Jernigan), is now five days out. The objection posture has sharpened materially since last week.

The U.S. Trustee objection. The U.S. Trustee filed an objection to confirmation of Harvest Sherwood's Third Amended Plan, filed on July 20, and adopted the arguments from an earlier Disclosure Statement objection at Docket No. 797. Two provisions survive the consensual resolution of prior disputes: the opt-out third-party release mechanism, and a proposed charitable donation of residual trust funds. The plan confirmation hearing is currently scheduled for August 5, 2026.

The release fight matters structurally. Post-Purdue (Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P., 603 U.S. 204 (2024)), opt-out — rather than opt-in — non-debtor releases in liquidating Chapter 11 plans occupy contested territory. The U.S. Trustee's position is that absent clear statutory authority, passive creditor inaction cannot be construed as consent to release non-debtor parties. This will put Judge Jernigan in a position that has divided courts across circuits since Purdue issued: whether the opt-out mechanism satisfies Purdue's consent requirement or whether it is impermissible under 11 U.S.C. § 524(g) and the general discharge-prohibition rule. (BankruptcyData.com, July 20, 2026BU.S. Trustee Objects to Plan Confirmation Over Opt-Out Third-Party Releases (ankruptcyData.com, July 2026))

The Atlas Grove financing and the litigation-asset thesis. The deeper investor story in this case is the estate's litigation portfolio. Atlas Grove Management received court approval on July 8, 2026, and funded a $150 million DIP facility to the Harvest Sherwood estate, and is also the stalking horse for the exit funding process.

As plan funder, Atlas Grove would seek to maximize value of more than $1 billion in antitrust litigation claims against more than 25 defendants, primarily across the chicken, pork, and beef producer industries.

Major unsecured creditors include National Beef Packaging ($15.5 million), Tyson Foods ($13 million), MegaCorp Logistics ($3.25 million), Dutch Farms ($3.67 million), Nebraska Beef ($1.92 million), and Wholestone Farms ($820,000). The presence of Tyson Foods on both sides of the ledger — as a major unsecured creditor and presumably a defendant in the antitrust claims — creates a governance dynamic worth tracking at confirmation. Creditors should determine whether the plan's release provisions sweep any antitrust defendants and, if so, whether that trade is being made on commercially reasonable terms or is simply a convenience for Clearlake-adjacent counterparties.

The charitable-donation provision is less doctrinally interesting but politically sensitive with the U.S. Trustee. Courts have permitted residual-fund charitable donations in prior cases where the amounts were de minimis and creditors were adequately compensated, but the U.S. Trustee's objection signals it views this as an impermissible distribution mechanism.

III. BlockFills / Reliz Technology Group Holdings — Post-Confirmation Posture (Bankr. D. Del. No. 26-10371, Judge Horan)

The tracked-case ground truth confirms six new docket entries in the 72 hours through July 29. The prior advisory reported that the Keyrock SA asset sale — a $3.25 million purchase of substantially all of BlockFills' technology assets and IP — cleared court approval, and that plan confirmation was targeted for July 13. The docket activity as of July 29 suggests the case continues to generate filings, but the nature of those entries is unverified in this run. We carry forward the prior advisory's substantive positions subject to that caveat.

What is verified: a Delaware bankruptcy judge approved the $3.25 million sale of BlockFills to Keyrock SA, a Belgian digital asset investment group, as BlockFills prepared for its Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.

Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume substantially all of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property.

The capital structure remains dominated by customer-creditor exposure: the capital structure is concentrated in customer and trade exposure rather than traditional funded debt, with roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and about $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.

The critical issue for creditors going forward is plan-economics: after a $3.25 million sale, customer claimants holding $145 million in aggregate general unsecured obligations face recovery rates in the low single-digit percentage range before any distribution from whatever the liquidating trust monetizes from residual assets and litigation recoveries. The opt-out release question flagged in the May 28 disclosure-statement order — where Judge Horan conditionally approved the disclosure statement but noted the opt-out release mechanism would be argued at confirmation — echoes the Harvest Sherwood fight and the broader post-Purdue landscape. IV. Structural Theme: Third-Party Releases in Liquidating Plans Post-Purdue

Three of the four most active cases on this desk — BlockFills, Harvest Sherwood, and the broader prepack wave now landing in Texas — share a common pressure point: post-Purdue release mechanics. Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P., 603 U.S. 204 (2024), eliminated non-consensual third-party releases in Chapter 11 plans but left significant ambiguity around what constitutes "consent" in an opt-out structure. The U.S. Trustee's office has operationalized that ambiguity as a standard confirmation objection in cases from Delaware to Texas. Courts are splitting.

The practical consequence for distressed investors: plans carrying opt-out releases are now pricing a meaningful confirmation-litigation premium. Plan negotiators in prepackaged cases are increasingly moving toward opt-in mechanics or narrowing the release universe to parties who have actually contributed value — the Sackler family payment structure being the implicit template — in order to reduce confirmation risk and litigation duration. The Alkegen prepack is the most relevant near-term data point: if it confirms without a release fight in 60 days, that will be evidence that adequately consented prepackaged releases can survive Texas court scrutiny post-Purdue.
