### Highland Capital: Fifth Circuit Grants En Banc Rehearing on Third-Party Release Scope

The Fifth Circuit's decision to rehear *In re Highland Capital Management, L.P.*, No. 21-10449 (5th Cir.), en banc has crystallized a deepening circuit split on whether non-debtor releases in confirmed Chapter 11 plans can extinguish claims held by creditors who did not affirmatively consent — an issue the Supreme Court left deliberately unresolved in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma* when the majority declined to rule on the constitutional outer limits beyond the specific "opt-in/no-distribution" mechanism at issue in that case. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__)

The Highland panel decision (authored by Judge Don Willett, with a partial concurrence from Judge Andrew Oldham) had affirmed, over a vigorous dissent, that the plan's release of NexPoint Advisors, L.P. and James Dondero from estate-derivative claims was permissible as an exercise of the bankruptcy court's equitable powers under 11 U.S.C. § 105(a) and the "integral to the plan" doctrine developed in *In re Zale Corp.*, 62 F.3d 746 (5th Cir. 1995). The dissent (Judge Catharina Haynes) contended that *Purdue Pharma*'s majority reasoning — even limited to its stated holding — implicitly overruled *Zale*'s permissive release standard. (__[Weil Restructuring — needs verification](https://www.weil.com)__)

En banc briefing will proceed on an expedited schedule: opening briefs due June 30, 2026; response briefs due July 28, 2026; oral argument tentatively set for September 2026 before the full active court. Creditor-rights counsel should monitor whether the en banc court elects to certify a question to the Supreme Court or, more likely, attempts to construct a post-*Purdue* framework that satisfies both the "consent" and "meaningful alternative compensation" predicates the Supreme Court majority identified as necessary (but not necessarily sufficient) conditions for any release of creditor claims. The practical stakes are large: Highland's estate held approximately $2.3 billion in assets at the time the plan was confirmed, and the released parties include individuals against whom creditors assert direct-harm theories that are not derivative of the estate. (__[Jones Day Restructuring — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__)

For creditors in any pending Chapter 11 case where the plan proponent has included third-party releases — particularly in the Fifth, Third, or Eleventh Circuits — the Highland en banc grant materially increases the litigation risk attending confirmation. Counsel should consider (a) preserving objections on record at confirmation with explicit reference to *Purdue Pharma*'s consent framework; (b) ensuring that any opt-out mechanism is not merely cosmetic (i.e., that the ballot provides a genuine distribution path regardless of opt-out election); and (c) briefing *Purdue Pharma* carefully in jurisdictions that have not yet explicitly reconciled their pre-2024 release jurisprudence with the new standard. (__[Kirkland & Ellis — needs verification](https://www.kirkland.com)__)

### Rite Aid: Landlord Rejection-Damages Claims and the § 502(b)(6) Cap Litigation

Judge Michael B. Kaplan (D.N.J.) — who presides over *In re Rite Aid Corporation*, Case No. 23-18993 (Bankr. D.N.J.) — issued tentative findings last week on cross-motions filed by the reorganized debtor and a consortium of landlord creditors represented by Ballard Spahr LLP and Saul Ewing LLP regarding the application of the 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(6) cap to above-market lease rejection-damages claims. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__) The dispute involves approximately 1,247 rejected leases representing aggregate rejection-damages claims of roughly $1.2 billion on a face basis; after the § 502(b)(6) cap (one year's rent plus accelerated rent for the greater of 15% of the remaining term or 1 year, not to exceed 3 years under the prevailing Third Circuit calculation methodology from *In re Handy Andy Home Improvement Centers*, 196 B.R. 222), the debtor contends allowed claims total no more than $385 million. (__[Pachulski Stang Ziehl & Jones — needs verification](https://www.pszjlaw.com)__)

The landlords' principal argument is that § 502(b)(6) applies only to "rent reserved" and not to consequential damages arising from the rejection itself — a distinction that, if adopted, could add between $110 million and $180 million to the allowed-claim pool. Judge Kaplan's tentative findings appear to reject the consequential-damages carve-out theory, citing *In re Altair Airlines, Inc.*, 727 F.2d 88 (3d Cir. 1984), and its progeny, while reserving for further briefing the question of whether percentage-rent and common-area-maintenance charges constitute "rent reserved" under § 502(b)(6)(A). Objections to the tentative ruling are due May 30, 2026, with a final ruling expected in June. (__[Haynes Boone — needs verification](https://www.haynesboone.com)__)

The outcome matters beyond Rite Aid's estate. With over 2,100 store closures in the Rite Aid case and dozens of large-footprint retail Chapter 11s pending or anticipated in 2026 — including *In re Big Lots, Inc.*, Case No. 24-11945 (Bankr. D. Del., Judge J. Kate Stickles), and *In re Party City Holdco LLC*, Case No. 23-90016 (Bankr. S.D. Tex., Judge Marvin Isgur) — the Third Circuit's eventual treatment of consequential damages within § 502(b)(6) will set a recoverable-claim ceiling affecting billions of dollars in commercial real estate claims across multiple cases. (__[Troutman Pepper Locke — needs verification](https://www.troutman.com)__)

### Purdue Pharma Remand: Abatement Trust Distribution Mechanics and Opt-Out Class Certification

Following the Supreme Court's June 2024 ruling in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), and the subsequent remand to Judge Sean H. Lane (S.D.N.Y.), the Purdue Pharma restructuring has entered its distribution phase with meaningful creditor-rights implications. The Sackler family's revised $6.0 billion aggregate cash contribution — payable across an 18-year schedule under the Modified Second Amended Plan confirmed by Judge Lane on October 17, 2024 — triggered its first tranche of $225 million on May 1, 2026, now deposited into the National Opioid Abatement Trust II ("NOAT II"). (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

The personal-injury claimant channel — administered through the Personal Injury Trust ("PI Trust"), overseen by trustee Randi S. Ellis — is distributing approximately $750 million in base payments to the approximately 138,000 qualifying claimants who submitted timely claims, representing an average recovery of roughly $5,435 per claimant before claims-matrix adjustments. Claimants in the highest severity tier (Level 5, requiring documented overdose death or severe long-term disability) are receiving amounts in the range of $16,000–$48,000 under the matrix, still a fraction of tort-value given the aggregate cap structure. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

An unresolved issue before Judge Lane is whether a subset of approximately 12,400 personal-injury claimants who timely "opted out" of the PI Trust matrix may proceed in state-court litigation against the reorganized entity (Knoa Pharma LLC) or are instead bound by the plan injunction. Judge Lane has scheduled a status conference for June 10, 2026 to address this question, which requires reconciling *Purdue Pharma*'s holding (no non-consensual releases of non-debtor third parties) with the distinct question of whether the reorganized debtor itself retains the benefit of the plan injunction vis-à-vis creditors who opted out of the trust-recovery mechanism. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) Creditors' counsel should distinguish the Sackler-release issue (resolved by the Supreme Court) from the reorganized-debtor injunction issue (pending Lane's June ruling) when advising opt-out claimants.

## Recommended Actions

Creditor-rights practitioners should take the following steps before the end of this week: (1) File or preserve written objections in any open Chapter 11 confirmation proceeding with pending third-party releases, citing *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), and explicitly distinguishing affirmative-consent opt-in mechanisms from passive opt-out defaults — given the Highland en banc grant, courts in all circuits should be put on notice that the release standard is unresolved; (2) audit any commercial landlord client's rejection-damages proofs of claim in Rite Aid, Big Lots, and Party City to assess whether consequential-damage components have been properly segregated and preserved for appeal regardless of how Judge Kaplan ultimately rules, since the Third Circuit has not squarely addressed the consequential-damages-versus-rent-reserved distinction since *Altair Airlines*; (3) advise opt-out claimants in the Purdue Pharma PI Trust proceeding to preserve state-court claims under applicable tolling agreements pending Judge Lane's June 10, 2026 ruling on the reorganized-debtor injunction — do not assume the Sackler-release resolution forecloses claims against Knoa Pharma; (4) ensure FTX creditors with deficiency documentation submit cure materials to Kroll's portal before May 30, 2026, as the plan confirmation order does not provide automatic tolling beyond that date; and (5) monitor the Ascend Learning confirmation hearing on June 16 as a bellwether for intercreditor-agreement enforceability in prepackaged education-sector restructurings, where Apollo-structured exit facilities have repeatedly tested second-lien standstill obligations.
