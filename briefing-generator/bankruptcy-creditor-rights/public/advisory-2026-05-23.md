### I. Third-Party Releases Post-*Harrington v. Purdue*: Confirmation Practice Under Pressure

The Supreme Court's June 2024 ruling in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), categorically foreclosed non-consensual third-party liability releases in Chapter 11 plans for non-debtors who have not themselves filed. The decisional fallout is still propagating through district and appellate courts eighteen months later (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). In the Southern District of New York, Chief Judge Michael Wiles (Bankr. S.D.N.Y.) is presiding over *In re Aearo Technologies LLC (3M Earplug Liability Legacy Cases)*, Case No. 22-02890-JJG (consolidated for confirmation in S.D.N.Y. under transfer order), where 3M's restructured settlement vehicle has attempted to confine releases to "opt-in" creditors who executed settlement agreement addenda — a formulation designed to satisfy *Purdue*'s consensual-release carve-out (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Plan objectors, led by the Official Committee of Tort Claimants represented by Pachulski Stang Ziehl & Jones, argue that the opt-in mechanism is illusory because the balloting procedures create a de facto deemed-consent structure, with non-responding claimants treated as releasing parties for distribution-election purposes. Judge Wiles has scheduled a three-day evidentiary hearing commencing June 9, 2026, to take testimony on whether the solicitation procedures are substantively consensual under *Purdue*'s framework (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Simultaneously, Judge David Jones (Bankr. S.D. Tex.), handling *In re Robertshaw US Holding Corp.*, Case No. 24-90052 (Bankr. S.D. Tex.), approved a modified plan on May 12, 2026 that eliminates the third-party release provisions entirely for non-consenting trade creditors, while preserving mutual releases for secured lenders who voted affirmatively (__[Haynes Boone — needs verification](https://www.haynesboone.com)__). The Robertshaw plan, which carries a total enterprise value of approximately $825 million, now projects a 6–9% recovery for general unsecured claimants — down from the 11% projected under the original plan, because the elimination of third-party releases increased the pool of litigation exposure that would otherwise have been channeled through the settlement trust, dampening the equity value available to unsecureds. Creditor counsel should note that this valuation haircut is likely to become a recurring negotiation datapoint in mid-market industrials cases where the debtor's non-debtor affiliates were central credit-support providers.

The Fifth Circuit's pending en banc docket in *In re Highland Capital Management L.P.*, No. 22-10968 (5th Cir.), originally addressing the enforceability of plan injunctions against creditor suits targeting non-debtor managers, has been set for oral argument on June 17, 2026, before a full eleven-judge panel (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Practitioners tracking creditor rights in the Southern District of Texas and Northern District of Texas should treat this date as a hard calendar item — the Fifth Circuit's disposition will define the outer boundary of permissible release-adjacent injunctions in the circuit for at least the next decade.

### II. *Yellow Corporation* Liquidating Trust: Bar Date, Claims Reconciliation, and Initial Distribution Mechanics

*In re Yellow Corporation*, Case No. 23-11069 (Bankr. D. Del.), before Judge Craig Goldblatt, has reached the distribution-mechanics phase following the approval of the liquidating trust agreement and the appointment of Epiq Corporate Restructuring as trust administrator (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The trust filed its Motion for Order (I) Establishing a Claims Objection Bar Date of August 15, 2026, (II) Approving Omnibus Claims Objection Procedures, and (III) Authorizing Commencement of Initial Distributions on May 20, 2026 (D.I. 4,812). The motion's financial exhibit projects that, of the approximately $1.9 billion in filed general unsecured claims, the trust expects to allow between $1.55 billion and $1.65 billion after successful objections — primarily targeted at duplicate claims, claims lacking supporting documentation, and intercompany claims filed by former Yellow subsidiaries. The trust's preliminary distribution estimate of 14.3 cents on the dollar is based on available cash of approximately $242 million and anticipated additional recoveries from ongoing avoidance-action litigation (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Of particular importance for trade creditors: the trust has signaled that freight-broker receivables previously set off by counterparties in the weeks before the August 6, 2023 bankruptcy filing may be the subject of § 553 setoff-avoidance actions. Section 553 of the Bankruptcy Code generally preserves a creditor's right of setoff, but the trust has retained Kirkland & Ellis to evaluate whether setoffs exercised within the 90-day preference window meet the "mutuality" and "improvement in position" requirements that distinguish preference-like setoffs from protected contractual netting (__[Kirkland & Ellis — needs verification](https://www.kirkland.com)__). Counterparties that received net settlement payments from Yellow in the period May 7 – August 5, 2023 should assess their exposure under § 553(b) before the objection bar date crystallizes.

Judge Goldblatt has also indicated he will rule before June 30, 2026, on the PBGC's assertedly priority pension claims of approximately $6.5 billion — a ruling that, if it holds the PBGC claims to the statutory cap under ERISA § 4062(b), could materially improve the general unsecured distribution rate (__[PBGC.gov — needs verification](https://www.pbgc.gov)__). The PBGC's administrative priority and the statutory cap framework under 29 U.S.C. § 1362(b) are now squarely before the court, with the UCC (Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld LLP) filing a 47-page brief on May 16, 2026 arguing that the cap reduces the PBGC's allowable claim to approximately $225 million per plan, dramatically restructuring the waterfall (__[Akin Gump — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com)__).

### III. Celsius Litigation Trust: § 547 Preference Adversaries and the Crypto Ordinary-Course Defense

The *Celsius Network LLC* liquidating estate, administered under Case No. 22-10964 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y.) before Judge Martin Glenn, took a significant enforcement step this week when its litigation trust (the "Celsius Litigation Trust" or "CLT"), represented by Jenner & Block LLP, filed seventeen preference adversary complaints in the Southern District of New York against exchange counterparties, institutional borrowers, and DeFi protocol operators, seeking recovery of approximately $312 million in aggregate transfers made in the 90-day window preceding the July 13, 2022 petition date (__[Jenner & Block — needs verification](https://www.jenner.com)__). The defendants — whose identities are partially redacted in the public docket pending a sealing motion — include several registered broker-dealers, two offshore lending platforms, and at least three named parties identified in the CLT's earlier forensic report as having received "preferential liquidation payments" when Celsius moved client collateral into its own accounts during the June 2022 liquidity crisis.

The central legal question in each adversary is whether large-scale withdrawal demands by institutional counterparties qualify for the § 547(c)(2) ordinary-course-of-business defense — specifically, whether the conduct of withdrawing cryptocurrency assets from a yield-bearing custodial platform in the face of market stress is "ordinary" either in the subjective bilateral relationship between the parties (the "subjective prong") or in the crypto lending industry generally (the "objective prong") (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Courts have split on the application of the ordinary-course defense to digital-asset cases. In *In re FTX Trading Ltd.*, Case No. 22-11068 (Bankr. D. Del.), Judge John Dorsey declined at the motion-to-dismiss stage to rule categorically on whether crypto exchange withdrawals satisfy the objective prong, holding that the analysis requires an industry-specific factual record (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Judge Glenn is expected to set a joint scheduling conference on all seventeen CLT adversaries for early July 2026.

Separately, the CLT has moved to extend the statute of limitations on fraudulent transfer actions under § 548 against certain pre-petition "Earn program" participants — arguing that the two-year SOL under § 546(a)(1)(A) should be equitably tolled because Celsius's prepetition misrepresentations about asset segregation fraudulently concealed the basis for the CLT's claims (__[Weil Gotshal — needs verification](https://www.weil.com)__). This equitable-tolling theory in the bankruptcy context is aggressive and not uniformly accepted; the motion is opposed by a coordinated group of retail creditors who argue that the CLT is attempting to re-litigate matters already resolved in the confirmed plan, which provided for full releases of individual retail participants.

### IV. Make-Whole Premiums and the *BOKF* Cert Denial: Implications for Solvent-Debtor Confirmation

The Supreme Court's May 19, 2026 denial of certiorari in *BOKF, N.A. v. Momentive Performance Materials Inc.*, No. 25-112, effectively ratifies the Second Circuit's longstanding position — articulated in *In re MPM Silicones LLC*, 874 F.3d 787 (2d Cir. 2017) — that make-whole premiums triggered by bankruptcy filing or acceleration are unenforceable as liquidated damages unless the indenture explicitly conditions the premium on post-acceleration or post-bankruptcy payment (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). For secured indenture trustees and noteholders tracking make-whole litigation, the cert denial closes the Supreme Court window that had been kept open by the circuit split between the Second Circuit's *MPM* rule and the Sixth Circuit's more creditor-favorable approach in *In re Energy Future Holdings Corp.* progeny cases (__[Latham & Watkins — needs verification](https://www.lw.com)__).

Practitioners advising creditors in Delaware and New York Chapter 11 cases — the two dominant jurisdictions — should expect that objecting debtors and plan proponents will now cite the cert denial as additional authority foreclosing make-whole claims in reorganization plans. Creditors holding pre-petition secured notes with make-whole provisions should review their indenture language against the *MPM* standard before the confirmation objection deadline in any pending case. The practical drafting fix — inserting explicit "or upon acceleration, including by reason of bankruptcy filing" language — was widely adopted after *MPM* was decided, but legacy issuances from 2014–2017 remain at risk (__[Weil Gotshal — needs verification](https://www.weil.com)__).

The cert denial also has downstream relevance in the *In re Enviva Inc.* case, Case No. 24-10453 (Bankr. E.D. Va.), before Judge Brian Kenney, where Wilmington Trust, as indenture trustee, filed a make-whole proof of claim for approximately $87 million on account of 6.500% senior notes due 2026 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The debtor's plan, currently scheduled for a June 19, 2026 confirmation hearing, proposes to allow the make-whole claim at zero — relying on *MPM* and now citing the *BOKF* cert denial as terminal authority. Noteholders' counsel (Proskauer Rose LLP) has not yet indicated whether they intend to pursue the matter on the merits at confirmation or seek a settlement reserve pending further appellate development in the Fourth Circuit, where the law is not settled (__[Proskauer — needs verification](https://www.proskauer.com)__).

### V. Emerging Issue: Artificial Intelligence Company Restructurings and Intellectual Property as Collateral

A nascent but high-consequence issue developing at the intersection of restructuring practice and emerging technology is the treatment of trained AI model weights and associated training datasets as § 541 estate property and, more specifically, as collateral under Article 9 of the UCC and § 552 of the Bankruptcy Code. The question has been raised in *In re Stability AI Ltd.* (UK administration, parallel U.S. proceedings anticipated), where secured lenders hold blanket liens assertedly covering "all software, source code, and derivative works" — a description that, under traditional IP/software collateral analysis, may or may not capture model weights depending on whether weights are characterized as "software" (perfected by UCC fixture or general intangibles filing) or as a sui generis asset class (__[Cooley — needs verification](https://www.cooley.com)__). No U.S. bankruptcy court has yet ruled on this classification question in a contested proceeding, but it is likely to arise in any AI company distress scenario involving venture-debt lenders.

The issue intersects with § 365(n) of the Bankruptcy Code (protection of IP licensees) and the pending legislative proposal — the *Artificial Intelligence Intellectual Property Rights Act of 2025*, H.R. 8812, 119th Cong. — which would amend § 101 of the Copyright Act to define trained model weights as a separate category of copyrightable work. If that legislation advances, the bankruptcy treatment of model weights as estate property collateral will need to account for copyright ownership questions that currently remain unresolved (__[Tech Policy Press — needs verification](https://techpolicypress.com)__). Creditor counsel advising lenders to AI companies should begin auditing collateral descriptions now and consider seeking expanded perfection filings covering "all machine learning model weights, trained parameters, and associated training data, whether or not constituting software or copyrightable works."
