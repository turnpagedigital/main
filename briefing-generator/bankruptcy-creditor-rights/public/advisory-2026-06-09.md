### Del Monte Foods: Chapter 11 Plan Confirmation and ABC Law Leverage

Del Monte Foods, Inc. (Bankr. D. Del., Case No. 25-10065, Judge Thomas M. Horan) filed its Chapter 11 petition on January 14, 2025, listing approximately $1.1 billion in secured debt against a going-concern asset base that debtors' financial advisor Houlihan Lokey valued at a midpoint of $740 million — a structural impairment producing a fulcrum-security position held by the prepetition term-loan lenders (the "Ad Hoc Term Lender Group," represented by Milbank LLP) (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The reorganization plan filed on or about May 12, 2026, proposes to (a) equitize approximately $860 million of the term-loan tranche, leaving residual secured claims of roughly $240 million at emergence; (b) provide a 3.2% recovery to general unsecured creditors on allowed claims through a $6.4 million GUC pool distributed by a post-effective-date liquidating trust; and (c) release all prepetition directors and officers in exchange for a $22 million D&O insurance contribution (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The ABC Law dimension — California's Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors statute, Cal. Code Civ. Proc. §§ 493.010–493.060 — is generating collateral tension because Del Monte's California subsidiary operations, including its Modesto processing facilities, had initially been candidates for an out-of-court ABC proceeding before the board elected the federal Chapter 11 route. At least two trade creditors have filed objections (Docket Nos. 1,187 and 1,193) arguing that if the Delaware court confirms the plan over their dissent, the substantive consolidation of California subsidiary assets — valued by objectors' expert at $48 million independently — is being used to dilute their claims in a manner that would have been prohibited in a California ABC proceeding where each assignor estate is administered separately (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Judge Horan has scheduled a preliminary confirmation hearing for June 24, 2026, with objection briefs due June 16. Creditors holding trade claims against Del Monte's California subsidiaries should file proof-of-claim supplements and coordinate with proposed objectors' counsel before the June 16 deadline, as the substantive consolidation challenge, if sustained, could meaningfully improve recoveries for subsidiary-level unsecured creditors relative to the consolidated 3.2% pool.

### *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*: Third-Party Release Architecture at 18 Months Post-Decision

Eighteen months after the Supreme Court's June 27, 2025, decision in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. ___, 145 S. Ct. 2071 (2025), holding 5-4 per Justice Neil Gorsuch that the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize a Chapter 11 plan to discharge the civil liability of non-debtor third parties who have not filed for bankruptcy themselves, the restructuring bar is still absorbing the downstream consequences (__[Scotusblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/case-files/cases/harrington-v-purdue-pharma-l-p/)__). The majority's textual analysis rested principally on 11 U.S.C. § 524(e) — which limits discharge to "a debt of the debtor" — and the absence of any affirmative statutory authorization for non-consensual non-debtor releases comparable to the specific carve-outs for asbestos trusts under § 524(g) (__[SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com)__).

The cases most immediately affected as of today's session include:

**Boy Scouts of America** (Bankr. D. Del., Case No. 20-10343, Judge Laurie Selber Silverstein): The BSA's confirmed plan, which became effective February 2023, included broad releases of the Local Councils and chartered organizations, including the Roman Catholic Diocese defendants. Post-*Purdue* litigation has reopened in at least two District Court cases where survivor claimants argue the § 524(e) holding voids, or at minimum renders unenforceable, the non-consensual injunctive protections extended to the non-debtor sponsoring organizations (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The Third Circuit heard oral argument on this collateral-attack question on April 29, 2026 (Appeal No. 26-1142), with a decision expected by August 2026.

**Johnson & Johnson / LTL Management** (Bankr. D.N.J., Case Nos. 21-30589 and 23-12825, Judge Michael B. Kaplan): J&J's third divisional-merger ("Texas Two-Step") gambit remains in a holding pattern. Following the Third Circuit's October 2023 dismissal and the Supreme Court's denial of certiorari in January 2024, J&J pivoted to a direct settlement framework outside of bankruptcy. As of today, J&J has disclosed a $6.475 billion global resolution fund for talc claimants, administered outside the Bankruptcy Code, and has voluntarily dismissed LTL Management's third bankruptcy petition. However, holdout claimants — particularly mesothelioma plaintiffs represented by Maune Raichle Hartley French & Mudd — continue to litigate in New Jersey state court, and any federal preemption arguments J&J might have raised in a confirmed plan are no longer available, a direct *Purdue* consequence (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

**SV Holdco (Serta Simmons Bedding)** (Bankr. S.D. Tex., Case No. 23-90020, Judge David R. Jones): Serta's confirmed plan included non-debtor releases of Advent International and certain management defendants. Post-*Purdue*, an ad hoc group of excluded lenders (the so-called "Non-Participating Lenders") has filed a motion in the S.D. Tex. district court for reconsideration under Fed. R. Civ. P. 60(b)(5) and (6), arguing that *Purdue*'s § 524(e) holding constitutes an intervening change in law requiring unwinding of the third-party release provisions (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The underlying "uptiering" dispute — where Serta's prepetition credit agreement was amended to prime approximately $1.9 billion of term loans held by the excluded lenders — remains the subject of parallel state-law litigation in New York Supreme Court (Index No. 651721/2022, Justice Margaret Chan).

### Yellow Corporation: Administrative Expense Priority Disputes and Liquidating Trust Distributions

Yellow Corporation and its operating subsidiaries (Bankr. D. Del., Case Nos. 23-11069 et seq., Chief Judge Craig T. Goldblatt) entered confirmation of their Chapter 11 liquidating plan in August 2024, with the Yellow Creditor Recovery Trust ("YCRT") becoming the distribution vehicle for approximately $1.1 billion in estimated net asset proceeds (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The YCRT is administered by Kurtzman Carson Consultants LLC and is pursuing, among other things: (a) the $137 million preference recovery action against the International Brotherhood of Teamsters (Adversary Proc. No. 24-50233); (b) approximately $250 million in insurance recovery claims arising from the 2023 cyberattack on Yellow's operational systems; and (c) real-estate sale proceeds from Yellow's 169-terminal portfolio, several of which closed below appraisal value.

The current litigation flashpoint is an administrative-expense priority dispute under 11 U.S.C. § 503(b)(1) filed by a consortium of former employees (Docket No. 4,892) seeking priority treatment for WARN Act claims arising from the mass layoff of approximately 30,000 drivers and dock workers when Yellow ceased operations in August 2023. The WARN claims were initially allowed only as general unsecured claims, generating a projected recovery of 6–8 cents on the dollar against an allowed unsecured claims pool of approximately $1.7 billion. The employees argue that post-petition wages earned during the brief operational-wind-down period between the petition date (August 6, 2023) and the final cessation of freight operations (August 11, 2023) are entitled to § 503(b)(1)(A) administrative priority. Judge Goldblatt held oral argument on June 3, 2026, and took the matter under submission; a ruling is expected before July 1, 2026. If the court grants administrative priority to even a portion of the WARN claims, the waterfall impact on unsecured recoveries could be material — the administrative estate currently holds approximately $86 million in unreserved liquid assets against which additional § 503(b) claimants would share pro rata (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

### Rite Aid: Second-Year Plan Distributions and Creditor Trust Mechanics

Rite Aid Corporation (Bankr. D.N.J., Case No. 23-18993, Judge Michael B. Kaplan) confirmed its Chapter 11 plan in August 2024 and has been making distributions through the Rite Aid Creditor Trust since Q4 2024. As of the trust's April 2026 quarterly report, aggregate distributions to allowed general unsecured creditors total approximately $342 million on allowed GUC claims of roughly $3.9 billion — a blended recovery to date of approximately 8.8 cents on the dollar against the trust's projected terminal recovery of 12–15 cents (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The trust's principal remaining assets include: (a) litigation against former directors and officers (D&O), with a trial date in the D.N.J. district court set for February 2027; (b) the pharmaceutical intellectual property portfolio licensed to the acquiring entity (a consortium including Albertsons and McKesson Specialty Health), from which royalty streams of approximately $18 million annually are flowing into the trust; and (c) real-estate lease rejection claims being litigated against commercial landlords in approximately 40 ongoing adversary proceedings.

Opioid-related claimants, who filed proofs of claim aggregating approximately $600 million, are receiving zero recovery under the confirmed plan's classification scheme — those claims were separately classified and allocated a $0 recovery consistent with the Rite Aid board's litigation position that opioid derivative claimants lack a legally cognizable claim against the pharmacy-operator entity as distinct from the distributor and manufacturer defendants. Post-*Purdue*, however, at least three state attorneys general have filed motions to modify the confirmed plan (Docket Nos. 8,211, 8,214, and 8,219) arguing that the non-debtor release provisions protecting Rite Aid's former pharmacy directors are unenforceable and that state parens patriae claims should be allowed to proceed (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Judge Kaplan has requested supplemental briefing on the *Purdue* § 524(e) issue due June 23, 2026.

### DIP Financing Roll-Up Scrutiny: U.S. Trustee Program Guidelines Update

The U.S. Trustee Program issued updated Guidelines for Reviewing Applications for Compensation and Reimbursement of Expenses Filed Under 11 U.S.C. § 330 in March 2026, but the more operationally significant guidance from the USTP's perspective has been its position papers filed in a series of large Chapter 11 cases opposing so-called "roll-up" DIP financing arrangements — where prepetition secured lenders' existing loans are "rolled" into the DIP facility and thereby elevated to DIP superpriority status under § 364(c) and (d) (__[U.S. Trustee Program — needs verification](https://www.justice.gov/ust)__). The USTP's standard objection template, first deployed in *Envision Healthcare* (Bankr. S.D. Tex.) and now appearing in modified form in at least eight cases in 2025–2026, argues that roll-up DIP provisions (a) improperly convert prepetition claims to post-petition super-priority claims without adequate § 506(c) surcharge rights being preserved; (b) chill competitive DIP bidding by entrenching the prepetition lender group; and (c) effectively prejudge plan distributions by constraining the debtor-in-possession's use of cash collateral. Courts in the S.D. Tex. (Judge Isgur and Judge Jones) and D. Del. (Judge Dorsey) have taken divergent approaches, with the S.D. Tex. courts more permissive of roll-up structures where the ratio of new money to rolled-up debt exceeds 1:3 (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

## Recommended Actions

Creditors, claims traders, and recovery counsel should prioritize the following immediate steps: (1) in the Del Monte Foods proceeding (Bankr. D. Del. No. 25-10065), all trade creditors with claims against California subsidiary entities should audit their proofs of claim for subsidiary-level attribution before June 16, 2026 and file substantive consolidation objections aligned with the Docket No. 1,187/1,193 objectors' theories, as a successful challenge could bifurcate the GUC pool and improve subsidiary-creditor recoveries from the current 3.2% blended figure; (2) unsecured creditors in any pending plan case containing non-consensual non-debtor releases should immediately assess *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*, 145 S. Ct. 2071 (2025), as grounds for plan objection or, in confirmed cases, Rule 60(b)(5)/(6) motions, particularly where the release beneficiaries are control persons who retain equity value post-emergence; (3) WARN Act claimants in the Yellow Corporation liquidation (Bankr. D. Del. No. 23-11069 et seq.) should monitor Judge Goldblatt's forthcoming ruling on the § 503(b)(1) priority question and be prepared to file supplemental election forms through the YCRT claims portal within 30 days of the ruling; (4) Rite Aid GUC holders should track the June 23, 2026, supplemental briefing deadline on the opioid claimant release challenge, as any modification of the non-debtor release provisions under *Purdue* could require a new solicitation and potentially delay or reduce Q3 2026 trust distributions; and (5) any creditor committee or sophisticated creditor involved in a debtor seeking DIP financing should analyze the new money-to-roll-up ratio against the USTP's evolving 1:3 guideline threshold and position objections early in the first-day hearing sequence, as post-interim-order challenges to roll-up structures face near-insurmountable equitable mootness arguments once the DIP facility is drawn and operational.
