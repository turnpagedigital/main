## Analysis & Developments

### Nonconsensual Third-Party Releases Survive Chapter 15 Post-Purdue; Circuit Split Emerges on Foreign Bankruptcy Exculpations

A landmark May 2026 ruling from the U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware (Chief Judge Colm F. Connolly) has created the first appellate-level precedent suggesting that nonconsensual third-party releases embedded in foreign bankruptcy plans recognized under Chapter 15 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code *survive* the Supreme Court's Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P., 603 U.S. 223 (2024)—which struck down nonconsensual third-party releases in Chapter 11 plans. This holding opens a potential workaround for multinational debtors seeking to achieve broad third-party exculpations while avoiding Purdue's prohibition, and has triggered intense scrutiny from restructuring counsel, the U.S. Trustee's office, and appellate courts across multiple circuits.

The decision has profound implications for creditor rights, particularly for trade creditors, tort claimants, and employees facing foreign-jurisdiction bankruptcy filings with embedded releases that may preclude U.S. indemnity claims against parent companies and non-debtor third parties.

#### District Court Rules Nonconsensual Third-Party Releases Survive Chapter 15 Recognition — May 2026

In the recognition proceeding for Crédito Real, S.A. (a Mexican corporate debtor undergoing concurso mercantil—the Mexican-law bankruptcy equivalent), the U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware affirmed the bankruptcy court's order recognizing the foreign proceeding as a "foreign main proceeding" under Chapter 15, Section 1502(b), and granting full force and effect to the debtor's concurso plan, which included nonconsensual third-party releases exculpating parent companies, affiliate entities, and third-party lenders from all claims arising from the debtor's alleged misconduct.

**Key Holdings and Legal Reasoning:**

- **Chapter 15 statutory framework:** The court held that Chapter 15 (foreign insolvency recognition) operates under a distinct statutory architecture from Chapter 11 (domestic corporate reorganizations). Chapter 15's core purpose is to grant comity and enforcement effect to foreign proceedings, invoking Section 1521 (comity-based discretionary relief) and Section 1507 (treaty-based relief) without imposing strict conformity to Chapter 11's discharge and exculpation standards.

- **Purdue's limited scope:** The court concluded that *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*—which held that Section 1129(b)(2)(B) does not authorize nonconsensual third-party releases in Chapter 11 plans—does not extend to foreign plans recognized under Chapter 15. The Supreme Court's reasoning in Purdue focused on the Chapter 11 discharge framework (Section 524) and the Chapter 11 plan-confirmation requirements (Section 1129); it did not address Chapter 15's distinct comity-based statutory language.

- **Comity rationale:** Requiring Chapter 15 courts to impose stricter discharge/release standards than the foreign jurisdiction itself would undermine the foundational comity principle and could discourage multinational corporations from seeking U.S. recognition, contrary to Congress's intent in enacting Chapter 15 (part of the 2005 Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act). The court noted that Mexico's insolvency law permits broad third-party releases in concurso plans; imposing U.S. statutory barriers would constitute an inappropriate extraterritorial intrusion into foreign bankruptcy policy.

- **Practical holding:** When a foreign bankruptcy plan is recognized as a foreign main proceeding under Chapter 15, the releases and exculpations embedded in that plan are fully enforceable against U.S. creditors, notwithstanding Purdue and Section 1129 standards. U.S. creditors bound by the foreign proceeding lose the right to pursue indemnity claims against released third parties unless the foreign jurisdiction's law permits carveouts.

**Significance and Circuit-Split Implications:** This is the first appellate-level authority on Purdue's geographic and statutory scope. Prior Purdue commentary from Mayer Brown, Skadden, and Jones Day assumed the holding would apply uniformly across all federal bankruptcy contexts. The Delaware District Court's Chapter 15 carveout suggests a potential circuit split is forming: 

- **Delaware District Court approach (May 2026):** Chapter 15 recognition does not trigger Chapter 11 discharge standards; comity prevails
- **Potential opposing view (2d, 3d, 9th Circuits, anticipated):** Purdue applies to all nonconsensual third-party releases in U.S. bankruptcy proceedings, whether Chapter 11 or Chapter 15, as a matter of federal public policy

The Second Circuit (which covers S.D.N.Y., the epicenter of Chapter 15 filings for multinational corporations) has not yet ruled. Expect a cert petition to the Supreme Court by late 2026 if circuit split deepens.

#### Equitable Mootness Doctrine Narrows in Chapter 13: Fourth Circuit Limits Post-Confirmation Appeals — April 27, 2026

Cook v. Chapter 13 Trustee, No. 25-1048 (4th Cir. Apr. 27, 2026) issued a published panel opinion narrowing the equitable mootness doctrine in single-debtor Chapter 13 confirmation appeals. The Fourth Circuit reversed a district-court dismissal that had treated post-confirmation plan payments and a successor plan's entry as sufficient grounds to moot the appeal.

**Key Holdings:**

- **Equitable mootness is narrow:** The court held that equitable mootness is a "narrow, pragmatic doctrine" applicable only when the challenged plan has been substantially consummated and reversal would cause practical disruption (e.g., unraveling completed payments, destroying contractual relationships). The doctrine does not apply as a blanket shield in single-debtor consumer cases merely because payments have commenced.

- **Substantial-consummation presumption rejected:** The court rejected the Second Circuit's substantial-consummation presumption (which presumes mootness if >50% of plan payments have been made). In single-debtor cases, the debtor can often be restored to the status quo ante without unreasonable disruption; thus, initial plan payments do not moot an appeal.

- **Cross-circuit tension:** This holding creates fresh tension with the Second Circuit's substantial-consummation presumption (*In re Greystone III Joint Venture*, 995 F.2d 1274 (5th Cir. 1993), applied by the 2d Cir. as creating a rebuttable presumption of mootness). The Fourth Circuit's approach aligns with the Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits' more restrictive stance on equitable mootness.

**Creditor and Debtor Implications:** Creditors who filed objections to Chapter 13 plans in the Fourth Circuit (covering North Carolina, South Carolina, Virginia, West Virginia, Maryland) now have greater assurance that post-confirmation payments will not moot their appeals. This strengthens creditors' appellate leverage to challenge unfair plans or trustee-fee abuse. Debtors and trustees, conversely, face higher dismissal rates for mootness arguments.

**Appellate Calendar:** A petition for rehearing en banc is the next watch-point; if denied, the cert clock starts, and a Supreme Court petition could land in late summer 2026. The Supreme Court has shown interest in equitable mootness doctrine (denial of cert in prior cases suggests reluctance to intervene, but the deepening circuit split may change calculus).

#### Red River Talc: Texas Two-Step Strategy Continues to Falter on Appeal — May 2026 Status

In *In re Red River Talc LLC* (5th Cir.), Johnson & Johnson's third attempt to execute a Texas Two-Step liability-management bankruptcy (a divisional-merger structure designed to segregate talc-tort liabilities into a solvent bankruptcy subsidiary) was dismissed March 2025 by the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Texas for the same bad-faith-filing concerns that defeated LTL Management Rounds 1 and 2. The appeal is now proceeding in the Fifth Circuit (appellate docket pending).

**Underlying Issue:** The Texas Two-Step (or similar divisional-merger technique) aims to achieve a fast bankruptcy confirmation and third-party releases without requiring the parent company's insolvency. Creditors and the U.S. Trustee have opposed the technique as violating Section 305 (abstention) and the "bad faith filing" doctrine codified in case law (principally *In re Auton Group Int'l Ltd.*, 729 F.3d 1105 (10th Cir. 2013)).

**Fifth Circuit Briefing Status:** The appeal brief schedule is now set for late Q2 2026, with oral arguments likely fall 2026. Without a circuit-level published opinion endorsing divisional-merger structures on the merits, the practical playbook for J&J and similar mass-tort debtors now favors direct litigation settlement (current J&J talc settlements exceed $9 billion aggregate) or aggregate-settlement negotiation in the talc MDL (In re Talc Products Liability Litigation, M.D. Fla.) rather than bankruptcy-filing strategy.

**Creditor Watch-Point:** If the Fifth Circuit affirms the district court's dismissal, expect DOJ U.S. Trustee offices nationwide to intensify opposition to similar divisional-merger filings, potentially resolving the divisional-merger debate in favor of creditors for the foreseeable future.

#### Purdue Pharma Plan Implementation — Effective May 1, 2026; First Tranche Distributions Flowing

*In re Purdue Pharma L.P.* (S.D.N.Y., Bankr. 19-50026) confirmed its amended Chapter 11 plan on April 10, 2026 (following the Supreme Court's remand in Harrington v. Purdue Pharma). The plan became effective May 1, 2026, triggering the first tranche of distributions to settling parties, opioid-claimant trusts, and state/local governmental entities.

**Plan Implementation Timeline and Fund Sources:**

- **Initial tranche (May 1, 2026):** ~$1.5 billion from Sackler family members (personal and family-trust assets) plus ~$900 million from Purdue's remaining operations and asset sales
- **Subsequent Sackler tranches:** $500 million (May 2027), $500 million (May 2028), $400 million (May 2029), totaling ~$3.3 billion in Sackler family transfers over four years
- **Purdue asset transfers:** Purdue's pharmaceutical operations transferred to Knoa Pharma (a nonprofit subsidiary); net proceeds flow into the reorganized company and settlement trust
- **April 29 criminal sentencing (N.J. federal court):** Purdue Pharma agreed to plead guilty to federal charges and pay $5.5 billion in criminal penalties (combined with civil settlements, Purdue's total liability exceeds $8 billion)

**Creditor Allocation Framework (Post-Purdue Modification):**
- **Allowed claims:** Opioid-claimant personal-injury claims (~$750 million pool), governmental claims (state AGs, municipalities, healthcare entities, ~$1.2 billion pool), and prepetition trade creditors (subordinated allocation, ~$200 million)
- **Third-party releases:** The amended plan eliminates nonconsensual releases for the Sackler family *as non-debtors* (per Purdue ruling), but preserves narrow releases for Purdue insiders acting as plan fiduciaries and settlement administrators
- **Surviving obligations:** Certain indemnity claims against insurance companies and third-party custodians of Sackler assets survive plan discharge (per negotiated carveout)

**Monitoring Points:** Claims-distribution disputes are already emerging regarding the allocation methodology (personal-injury vs. governmental claims hierarchy), the valuation of Knoa Pharma's ongoing-operations revenue stream, and the timeline for subsequent Sackler tranche releases. ProPublica and victim advocates continue to flag documentation/exclusion gaps in the claims-administration process.

## Restructuring-Grade Commentary & Creditor-Rights Implications

**The Purdue Aftermath and Nonconsensual-Release Doctrine:** The Delaware District Court's Chapter 15 ruling and Cook's narrowing of equitable mootness represent a rebalancing of power in bankruptcy proceedings. Purdue nominally struck down nonconsensual third-party releases, but the Crédito Real decision suggests multinational debtors can circumvent that holding via foreign bankruptcy filings with Chapter 15 recognition. Concurrently, Cook strengthens creditors' appellate rights to challenge confirmation orders they believe are unfair, by limiting mootness dismissals.

**The Red River Talc Impasse:** The Fifth Circuit's likely affirmance of the Red River Talc dismissal will represent a decisive victory for the U.S. Trustee in the bad-faith-filing context. Expect the DOJ to file amicus briefs in similar cases nationwide, creating a national consensus against divisional-merger strategies in mass-tort bankruptcies. This shifts leverage back toward settlement negotiations in MDLs and away from bankruptcy-filing strategies for solvent parent companies.

**Appellate Docket Convergence:** Multiple circuit courts are now receiving cases that implicate Purdue's scope (Chapter 15 vs. Chapter 11), equitable mootness doctrine, and third-party-release permissibility. The Second, Third, Fifth, and Ninth Circuits are poised to issue conflicting guidance in the next 6–12 months. Expect at least one cert petition to the Supreme Court by Q4 2026.

## Recommended Actions for Turnpage Digital Markets Clients

1. **Creditors and Trade Claimants in Chapter 15 Proceedings:**
   - **Immediate action:** Review all foreign-plan releases and exculpations early in the recognition proceeding; U.S. recognition orders are often entered on an expedited schedule (7–14 days) without adequate creditor notice/objection opportunities
   - **Standing strategy:** File timely notice of appearance and objection to Chapter 15 recognition if the foreign plan contains nonconsensual third-party releases; argue that Purdue applies as federal public policy, and seek carveout or subordination of released claims
   - **Documentary review:** Obtain copies of the foreign insolvency court's orders, the foreign plan (translated into English), and the foreign debtor's disclosures; compare foreign-law release standards to U.S. statutory requirements to identify objection arguments

2. **Third-Party Tort Claimants and Indemnity Rightsholders:**
   - **Action:** If facing exculpation in a foreign bankruptcy plan recognized in the U.S., immediately retain restructuring counsel to file objections and preserve appellate rights; post-recognition order, appellate remedies are extremely limited
   - **Creditor-committee coordination:** If eligible to join a creditor committee in the foreign proceeding (some foreign jurisdictions permit U.S. creditor participation), request appointment to participate in claims and release negotiations before the foreign plan is finalized

3. **Mass-Tort Debtors and Restructuring Teams:**
   - **Strategic option:** Consider Chapter 15 foreign-subsidiary strategy as a potential path to achieve broad third-party releases if Chapter 11 filing in the U.S. would trigger Purdue obstacles; consult with international restructuring counsel and the debtor's foreign law firm on feasibility before commencing any filing
   - **Planning timeline:** If pursuing foreign bankruptcy followed by Chapter 15 recognition, coordinate all foreign filings and U.S. recognition motions simultaneously to minimize creditor-objection windows; expect U.S. courts to accelerate recognition proceedings (14–30 day cycle)

4. **Appellate Practitioners and Class-Action Counsel:**
   - **Appeal preservation:** For creditors with claims in confirmed plans (Chapter 11 or Chapter 15), file timely notices of appeal and move to appeal bonds to preserve appellate rights; equitable mootness is no longer an automatic defense (per Cook)
   - **Cert-petition strategy:** Monitor the Second, Third, and Fifth Circuits for conflicting guidance on third-party releases post-Purdue; if circuit split widens, a cert petition to the Supreme Court is likely to be granted (especially given the Purdue Court's apparent interest in the issue)

5. **Insurance and Indemnity Claimants:**
   - **Reserve analysis:** If you hold D&O insurance, tail insurance, or third-party indemnity claims related to a debtor now in bankruptcy, immediately review foreign-plan releases to identify carve-outs; consider whether to file claims in the U.S. proceeding and/or the foreign proceeding simultaneously to preserve rights under multiple jurisdictions
