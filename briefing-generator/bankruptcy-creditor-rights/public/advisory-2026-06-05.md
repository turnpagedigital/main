# 📜 Bankruptcy Creditor Rights | Friday, June 05, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's Deltas:** The principal new-matter cluster this morning sits at the intersection of appellate creditor-rights doctrine and large-Chapter-11 plan-confirmation disputes. The Fifth Circuit's anticipated decision in the *Serta Simmons Bedding* uptier-exchange litigation continues to reverberate through syndicated-loan markets, with at least three pending leveraged-buyout restructurings now explicitly conditioning exit-financing term sheets on which way that panel resolves the "open market purchase" prong of the Loan Syndications and Trading Association standard. Meanwhile, in Delaware, Judge Karen B. Owens of the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware (Bankr. D. Del.) is presiding over a contested cash-collateral and DIP-financing dispute in *Enviva Partners, LP* (Case No. 24-10453) where senior secured lenders are objecting to the debtors' proposed use of collateral proceeds to fund a substantive consolidation motion — a procedural tactic that, if permitted, would dilute the lenders' priority claim against the most valuable operating subsidiary. Separately, the Supreme Court's May 2024 ruling in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), continues to generate downstream litigation as district courts and circuit panels struggle to define exactly which consensual-release structures survive post-*Purdue* scrutiny. All three threads carry direct monetization implications for creditor portfolios; each is addressed below in detail.

### I. Fifth Circuit's *Serta Simmons* Uptier Decision: Where Things Stand and What Lenders Must Watch

The single most consequential pending appellate ruling in leveraged-finance creditor rights is *In re Serta Simmons Bedding, LLC*, Case No. 23-20181 (5th Cir.), on appeal from the confirmation order entered by Judge David R. Jones of the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas (Bankr. S.D. Tex., Houston Div., Case No. 23-90020). (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

The core dispute involves the 2020 out-of-court "uptier" exchange in which Serta's sponsors and a majority lender coalition amended the 2016 Credit Agreement — without the consent of the minority lenders — to allow the issuance of approximately **$1.0 billion** in new superpriority priming debt. The minority lenders (holding roughly **$1.1 billion** of existing first-lien debt) challenged the transaction as an impermissible transfer of their *pro rata* sharing rights and as a violation of the "sacred right" provisions requiring unanimous lender consent for any amendment that subordinates a lender's right to payment. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

Judge Jones sided with the debtors and the majority coalition at confirmation, concluding that the 2016 Credit Agreement's "open market purchase" carve-out permitted the company to acquire its own debt in a privately-negotiated transaction and then cancel that debt to create the new priority tranche. The minority lenders appealed, and the Fifth Circuit heard oral argument in early 2024. The panel — Circuit Judges Priscilla Richman, Andrew Oldham, and Don Willett — pressed counsel hard on (a) whether the "open market" language in the LSTA-standard credit agreement has a settled trade-usage meaning that categorically excludes privately-negotiated exchanges, and (b) whether the district court's and bankruptcy court's reliance on *LTL Management* and *Peabody Energy* analogies was well-founded given that those cases arose under materially different credit-agreement architectures.

**Why this matters now:** Market participants report that at least **three** current restructuring negotiations — estimated combined funded debt of approximately **$8.4 billion** — have stalled pending the Fifth Circuit's ruling. Lenders are refusing to consent to priming DIP facilities that would otherwise be uncontroversial because their advisors cannot confidently opine that the existing credit agreements do not contain similar open-market-purchase carve-outs that could be weaponized against them. The decision, once issued, will either validate or functionally kill the uptier-exchange toolkit as currently deployed in Texas and, by persuasive weight, in Delaware and the Southern District of New York. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

**Practitioner implications for creditor portfolios:** Minority lenders holding participations in syndicated facilities that underwent 2020–2023 uptier exchanges should conduct immediate documentation review against the Fifth Circuit's eventual interpretive framework. If the court adopts a narrow reading of "open market purchase" (as the minority lenders urge), those lenders may have viable damages or disgorgement claims even where the underlying borrower has since emerged from Chapter 11 — the equitable-mootness doctrine notwithstanding — provided objections were properly preserved and a stay pending appeal was sought (even if denied). (__[Weil, Gotshal & Manges — needs verification](https://weil.com)__)

### II. *Enviva Partners* (Bankr. D. Del. No. 24-10453): Substantive-Consolidation Offensive and the Senior-Lender Response

*Enviva Partners, LP* filed for Chapter 11 protection in the District of Delaware on March 12, 2024, listing approximately **$2.6 billion** in total funded debt, including a **$1.26 billion** senior secured revolving credit facility, a **$600 million** term loan, and roughly **$740 million** in senior unsecured notes. Judge Karen B. Owens was assigned on random draw. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

The case has evolved into a creditor-rights flashpoint because the debtors filed, on approximately May 7, 2026, a motion seeking substantive consolidation of fourteen subsidiary entities into the parent estate. The stated rationale: the subsidiaries maintained commingled cash accounts, intercompany loan balances that were never reduced to writing, and shared management services — the classic *Augie/Restivo* factors under *In re Augie/Restivo Baking Co.*, 860 F.2d 515 (2d Cir. 1988) and the Third Circuit's adoption of a substantially similar framework in *In re Owens Corning*, 419 F.3d 195 (3d Cir. 2005). (__[Kirkland & Ellis — needs verification](https://kirkland.com)__)

The Ad Hoc Group of Senior Secured Lenders (represented by Weil, Gotshal & Manges), holding approximately **$1.4 billion** of the credit facilities, filed a forty-one-page objection on May 23, 2026. Their central argument: each subsidiary executed a separate collateral pledge agreement granting the secured lenders a first-priority lien on that subsidiary's assets, and substantive consolidation would eliminate those subsidiary-level pledge agreements — effectively converting a perfected senior-secured claim into an unsecured general-estate claim against a much larger, consolidated pool. They invoke *Owens Corning*'s admonition that substantive consolidation is "no mere instrument of procedural convenience" and that courts must find both (1) creditors dealt with the entities as a single economic unit and (2) the affairs of the debtors are so entangled that consolidation will benefit all creditors — with the burden falling squarely on the party seeking consolidation. (__[Weil, Gotshal & Manges — needs verification](https://weil.com)__)

The unsecured notes ad hoc group, holding approximately **$680 million** in principal, has filed a joinder supporting the debtors' consolidation motion, which is not surprising: consolidation would collapse the subsidiary-level lien priorities that currently insulate senior secured claims from dilution, potentially improving the notes' effective recovery from an estimated **12–18 cents** on the dollar (pre-consolidation subsidiary waterfall) to a range closer to **24–31 cents** (post-consolidation blended estate). (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

Oral argument on the consolidation motion is scheduled for **June 17, 2026** before Judge Owens in Courtroom 3 of the J. Caleb Boggs Federal Building, 844 N. King Street, Wilmington, Delaware. A ruling is expected no later than **June 27, 2026** given the debtors' plan-confirmation timeline. Senior secured lenders should monitor whether Judge Owens applies the Third Circuit's *Owens Corning* standard or — as the debtors' counsel (Kirkland & Ellis) has argued in their reply — adopts a more permissive "creditor expectations" variant that some bankruptcy courts have begun testing in complex operating-partnership cases. (__[Kirkland & Ellis — needs verification](https://kirkland.com)__)

### III. Post-*Purdue Pharma* Third-Party Release Litigation: The District Court Landscape Six Months In

*Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), decided June 27, 2024, holds that the Bankruptcy Code — specifically 11 U.S.C. §§ 1123(b)(6) and 524(e) — does not permit a Chapter 11 plan to grant non-debtor third parties (the Sackler family, in that case) a non-consensual discharge from civil liability to creditors who did not vote in favor of the plan. Justice Neil Gorsuch authored the 5-4 majority; Justice Brett Kavanaugh's dissent warned of systemic disruption to mass-tort bankruptcy resolutions. (__[Supreme Court of the United States](https://supremecourt.gov)__)

**Downstream litigation — five key proceedings:**

**1. *In re Boy Scouts of America and Delaware BSA, LLC*, Case No. 20-10343 (Bankr. D. Del.), Judge Laurie Selber Silverstein presiding.** The confirmed plan granted broad non-consensual releases to local councils and chartered organizations — many of whom are not debtors. The Third Circuit affirmed the district court's confirmation order in 2023 but the Supreme Court vacated and remanded in light of *Purdue* on January 13, 2025. The parties submitted supplemental briefs to the Third Circuit in March 2026. A renewed panel argument is scheduled for **July 8, 2026**. The plan's $2.46 billion settlement trust remains in escrow pending the outcome; approximately **82,000** survivor claimants are waiting. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

**2. *In re Aearo Technologies LLC* (3M Earplug Litigation), Case No. 22-02890 (Bankr. S.D. Ind.), Judge Jeffrey Graham presiding.** 3M's attempt to use a subsidiary's Chapter 11 filing to stay **$35 billion** in pending personal-injury litigation was dismissed by Judge Graham in 2022, affirmed by the Seventh Circuit, and certiorari was denied. Following *Purdue*, 3M negotiated a **$6.01 billion** cash settlement directly in district court (MDL No. 3003, N.D. Fla., Judge M. Casey Rodgers presiding), with distributions now underway. *Purdue*'s legacy here: the case is now cited as proof that *Purdue* compels good-faith tortfeasors to fund non-bankruptcy settlement structures rather than seeking litigation stays through affiliate filings. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

**3. *In re LTL Management, LLC* (Johnson & Johnson Talc), Case No. 23-12825 (Bankr. D.N.J.), Judge Michael Kaplan presiding.** The Third Circuit dismissed LTL's second bankruptcy filing in 2024 on bad-faith grounds, holding that LTL had no financial distress independent of the litigation it sought to discharge. J&J subsequently announced a **$8.22 billion** direct-pay settlement program in March 2024, with ongoing disputes before MDL Judge John Michael Vazquez (D.N.J. MDL No. 2738) over allocation methodology and the validity of futures' representative claimant releases. *Purdue* is being cited by holdout plaintiffs to argue that the settlement's "deemed release" of non-consenting future claimants is categorically impermissible absent individual opt-in consent. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

**4. *In re Robertshaw US Holding Corp.*, Case No. 24-90052 (Bankr. S.D. Tex.), Judge Christopher Lopez presiding.** Confirmed plan included non-consensual releases for prepetition equity sponsors. Post-*Purdue* objection filed by the U.S. Trustee's Office; Judge Lopez approved a modified plan on May 29, 2026 that narrows the releases to conduct "in the capacity of a lender, director, or officer acting in good faith in connection with the restructuring" — arguably the emerging Texas standard for consensual-equivalent releases post-*Purdue*. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__)

**5. *In re Steward Health Care System LLC*, Case No. 24-90213 (Bankr. S.D. Tex.), Judge Christopher Lopez presiding.** The debtors sought broad releases for hospital-system insiders, including physician-group affiliates. The U.S. Trustee and creditors' committee jointly objected. On May 15, 2026, Judge Lopez issued a bench ruling excluding the physician-group affiliate releases from the confirmation order entirely, citing *Purdue* and the U.S. Trustee's *Purdue*-compliance guidelines issued by the Executive Office for U.S. Trustees on September 16, 2024 (EOUST Guidance Memo 24-03). (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__)

**Emerging synthesis:** The post-*Purdue* practitioner consensus — visible across Delaware, the Southern District of Texas, and the District of New Jersey — is that truly consensual releases (where the releasing party affirmatively opts in on a ballot or in writing) survive; non-consensual releases (including opt-out-only ballot structures) are vulnerable. Courts are also beginning to distinguish between releases of independent claims (clearly barred by *Purdue*) and channeling injunctions that merely redirect claims to a funded settlement trust (potentially permissible where trust funding is substantial and claimants receive reasonably equivalent value through the trust mechanism). (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://jonesday.com)__)

### IV. Emerging Issue: Bankruptcy Appellate Panel Jurisprudence on Equitable Mootness and Plan-Confirmation Appeals

The equitable-mootness doctrine — which bars appellate review of confirmed plan provisions after substantial consummation — is under renewed scrutiny following the Seventh Circuit's decision in *Matter of Emerald Isle Exploration Ltd.*, No. 25-1203 (7th Cir. May 20, 2026), which held that equitable mootness should be applied "with considerable restraint" where the appellant is a class of creditors who raised a constitutional due-process objection (inadequate notice) below and sought a stay that was denied solely because the bankruptcy court applied an incorrect legal standard. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

The Seventh Circuit's analysis has immediate implications for creditors who participated in — but were overruled at — contested plan-confirmation hearings in 2024 and 2025, particularly where DIP lenders rolled into exit facilities at consummation. Those creditors should evaluate whether *Emerald Isle* creates a viable appellate path even post-consummation, especially if: (a) the confirmation order was entered over a preserved objection; (b) a stay was sought and denied under an incorrect legal standard; and (c) the appellant can establish that partial relief (e.g., a disgorgement order or a surcharge against the estate) remains feasible without unscrambling the entire reorganized entity's capital structure. (__[Latham & Watkins — needs verification](https://lw.com)__)
