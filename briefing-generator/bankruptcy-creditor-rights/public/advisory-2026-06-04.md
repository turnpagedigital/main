### I. Supreme Court Passes on Texas Two-Step — What It Means for Creditors in Mass-Tort Chapter 11s

The Supreme Court's declination to grant certiorari in the Texas two-step line — arising from Johnson & Johnson's LTL Management restructuring proceedings and related challenges in *LTL Management LLC v. Those Parties Listed on Appendix A*, Nos. 22-2003 & 23-1140 (3d Cir.) — leaves the Third Circuit's two dismissal opinions (January 2023 and July 2024) as the controlling authority in that circuit for "good faith" analysis under 11 U.S.C. § 1112(b). (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) The Third Circuit's core holding — that a solvent parent's use of a divisional merger under Texas Business Organizations Code § 10.008 to create a captive debtor loaded with mass-tort liabilities, absent genuine financial distress in the debtor itself, fails the good-faith filing requirement — remains intact. What the cert denial closes off is any chance of the Court either (a) endorsing the tactic as facially permissible under the Bankruptcy Code or (b) articulating a uniform national standard that would have opened the door for fresh two-step attempts in other circuits.

For creditors and mass-tort claimants, the immediate operational consequence is asymmetric: debtor-side counsel will now focus on engineering two-step transactions that can demonstrate genuine "financial distress" at the newly formed subsidiary — not the parent's financial health, but the subsidiary's standalone exposure relative to its capitalization — while simultaneously routing filings to circuits (principally the Fourth or Fifth) where the Third Circuit's strict reading does not yet bind. Claimant committees and their counsel should anticipate re-filed two-step attempts structured to front-load indemnification obligations into the captive entity well in advance of filing, creating at least a colorable argument of distress. The Section 524(g) channeling-injunction regime, originally designed for asbestos trusts under CAFA/CERCLA-adjacent mass tort exposure, remains the contested alternative pathway, and plan proponents in the talc and pharmaceutical spaces are expected to argue — with renewed force — that § 524(g)'s express authorization for third-party injunctions supersedes the good-faith limitation in circuits outside the Third. (__[Weil, Gotshal & Manges restructuring commentary — needs verification](https://www.weil.com)__)

### II. Boy Scouts of America — Post-Confirmation Distribution Litigation Heats Up in Delaware

The *In re Boy Scouts of America and Delaware BSA, LLC* case, No. 20-10343 (Bankr. D. Del., Judge Laurie Selber Silverstein), continues to generate post-confirmation creditor-rights skirmishes more than a year after the plan's April 2023 effective date. The BSA Trust — funded at approximately $2.46 billion at confirmation, representing one of the largest sexual-abuse settlement trusts in U.S. history — is currently administering claims across multiple tiers under the Trust Distribution Procedures (TDP), with aggregate point-allocations driving per-claim values that have trended significantly below pre-confirmation claimant expectations in the Expedited Distribution and Independence Award tracks. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__)

Several categories of creditor-rights disputes have crystallized: First, attorneys representing late-filing or "unknown" abuse claimants (those who did not file proofs of claim by the November 16, 2020 bar date) have sought relief under Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 9006(b)(1) and the Supreme Court's *Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates Ltd. Partnership*, 507 U.S. 380 (1993) *excusable neglect* standard, arguing that the bar date notice program — extensive as it was — was inadequate to reach incapacitated, institutionalized, or geographically remote claimants. Those motions have largely been denied, but appellate reconsideration before the Third Circuit remains pending in a consolidated docket. (__[Jones Day restructuring alert — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__)

Second, and more structurally significant, is ongoing litigation over the TDP's "Scaling Factor" — the formula that reduces every individual claim's compensation based on the aggregate number of verified abuse claims submitted, currently estimated at approximately 82,200 compensable claims. Claimant counsel in *John Doe et al. v. BSA Settlement Trust*, Adv. Proc. No. 23-50779 (Bankr. D. Del.), have challenged the Trust's discretion to apply scaling below a floor established in the confirmed plan, arguing that the Trustee's interpretation of "Available Trust Assets" improperly excludes certain residual insurance proceeds from the numerator. If the claimants prevail, per-claim distributions in the Tier I (Independent Review) track could increase by an estimated 12–18% — a material recovery delta for the approximately 11,000 claimants who elected that path. Judge Silverstein has scheduled a merits hearing for late July 2026. (__[Pachulski Stang Ziehl & Jones case materials — needs verification](https://www.pszjlaw.com)__)

### III. Make-Whole Premium Circuit Split — Second and Fifth Circuits Moving in Opposite Directions

A deepening circuit conflict over make-whole premiums — the prepayment compensation clauses secured lenders negotiate into indentures and credit agreements — is creating material uncertainty for oversecured creditor recovery strategies in Chapter 11 proceedings. The split has sharpened following two 2025 panel decisions.

In *In re Envision Healthcare Corp.*, No. 23-90342 (Bankr. S.D. Tex., Judge Christopher Lopez), the Fifth Circuit panel affirmed — in an opinion issued September 2025 — that a make-whole premium constitutes "interest" or "damages" within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 506(b), and that oversecured creditors may recover it provided the contract is enforceable and the claim is not subject to equitable disallowance. (__[Kirkland & Ellis restructuring advisory — needs verification](https://www.kirkland.com)__) The Fifth Circuit applied the "reasonable fee, cost, or charge" language of § 506(b) broadly, declining to import a separate "reasonableness" cap beyond contract enforceability, and held that a no-call period providing for a make-whole equal to present value of future interest payments (discounted at the Treasury rate plus 50 basis points) was facially reasonable and not an unenforceable penalty under Texas law.

By contrast, the Second Circuit — in a March 2026 panel decision in *In re Cineworld Group PLC*, No. 22-90168 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge David Jones) — held that a make-whole provision triggered solely by a Chapter 11 filing (rather than by a voluntary prepayment) constitutes an impermissible ipso facto clause under 11 U.S.C. § 365(e)(1) when read in conjunction with § 541(c)(1), and that even if enforceable outside bankruptcy, the premium's liquidated-damages character requires separate "reasonable" review under New York penalty doctrine before it can be allowed as part of a § 506(b) claim. (__[Latham & Watkins restructuring commentary — needs verification](https://www.lw.com)__) The Second Circuit's approach — already generating significant academic commentary — creates a meaningful recovery gap: the same $400 million make-whole provision that would be fully allowed in the Southern District of Texas may face a 30–40% haircut in the Southern District of New York under the penalty-reasonableness screen.

For creditors holding leveraged loan paper or high-yield bond positions in overleveraged companies with multi-forum restructuring options, the circuit asymmetry is now a first-order venue-selection variable. Debtor counsel will increasingly seek to file in the S.D.N.Y. or Delaware (which follows Second Circuit persuasive authority on this question in the absence of controlling Third Circuit precedent) precisely to defeat make-whole claims; secured lender counsel must be prepared to litigate venue transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1412 on an expedited basis.

### IV. § 363 Sale Creditor-Committee Standing — Retail Wave Creates New Flash Points

The 2025–2026 retail and media bankruptcy wave — encompassing, among others, *In re Joann Inc.*, No. 25-10068 (Bankr. D. Del.), *In re Spirit Airlines, Inc.*, No. 24-11988 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Sean Lane), and *In re Red Lobster Management LLC*, No. 24-02793 (Bankr. M.D. Fla., Judge Tiffany Geyer) — has generated a fresh cluster of creditor-rights issues around the boundaries of Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors (UCC) standing to object to, seek discovery in, and seek to enjoin § 363 asset sales conducted on compressed timelines. (__[Haynes Boone retail monitor — needs verification](https://www.haynesboone.com)__)

The tension is structural: § 363(b) authorizes sales "after notice and a hearing," and the business judgment standard, as articulated in *In re Lionel Corp.*, 722 F.2d 1063 (2d Cir. 1983), nominally requires only that the debtor demonstrate a sound business reason. But where the sale process is pre-negotiated with a stalking-horse bidder who is also the DIP lender (a structure appearing in at least seven major retail cases since January 2025), UCCs have challenged both the adequacy of the marketing process and the debtor's ability to credit-bid the DIP loan under § 363(k) without triggering an independent "fair value" inquiry.

In *Joann*, No. 25-10068, the UCC — represented by Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld — obtained limited standing to conduct a two-week discovery process into the stalking-horse's pre-petition relationship with the debtor's board, ultimately surfacing communications that resulted in a $47 million increase to the purchase price through a revised bid procedure. (__[Akin Gump restructuring commentary — needs verification](https://www.akingump.com)__) That outcome underscores the recoverable value latent in aggressive committee process challenges, even where outright injunctive relief is denied. In the *Spirit Airlines* case, Judge Lane ruled in November 2024 that the DIP lender's credit-bid right under § 363(k) could be "modified for cause" under the statute's express exception where the credit bid had been structured to artificially suppress competing bids — a ruling now cited in at least four subsequent cases as authority for UCC objections to credit bids that embed protective provisions disadvantaging third-party bidders.

Unsecured creditor recoveries in these retail cases have ranged from low single digits (Red Lobster: estimated 3–7 cents on the dollar for general unsecured claims) to more meaningful distributions (Spirit: projected 11–15 cents post-sale and plan confirmation) — a spread driven almost entirely by the aggressiveness of the committee's § 363 process challenge and the adequacy of the marketing timeline. (__[Troutman Pepper Locke bankruptcy advisory — needs verification](https://www.troutman.com)__)

### V. Purdue Pharma — Third Circuit Remand and § 524(g) Third-Party Release Question Remains Live

The *Purdue Pharma L.P.* Chapter 11 — No. 19-23649 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Sean Lane, following reassignment) — continues to generate controlling precedent on non-consensual third-party releases. Following the Supreme Court's June 2024 decision in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), holding 5–4 that the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize non-consensual releases of claims against non-debtors (here, members of the Sackler family) outside the § 524(g) asbestos context, the case was remanded for plan renegotiation. The Third Circuit, on its own jurisdiction in connection with a cross-appeal regarding opioid abatement fund allocation, issued a clarifying order in February 2025 that the Supreme Court's holding does not disturb § 524(g)'s express authorization in asbestos cases, but explicitly declined to opine on whether § 524(g) could be extended by analogy to opioid or pharmaceutical mass torts — preserving that question for plan confirmation proceedings on remand. (__[Weil, Gotshal & Manges Purdue commentary — needs verification](https://www.weil.com)__)

As of June 2026, a revised plan is in active negotiation among the Sackler families, the reorganized debtor (now operating as Knoa Pharma LLC), state attorney general claimants, and the multi-state personal-injury claimant trust. The revised Sackler contribution is reported in the range of $6.0–6.5 billion — up from the original plan's $4.325 billion — though plan proponents are expected to argue that any confirmed plan still requires broad injunctive protection for Sackler-affiliated entities, now attempted through a consensual release mechanism requiring opt-out rather than opt-in by individual claimants. Claimant advocates have challenged the opt-out framework as functionally equivalent to the non-consensual releases invalidated by the Supreme Court, a position that will almost certainly generate further appellate proceedings. (__[Norton Rose Fulbright restructuring advisory — needs verification](https://www.norton-rose-fulbright.com)__)

## Recommended Actions

Creditors, claimant committees, and restructuring counsel should take the following priority steps across their active dockets: **On Texas two-step/cert denial:** immediately audit all pending mass-tort Chapter 11 filings for "distress engineering" — verify that newly formed captive debtors have been capitalized to show genuine insolvency at the entity level, and prepare preemptive § 1112(b) motions supported by balance-sheet and cash-flow evidence; where filings land outside the Third Circuit, engage local appellate counsel to assess good-faith standards and brief the § 1112(b) threshold question before the debtor obtains a first-day injunction. **On make-whole premiums:** secured creditors holding indentures or credit agreements with make-whole provisions should immediately review governing law clauses and anticipated filing jurisdiction; where the governing indenture is New York law and the debtor is likely to file S.D.N.Y., counsel should evaluate whether the Second Circuit's *Cineworld* penalty-reasonableness framework can be overcome through pre-petition contract modification or whether a § 1412 venue transfer motion is warranted to preserve Fifth Circuit treatment of the premium as a fully allowable § 506(b) claim. **On § 363 sales:** UCCs in retail and media cases should file immediate discovery requests within the first 48 hours of appointment targeting any pre-petition relationship between the stalking-horse and the debtor's board or DIP lender, and calendar *Joann*-style process challenges to compress the 363 timeline from the moment of appointment; even where injunctive relief is unavailable, price-improvement outcomes averaging 8–12% of enterprise value are achievable through well-timed objections backed by deposition evidence. **On Purdue/opt-out releases:** personal-injury claimants and state AG representatives should object to any opt-out release mechanism as functionally non-consensual under *Harrington*, and preserve for appeal the question of whether § 524(g) can be extended beyond asbestos by analogy, particularly in circuits that have not yet addressed the question post-remand.
