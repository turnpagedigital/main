# 📜 Bankruptcy Creditor Rights | Tuesday, June 02, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Today's principal delta is decisive and circuit-shaping: the United States Supreme Court has declined certiorari in the Georgia-Pacific / Bestwall two-step bankruptcy challenge, leaving intact the Fourth Circuit's ruling that permitted the subsidiary spin-and-file maneuver and handing mass-tort defendants a significant — if tenuous — procedural lifeline even as asbestos claimants, talc plaintiffs, and their counsel recalibrate litigation strategy for 2026 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The denial of cert lands alongside Law360's confirmation that Impac Mortgage Holdings has received Chapter 11 plan confirmation and that Bestar Inc. has secured Chapter 15 recognition, adding two discrete data points to the post-Purdue restructuring landscape (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Taken together, this morning's developments reinforce a creditor-rights environment defined by: (1) the Supreme Court's continued reluctance to police the outer limits of divisive-merger-enabled mass-tort bankruptcy; (2) the downstream pressure that reluctance places on the MDL plaintiffs' bar and on the Third Circuit's now-isolated *LTL Management* framework; and (3) the operational significance of Chapter 15 as a recognition vehicle for cross-border restructurings affecting U.S. creditor classes.

### I. Supreme Court Certiorari Denial in the Bestwall / Georgia-Pacific Two-Step — Doctrinal Fallout and Creditor-Rights Implications

The Court's denial of certiorari in *In re Bestwall LLC*, No. 23-____ (U.S., cert. denied June 2, 2026) forecloses, at least for this Term, a definitive ruling on whether a solvent parent corporation may engineer a divisive merger under state law — most commonly North Carolina or Texas — to offload mass-tort liabilities into a newly-created, undercapitalized subsidiary, file that subsidiary in Chapter 11, and thereby channel plaintiffs into a § 524(g) asbestos trust while the parent continues operations free of litigation exposure (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

The underlying Fourth Circuit decision, *In re Bestwall LLC*, 71 F.4th 168 (4th Cir. 2023), had affirmed Bankruptcy Judge J. Craig Whitley (W.D.N.C., Charlotte Division, Case No. 17-31795) on the core question of good-faith filing: the court held that even a solvent debtor with litigation management as its primary purpose can be a good-faith Chapter 11 debtor, provided that reorganization — i.e., trust formation — is a genuine reorganizational objective and not a naked litigation stay. Critics argued that "reorganizational purpose" was hollowed out where the entity had no ongoing business operations, no employees, and no assets beyond the liability assignment itself (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The cert denial is not, technically, a merits ruling. But its practical effect is substantial. First, it signals that at least six Justices — the threshold for a certiorari grant — declined to view the two-step question as ripe for uniform national resolution. This creates an extended window during which divisive-merger defendants may continue to exploit the maneuver in the Fourth Circuit. Second, it deepens the circuit split with the Third Circuit's *LTL Management* line: recall that in *In re LTL Management LLC*, 64 F.4th 84 (3d Cir. 2023) (*LTL II*), the Third Circuit (Judge Ambro, joined by Judges Krause and Freeman) dismissed Johnson & Johnson's second Chapter 11 filing for lack of good faith, holding that a debtor whose non-bankruptcy alternatives are not "in financial distress" cannot invoke Chapter 11 as a mass-tort settlement vehicle (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). That split now hardens without Supreme Court intervention: the Fourth Circuit permits the maneuver; the Third Circuit does not.

For creditors and claimants, the operative question is forum dependency. Companies with North Carolina or Texas incorporation histories — Bestwall/Georgia-Pacific, Aldrich Pump/Murray Boiler (both W.D.N.C., Case Nos. 19-30088 and 19-30089, respectively, before Judge Whitley) — retain access to the four-circuit maneuver. Companies headquartered or incorporated in Delaware, New Jersey, or Pennsylvania face Third Circuit hostility. The forum-selection pressure on future defendants is real and immediate: expect restructuring counsel to advise divisive mergers in states whose entity law clearly authorizes them (N.C. Gen. Stat. § 55-11A-01 et seq.; Tex. Bus. Orgs. Code §§ 10.001–10.008) and to select W.D.N.C. or S.D. or N.D. Texas as filing venues.

**Creditor countermoves.** The asbestos claimants' committee in Bestwall — represented by Caplin & Drysdale and Montgomery McCracken — had argued for a § 1112(b) dismissal on bad-faith grounds and for equitable consolidation of Bestwall's liabilities with Georgia-Pacific's balance sheet. Both theories failed at confirmation. Claimants pursuing similar strategies in pending two-step cases (including *In re Garlock Sealing Technologies*, W.D.N.C. No. 10-31607, and the *Aldrich Pump* cases) should note that the dismissal-on-bad-faith avenue is now significantly more difficult unless filed in the Third Circuit. Mediation leverage — pressing for trust funding ratios above 50% of projected liability — remains the primary recovery tool (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

The confirmation is notable for several creditor-rights reasons. First, the plan appears to have effected a § 1129(b) cramdown on at least one class of junior unsecured noteholders, with the debtor invoking the absolute priority rule and offering junior creditors new equity warrants structured to comply with the new value corollary doctrine following *Bank of America v. 203 North LaSalle Street Partnership*, 526 U.S. 434 (1999). For mortgage-sector creditors watching Impac as a bellwether: the compressed prepack timeline — estimated at fewer than 90 days from filing to confirmation — reflects the J. ### III. Bestar Inc. Chapter 15 Recognition — Cross-Border Coordination and U.S. Creditor Class Exposure

Bankruptcy Judge ____________ (Bankr. __. D. ____) entered an order recognizing *In re Bestar Inc.*, Case No. 26-_____ as a foreign main proceeding under Chapter 15 of the Bankruptcy Code (11 U.S.C. §§ 1515–1524), effective as of the order date (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Bestar, a Canadian furniture manufacturer with meaningful U.S. trade-creditor exposure arising from retail supply contracts with major box-store retailers, filed its primary insolvency proceeding under the *Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act* (CCAA) in the Superior Court of Québec, Commercial Division.

Recognition as a "foreign main proceeding" under § 1517 triggers the automatic stay of § 362 against all U.S. creditor actions against Bestar's U.S.-sited assets, and empowers the foreign representative to administer those assets consistent with the Canadian monitor's mandate (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The practical consequence for U.S. trade creditors — estimated to number in the low hundreds based on Bestar's disclosed supplier list — is that they are now subject to the Canadian restructuring timeline and must file proofs of claim in the CCAA proceeding through the monitor, PricewaterhouseCoopers Inc. (Québec), rather than pursuing independent collection actions in U.S. courts.

Key creditor-rights considerations under the Chapter 15 framework: (1) § 1522 authorizes the U.S. court to condition or modify the automatic stay to protect U.S. creditors from "manifestly contrary" treatment relative to domestic insolvency standards; (2) the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency, as adopted in Chapter 15, does not automatically subordinate U.S. secured creditors to Canadian restructuring priorities — perfected liens under Article 9 of the UCC generally survive recognition provided the U.S. court does not affirmatively restructure those interests; and (3) U.S. creditors have standing to appear and object in the recognition proceeding within 21 days of service under Fed. R. Bankr. P. 1012 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

### IV. Broader Creditor-Rights Context: Post-*Purdue* § 524(g) Trust Dynamics and Third-Party Release Architecture

The Bestwall cert denial must be read against the continuing fallout from *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024), in which the Supreme Court (5-4, Justice Gorsuch for the majority) held that the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize non-consensual third-party releases of non-debtor personal liability in a Chapter 11 plan — thereby invalidating the Sackler family's $6 billion in third-party liability protection and remanding for revised plan proceedings (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

As of this advisory, the *Purdue Pharma II* remand proceedings remain pending before Bankruptcy Judge Sean Lane (S.D.N.Y., Case No. 19-23649). The Purdue estate and the Sackler families have been engaged in court-ordered mediation since the remand, with Judge Lane having appointed Judge Shelley C. Chapman (Ret.) as mediator. A revised plan term sheet was reportedly circulated to the official unsecured creditors' committee and the ad hoc group of personal injury claimants in April 2026, though no amended disclosure statement has been filed on the public docket as of May 30, 2026 (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

The tension between *Purdue* and the Bestwall two-step is structural: *Purdue* constrains what a Chapter 11 plan can *grant* (non-consensual non-debtor releases); the two-step constrains what plaintiffs can *reach* (solvent parent assets). A world in which both rulings stand is one in which mass-tort defendants can insulate parent-company balance sheets from tort liability through divisive merger while simultaneously being unable to use the plan-release mechanism to permanently enjoin claims against those same parent entities outside § 524(g)'s asbestos-specific framework. The result is an unstable equilibrium that will likely drive further Congressional attention — note the pending *Nondebtor Release Prohibition Act of 2025*, S. 1478, 119th Cong. (2025), which passed the Senate Judiciary Committee in March 2026 and awaits floor scheduling (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

For § 524(g) asbestos trust practitioners: the cert denial in Bestwall does not disturb the § 524(g) machinery itself. Trusts already operating — including the Manville Personal Injury Settlement Trust, the Armstrong World Industries Asbestos Personal Injury Settlement Trust, the W.R. Grace & Co. Asbestos PI Trust, and the Garlock Sealing Technologies Asbestos Settlement Trust — continue to process claims under their respective Trust Distribution Procedures (TDPs). Payment percentages across major trusts continue to range from roughly 6% (Manville, on certain disease categories) to over 40% (Grace, mesothelioma disease level V) depending on trust funding ratios and claims volume (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

### V. Preference and Fraudulent Transfer Litigation Watch — 90-Day Lookback and Solvency Presumptions

Separate from the headline two-step development, preference litigation cycles initiated in 2024–2025 Chapter 11 filings (Rite Aid, Yellow Corporation, Envision Healthcare) are now generating adversary proceeding trial calendars in the Bankr. D. Del. and Bankr. S.D.N.Y. for Q3–Q4 2026. Rite Aid's plan administrator (Kroll Restructuring Administration) has filed in excess of 340 preference complaints under § 547 of the Bankruptcy Code, with the aggregate asserted recovery target estimated at approximately $180 million (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The ordinary course of business defense under § 547(c)(2) — now requiring defendants to establish that the transfers were made in the ordinary course of business or financial affairs of both debtor and transferee, OR consistent with ordinary business terms in the industry (post-BAPCPA disjunctive standard) — remains the primary shield for trade creditors. Recent Bankr. D. Del. practice under Chief Judge Laurie Selber Silverstein (CICA Preference Working Group protocols) has encouraged early-stage mediation referrals for preference adversaries below $500,000, which is reducing trial volume but compressing settlement timelines (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Yellow Corporation's preference program (Case No. 23-11069, Bankr. D. Del., Judge Craig T. Goldblatt) is similarly advanced, with the plan administrator — Drivetrain LLC — having dispatched approximately 500 demand letters in Q1 2026. Freight-carrier counterparties and fuel-vendor defendants should note that Judge Goldblatt issued a case management order in April 2026 setting a June 30, 2026 deadline for completion of Rule 26(f) conferences in preference adversaries with asserted values above $100,000 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).
