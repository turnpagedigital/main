**BlockFills (Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.) — § 363 Sale Approved, Confirmation Hearing Opens Today**

The two principal developments this morning are concurrent: Judge Thomas M. Horan in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware approved the § 363 sale in *In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.*, Case No. 26-10371 (TMH), on June 16, and the plan confirmation hearing — targeted from the outset on a compressed timeline — is docketed to begin today, June 22, 2026, at the same court. Both events drive the recovery calculus for approximately $145 million in general unsecured customer and trade obligations in this case.


A Delaware bankruptcy judge on June 16, 2026 agreed to approve the $3.25 million sale of cryptocurrency financial technology firm BlockFills to a Belgian digital asset investment group as BlockFills prepares for a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.
 The buyer is Keyrock SA, a Brussels-based market maker. 
Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property.
 Regulatory approvals remain a condition to closing. 
Final completion of the transaction remains subject to final court approval and the appropriate regulatory approvals referenced in Keyrock's bid.



Less than four weeks after the Petition Date, the Debtors filed a Joint Chapter 11 Plan, a Disclosure Statement, and a Solicitation Procedures Motion on April 7, 2026, establishing a compressed timeline targeting a June 22, 2026 Confirmation Hearing. The Plan features a dual-track "toggle" mechanism allowing the Debtors to elect between a customer-led NewCo Transaction and an Alternative Transaction through a § 363 competitive sale process.
 With Keyrock declared the successful bidder at the May 26 auction and sale now court-approved, the debtors will almost certainly elect the Alternative Transaction path for confirmation today. The NewCo toggle is functionally mooted.

The structural fault line in this case — the one that determines how much general unsecured creditors actually recover — is the asset-commingling dispute. 
On January 16, 2026, Alberta transferred 40.001 BTC and 650,050 USDC to BlockFills; these assets were swept from the deposit wallet within minutes and the wallet was empty within 1.5 hours. On February 11, 2026, the Debtors' representatives disclosed to clients that digital assets were "not segregated per client," "not segregated on separate wallets per customer," and were commingled into "one balance sheet."
 The debtors have argued procedural deficiency on the other side: 
the objectors have not filed an adversary proceeding as required by Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 7001(b) for determination of property of the estate, and have not attempted tracing of specific funds. If customer digital assets are determined to be estate property, they are available to fund the reorganization, to be distributed pro rata under the Plan, and to serve as the basis for the cash collateral arrangement.


The $3.25 million headline sale price against 
roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and about $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens,
 signals deeply impaired recoveries unless the plan provides supplemental consideration through the liquidating trust. Claim-transfer activity should be monitored closely via Rule 3001 transfer notices. Docket No. 446 (filed June 19) is the most recent material entry but description is not yet available via CourtListener; distressed investors should pull the document directly from Verita's claims portal at veritaglobal.net/blockfills.

The adversary proceeding against Artha Investment Partners LLC — 
Adv. Pro. No. 26-50230 (TMH), filed April 9, 2026, in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, Case No. 26-10371 (TMH)
 — remains pending. Its resolution could augment estate recoveries depending on the exposure asserted. No public description of the underlying claim has been confirmed this run; investors should review the adversary docket directly.

The September 11, 2026 bar date is the next hard creditor deadline. 
The claims bar date is September 11, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET, with a June 16, 2026 hearing having already passed.

**Sleep Number Corporation — § 363 Auction Set July 13; Final DIP Hearing July 9; Nasdaq Delisting Effective Tomorrow**


On June 12, 2026, Sleep Number Corporation and its subsidiaries filed voluntary petitions for relief under Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York.
 
The Bankruptcy Court granted a motion seeking joint administration of the cases under the caption *In re: Sleep Number Corporation, et al.*, Case No. 26-11399.
 No judge has been assigned to the docket as of this writing; the tracked-case ground truth reflects "NOT ASSIGNED" and that posture has not changed through June 21.

The case is framed as a going-concern § 363 sale, not a plan reorganization. 
The filing aims to effectuate a value-maximizing, going-concern sale of substantially all of the Debtors' assets under § 363, backstopped by a stalking horse bid from SNBR Inc., an affiliate of Sleep Country Canada Inc.
 
Pursuant to the Stalking Horse APA dated June 12, 2026, the Stalking Horse Bidder has committed — subject to Court approval — to acquire substantially all of the Company's assets for $415 million in cash plus the assumption of certain liabilities.
 
An auction is scheduled for July 13, but the company is open to higher and better offers.


The balance sheet is deeply underwater. 
Sleep Number Corporation reports $642.3 million in assets and $1.3 billion in liabilities.
 
The company enters bankruptcy with $672 million in debt, blaming tariffs and inflation for the filing.
 
Sleep Number reports that the number of creditors is in the range of 50–99.
 That creditor count is unusually lean for a 572-store national retailer — the tight cohort likely reflects the secured-lender-dominated capital structure.


The DIP lenders are expected to provide up to approximately $260 million of debtor-in-possession financing, comprising new money superpriority senior secured term loan commitments in an aggregate principal amount of up to $65 million (the "DIP Loans"), available in multiple draws — up to $50 million upon entry of the interim DIP order and up to the remaining $15 million upon entry of the final DIP order.
 
The Southern District of New York entered the interim DIP order approving the transaction on an interim basis through the final hearing, which is currently scheduled for July 9, 2026.



The claims of the DIP Lenders are, or are expected to be, entitled to superpriority administrative expense claim status and, subject to certain customary exclusions, secured by a perfected first-priority lien on all DIP Collateral (to the extent unencumbered) and a perfected priming senior security interest in and liens on the prepetition collateral.
 The priming lien structure means any unsecured or junior creditor recovery depends entirely on the spread between $415 million (or any higher auction result on July 13) and the quantum of secured and superpriority claims.

The DIP facility includes a roll-up component. 
Sleep Number's obligations under the DIP Loans and the Roll-Up Loans are guaranteed by each subsidiary of the Company.
 The roll-up is a structural creditor-rights concern: prepetition secured lenders who both supply the DIP and benefit from the roll-up capture disproportionate value in a § 363 process relative to trade creditors and landlords.

On the equity side: 
on June 16, 2026, Sleep Number received a written notice from Nasdaq notifying it that Nasdaq has determined to delist the Company's common stock.
 
Trading on Nasdaq will be suspended at the beginning of business on June 23, 2026. Nasdaq will file a Form 25-NSE with the SEC. The Company does not intend to appeal.
 Equity is functionally worthless at current valuation.

Landlord exposure is the most active unsecured creditor vector. 
Sleep Number has 572 retail stores in the U.S.
 Lease assumption or rejection decisions — and the resulting § 365(d)(3) stub-rent obligations and § 502(b)(6) capped rejection claims — will shape the aggregate unsecured claims pool materially. No landlord committee or ad hoc group has been reported in the docket as of June 21; that absence may represent an opportunity or a signal that the prepetition lender group has already pre-negotiated the distribution waterfall.

**Chapter 15 Provisional Relief — Cross-Border Toolbox Expands**

The news scan flags a Reuters headline on provisional relief as a "new tool" in the Chapter 15 context. Verified primary-source developments fill that frame with specificity.


A Canadian AI company has secured Chapter 15 provisional relief in Delaware and a Bermudian reinsurer has obtained protection in New York, while emergency recognition requests are still pending for a Swiss tech company in Texas and a Brazilian company — with a Canadian integrated real estate technology company also having secured provisional Chapter 15 relief pending recognition of its CCAA proceeding, despite a Delaware judge raising concerns that the debtor was seeking more relief in the U.S. than it secured in Canada.


The doctrinal substrate for these grants is § 1519 of the Bankruptcy Code, which permits provisional relief pending a formal recognition hearing under § 1517. 
Because the Bankruptcy Code requires notice of a hearing to grant recognition of a foreign proceeding under Chapter 15, Chapter 15 debtors may seek provisional relief. Provisional relief includes the imposition of the automatic stay and temporary recognition of the foreign court's insolvency order on an interim basis until the hearing on recognition of the foreign proceeding.
 
Provisional relief is only granted as to those parties that the foreign representative specifies. Typically, a debtor's key contract counterparties, lenders, and parties in litigation with the debtor are among those against whom provisional relief is sought.


Creditors in U.S.-facing cross-border cases face a specific asymmetry: 
noticing in a Chapter 15 case is often not as robust as it is in a Chapter 11 case.
 A U.S. creditor who fails to appear before provisional relief is granted may find itself enjoined without having had a practical opportunity to object. The gap between notice and stay imposition is the primary litigation pressure point.

The eligibility circuit split — whether § 109(a) of the Bankruptcy Code is a precondition to Chapter 15 recognition — remains live and unresolved at the appellate level. 
Courts are split over whether the satisfaction of § 109(a) is a precondition to recognition under Chapter 15. *Compare In re Barnet*, 737 F.3d 238 (2d Cir. 2013) (holding § 109(a) satisfaction is a precondition) *and In re Siu-Fung Ceramics Holdings Limited*, No. 24-33299, 2026 WL 382424, at *19 (Bankr. S.D. Tex. Feb. 10, 2026) (same) *with In re Al Zawawi*, 97 F.4th 1244, 1252–53 (11th Cir. 2024) (holding § 109(a) satisfaction was not a condition to Chapter 15 relief).
 No circuit court has weighed in since *Al Zawawi*; this split is ripe for SCOTUS attention.

The most notable recent expansion involves third-party release recognition through Chapter 15's comity framework. 
Recent filings suggest that companies are leveraging recognition proceedings under Chapter 15 to obtain relief they couldn't achieve under foreign or U.S. bankruptcy law alone. Noteworthy decisions highlight a judicial openness — grounded in comity — to recognize foreign proceedings in the U.S. that include third-party releases.
 This matters directly to distressed-debt investors evaluating recovery profiles: a non-U.S. plan that includes third-party releases of potentially liable parties could extinguish U.S. creditor claims against solvent non-debtor entities through Chapter 15 recognition, even post-*Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024), which bars such releases in domestic Chapter 11 plans.

## Recommended Actions

Distressed investors monitoring **BlockFills** should pull Docket Entry 446 (filed June 19) directly from CourtListener or the Verita claims portal before today's confirmation hearing and assess whether any plan modifications, late objections, or cure disputes have been filed since the June 16 sale approval — the window between sale approval and confirmation is precisely where unsecured creditor economics get finalized in the plan documents, including any liquidating trust funding amount and distribution waterfall. For **Sleep Number**, the actionable near-term posture is: (1) confirm whether a creditors' committee has been constituted and who its counsel is, as no committee appointment is confirmed in the docket as of June 21; (2) track the July 9 final DIP hearing for any modification to the roll-up or priming structure that could shift unsecured creditor recovery; (3) monitor July 13 auction results — any overbid above $415 million flows initially to DIP lenders and rolls up secured debt before reaching unsecured creditors, but the quantum matters; and (4) for landlords with 572 leases in play, file a § 503(b)(9) administrative claim if goods were delivered within 20 days of the June 12 petition date, and position for the § 502(b)(6) cap litigation that will follow rejection. On **Chapter 15 provisional relief**, any U.S. creditor that has received notice of a foreign insolvency proceeding by a counterparty should treat that notice as potentially days away from a provisional automatic stay — retain counsel immediately and consider filing objections to COMI designation if there is any basis to argue the debtor's center of main interests is in the United States.
