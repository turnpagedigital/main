### III. Jones Day *BRR* Vol. 25 No. 3 — Three Doctrine Developments Creditors Must Track

The May–June 2026 edition of Jones Day's *Business Restructuring Review* — published this week — surfaces three rulings with immediate cross-portfolio creditor-rights significance. (__[Jones Day, June 10, 2026](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/business-restructuring-review-vol-25-no-3-mayjune-2026)__)

**A. Post-Purdue Third-Party Releases (S.D. Tex.).** The Southern District of Texas has clarified the post-*Purdue* course for consensual third-party releases and gatekeeping provisions. Following *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma, L.P.*, 144 S. Ct. 2071 (2024) — the Supreme Court's 5-4 ruling that Chapter 11 plans may not discharge non-debtor claims without claimant consent — the question of what constitutes meaningful "consent" and what gatekeeping mechanisms survive remains heavily litigated. A clarifying ruling from the Southern District of Texas is operationally important: Texas hosts a disproportionate share of large Chapter 11 cases, and plan negotiators can now calibrate release language and opt-out mechanics against an explicit judicial framework rather than relying on pre-*Purdue* templates. The *Purdue Pharma* ruling effectively limited the use of broad involuntary third-party releases, shifting litigation toward exploring consensual alternatives.

**B. Initial-Transferee Fraudulent-Transfer Liability (Bankr. D. Del.).** The Delaware Bankruptcy Court has ruled that an initial transferee need not be a defendant in fraudulent transfer avoidance and recovery litigation. This is a plaintiff-favorable development for trustees and committees asserting claims under 11 U.S.C. §§ 544, 548, and 550. Under § 550(a), a trustee may recover avoided transfers from "the initial transferee of such transfer or the entity for whose benefit such transfer was made" or "any immediate or mediate transferee of such initial transferee." The Delaware ruling — consistent with the Third Circuit's broad reading of standing in avoidance litigation — expands the universe of potential recovery defendants and should factor into pre-confirmation settlement discussions in any case where the initial transferee is insolvent or judgment-proof.

**C. Adequate Assurance and Plan Exculpation (Bankr. S.D.N.Y.).** The New York Bankruptcy Court has weighed in on adequate assurance of future performance under assigned leases and Chapter 11 plan exculpation provisions. On adequate assurance under 11 U.S.C. § 365(b)(1)(C), landlords in the current retail- and commercial-distress cycle have aggressively contested lease assignments, and a Southern District ruling refining the standard tightens — or loosens — the § 365(b) hurdle for debtors seeking to monetize real-property lease portfolios through § 363 sales. The exculpation limb of the ruling touches the post-*Purdue* boundary: plan exculpation of estate fiduciaries (directors, officers, committee members, and their professionals) has survived the broad third-party release prohibition, but courts are increasingly parsing whether exculpation clauses creep into de facto non-debtor releases for insiders.

### IV. Macro Posture — Filing Rates, Liability Management, and ConvergeOne's Equal-Treatment Warning

The most prominent trend for 2026 is a sustained increase in bankruptcy filings; while filings have not reached pre-COVID levels, month-over-month growth suggests they may surpass them soon, with filings up 10.6% as of September 30, 2025, compared to the prior year.

Against that backdrop, creditors in leveraged structures must contend with the post-*Serta* and post-*ConvergeOne* liability management environment. In December 2025, a Texas district court overturned a company's confirmed reorganization plan after finding it violated the Bankruptcy Code's equal treatment rule, because backstop participation rights were provided only to certain creditors. The key takeaway from *Serta* and *Westco/Incora* is that the outcome of challenges to liability management transactions turns on the specific facts and loan document language; the *ConvergeOne* decision may dampen the relatively common practice of non-pro-rata exit financing structures in Chapter 11 cases. For creditors holding performing but at-risk paper in leveraged buyouts and syndicated credit facilities — particularly in sectors exposed to tariff-driven cost pressure — the equal-treatment principle now operates as a hard constraint on any uptier or out-of-court exchange that does not achieve universal participation or explicit waiver.

S&P Global Ratings projected the U.S. speculative-grade corporate default rate could hit 4.25% by June 2026, a milestone that, if realized, would validate the claims-trading and distressed-debt thesis across multiple watched sectors. Creditor committees formed in early 2026 cases should be pressing debtors now for §1102(b)(3) access agreements and forensic accounting, particularly where pre-petition liability management activity created non-pro-rata outcomes for committee constituency creditors.

### V. Chapter 15 Creditor-Access Constraint — *In re Siu-Fung Ceramics* (Bankr. S.D. Tex.)

In *In re Siu-Fung Ceramics Holdings Limited*, No. 24-33299, 2026 WL 382424 (Bankr. S.D. Tex. Feb. 10, 2026), the Texas bankruptcy court denied petitions for Chapter 15 recognition of Hong Kong bankruptcy and liquidation proceedings: the corporate debtors' Hong Kong liquidation was ineligible because the attorney retainer was not funded until nine months after the Chapter 15 petition date, and the individual debtor's Hong Kong bankruptcy could not be recognized because his center of main interests on the petition date was not in Hong Kong and he did not maintain an "establishment" there. This ruling, highlighted in the Jones Day BRR Vol. 25 No. 3, tightens creditor access to Chapter 15 as an enforcement vehicle for cross-border asset recovery. Creditors pursuing offshore debtors should audit COMI evidence and ensure that retainer funding predates any recognition petition — funding a retainer post-petition to satisfy the "assets in the U.S." requirement will not cure eligibility under *Barnet* or the *Siu-Fung* extension of that rule. (__[Jones Day, June 10, 2026](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/business-restructuring-review-vol-25-no-3-mayjune-2026)__)
