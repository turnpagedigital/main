### II. Chapter 15 Cross-Border Developments — The Cannabist Blueprint and Its Limits

The most consequential structural development for cross-border insolvency practitioners this week is the crystallization of the *Cannabist* Chapter 15 framework and its doctrinal limits under § 1506.

On May 9, 2026, the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware granted recognition under Chapter 15 of the Bankruptcy Code to The Cannabist Company's Canadian restructuring proceeding — marking the first instance in which a U.S. bankruptcy court has recognized a foreign insolvency proceeding involving a cannabis-related enterprise despite the continuing federal prohibition on cannabis under the Controlled Substances Act. The recognition order is *In re The Cannabist Co. Holdings Inc.*, No. 26-10426 (BLS), Dkt. 82 (Bankr. D. Del. May 9, 2026), before Judge Brendan L. Shannon. The Parent Company and its Canadian affiliate are co-issuers of approximately $179 million in senior secured notes governed by Canadian law; the CC Group faced significant headwinds including a default on those notes in January 2026. On March 24, 2026, the Debtors commenced proceedings under the Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act (CCAA) in the Ontario Superior Court of Justice.

The § 1506 public-policy objection was resolved by reservation. The Foreign Representative resolved the secured creditor's objection through additional language in the recognition order reserving the secured creditor's rights to prosecute its objection if the parties are unable to reach an agreement by May 26; if no agreement is reached, the secured creditor may elect to proceed with its objection on an expedited basis. A key regulatory enabler: on December 18, 2025, the President issued an executive order directing the Attorney General to complete the rescheduling of cannabis from Schedule I to Schedule III under the CSA; on April 23, 2026, just weeks before the recognition, the Acting Attorney General issued a final order rescheduling medical cannabis products from Schedule I to Schedule III.

Jason Rosell, who leads Pachulski Stang Ziehl & Jones' cannabis restructuring group, told Bloomberg Law the Trustee's silence may signal a shift, noting: "This has created a road map for other Canadian-based cannabis companies to use. The problem is that it's likely limited from a practical perspective to a few companies." Jonathan Robbins, chair of Akerman's cannabis practice, noted that rescheduling forced the federal government to formally acknowledge accepted medical uses for marijuana and would remove the Section 280E tax burden — in Cannabist's case, the IRS had asserted $51 million in federal income tax claims for 2022 and 2023, according to court filings. Creditors of other distressed cannabis multi-state operators should evaluate whether their counterparty is incorporated in Canada or another jurisdiction with an accessible insolvency regime; a CCAA filing followed by a Delaware Chapter 15 petition now constitutes a viable path to § 362 stay protection for U.S.-sited assets, provided the debtor's COMI is genuinely offshore.

The Arnold & Porter client advisory (June 9, 2026) on recent Chapter 15 developments also highlights a materially different outcome on the far end of the comity spectrum: in March 2026, a Russian bankruptcy trustee filed a petition in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Massachusetts seeking U.S. recognition of an ongoing Russian insolvency proceeding that would have allowed broad discovery into the U.S. assets of a Russian-Israeli former bank director; the respondent raised public policy objections, arguing the Russian proceeding was politically motivated and corrupt; the court denied the petition in full. The contrast between the *Cannabist* grant and the Russian trustee denial illustrates that § 1506 public-policy analysis is fact-intensive and court-specific — a lesson for any creditor or asset-recovery party seeking to leverage foreign proceedings to reach U.S. assets.

### III. Jones Day *Business Restructuring Review* Vol. 25 No. 3 — Current-Year Doctrine Mapping

The May–June 2026 issue of Jones Day's *Business Restructuring Review* (jonesday.com, June 10, 2026) surfaces several rulings with direct creditor-rights implications that practitioners should have in hand going into the second half of 2026:

**Fraudulent-Transfer Avoidance: Initial Transferee Need Not Be Defendant.** The Bankruptcy Code permits recovery of an avoided transfer from not only the initial recipient, but also from subsequent transferees. Courts disagree over whether a transfer must actually be avoided before the trustee can seek recovery from a subsequent transferee and whether the initial transferee must also be a defendant. The U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware weighed in on this debate in *Phillips v. SS Associates LLC* (*In re ONH AFC CS Investors, LLC*), No. 23-10931 (CTG), 2026 WL 312892 (Bankr. D. Del. Feb. 4, 2026), adopting the majority view and ruling that the initial transferee need not be a defendant in avoidance and recovery litigation against a subsequent transferee. This ruling is significant for trustees and creditors' committees prosecuting multi-step fraudulent transfer chains: the practical burden of joining all upstream transferees is eliminated, sharpening the ability to pursue deep-pocket downstream recipients.

**Post-*Purdue Pharma* Architecture in the S.D. Tex.** The May–June 2026 issue also covers the Southern District of Texas's clarification of the post-*Purdue* course for consensual third-party releases and gatekeeping provisions, as well as a New York Bankruptcy Court ruling on adequate assurance of future performance under assigned leases and Chapter 11 plan exculpation provisions. The post-*Harrington v. Purdue Pharma* (U.S. 2024) landscape remains unsettled at the circuit level. The most significant legal constraint impacting Chapter 11 reorganization efforts remained the June 27, 2024 Supreme Court decision in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma, L.P.*, a 5–4 ruling holding that the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize a plan of reorganization to discharge claims against a non-debtor without the consent of affected claimants; in late 2025, the bankruptcy court approved a revised Purdue plan featuring approximately $7 billion in Sackler contributions. Creditors facing plan documents containing broad non-debtor release language should evaluate whether the proposed gatekeeper injunction is fully consensual within the meaning of *Purdue Pharma* or whether it constitutes the type of non-consensual release the Supreme Court prohibited.

**Chapter 15: *Siu-Fung Ceramics* and the Barnet Asset-Presence Rule.** In *In re Siu-Fung Ceramics Holdings Limited*, No. 24-33299, 2026 WL 382424 (Bankr. S.D. Tex. Feb. 10, 2026), the court denied petitions for Chapter 15 recognition of Hong Kong bankruptcy and liquidation proceedings because: (i) the corporate debtors' Hong Kong liquidation proceeding was not eligible for Chapter 15 recognition because the attorney retainer on which such relief was based was not funded until nine months after the Chapter 15 petition date; and (ii) the individual debtor's Hong Kong bankruptcy case could not be recognized because the debtor's "center of main interests" on the Chapter 15 petition date was not in Hong Kong. Read together with *Cannabist*, the pattern is clear: Chapter 15 petitioners must rigorously document (a) timely-funded U.S. assets at the petition date and (b) contemporaneous COMI evidence; deficiencies in either prong are now dispositive in the S.D. Tex.

### IV. Macro Backdrop — Elevated Filings, UCC Priority Disputes, and Lien-Perfection Risk

Bankruptcy activity is expected to remain elevated in 2026 as persistent geopolitical and economic pressures continue to strain both consumers and businesses; creditors should prepare for increased UCC priority disputes, making proactive lien management and documentation even more critical. In 2025, Chapter 11 practice pivoted around two central forces: the constraint of judicial power to grant broad non-debtor relief, and the continued expansion of financial and procedural complexity within distressed business contexts. For lenders and trade creditors operating in the elevated-filing environment, lien perfection remains a major compliance risk: failure to perfect a lien within 30 days of loan origination can result in a creditor being treated as unsecured in bankruptcy, and courts show little leniency for delays.

On the insolvency-finance side, the Southern District of New York has issued guidance on insider DIP financing that creditors' committees should internalize when evaluating sub-rosa plan structures. In *In re SPAC Recovery Co.*, No. 25-12109 (JPM), 2026 WL 18778 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. Jan. 2, 2026), the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York authorized a debtor whose sole asset was litigation claims to borrow money from insiders on a "priming lien" basis to fund the litigation, concluding that the loan was both necessary, beneficial to the estate, and fair. While the *SPAC Recovery* outcome was creditor-positive in that it preserved estate litigation value, it underscores the risk that insider DIP financing can embed plan-like terms early in a case — a dynamic creditors' committees must scrutinize at the approval stage.
