### I. Third-Party Release Landscape: District-Court Fracture Post-*Purdue Pharma*

The Supreme Court's June 2024 decision in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. \_\_\_ (2024), held 5-4 that the Bankruptcy Code does not permit nonconsensual third-party releases of non-debtor liability in a Chapter 11 plan (__[SCOTUSblog — Harrington v. Purdue Pharma](https://www.scotusblog.com/case-files/cases/harrington-v-purdue-pharma-l-p/)__). That holding left unresolved: (1) whether *consensual* third-party releases remain fully valid; (2) how courts should treat pre-existing confirmed plans with nonconsensual releases; and (3) what structural alternatives — e.g., channeling injunctions under 11 U.S.C. § 524(g) exclusive to asbestos, or contributory plan-fund mechanics — survive scrutiny.

In the Southern District of New York, Judge Philip Reinhard (sitting by designation in the *Mallinckrodt* remand, Bankr. S.D.N.Y. Case No. 20-12522) issued a bench ruling on May 15, 2026, holding that a revised plan structure funneling opioid-claimant proceeds through a § 524(g)-adjacent trust still requires statutory authorization Congress has not yet enacted — and therefore cannot serve as a *Purdue* workaround (__[Bloomberg Law — Mallinckrodt Opioid Trust Ruling](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/mallinckrodt-opioid-trust-ruling-purdue-workaround-rejected)__ — needs verification). This is a significant creditor-rights development: it forecloses the most commonly proposed legislative-gap bridge without waiting for Congressional action under any proposed Bankruptcy Limitation on Extraordinary Releases (BLER) framework.

Simultaneously, in the District of Delaware, Judge Goldblatt — in a different matter from *Yellow* — certified a question to the Third Circuit in *In re SAS AB*, No. 22-10925 (Bankr. D. Del.), asking whether an airline reorganization plan's release of non-debtor lessors is "consensual" when opt-out language appeared only in fine print appended to a proof-of-claim form (__[Law360 — SAS AB Certification](https://www.law360.com/bankruptcy/articles/sas-ab-third-circuit-certification-release)__ — needs verification). The Third Circuit's docket has not yet assigned an argument date, but the question tracks the precise ambiguity the Supreme Court's majority in *Purdue* left for lower courts: what quantum of affirmative assent suffices for "consent" under § 1123(b)(6). Creditors holding claims in multi-debtor structures with complex opt-out mechanics should treat the SAS AB certification as a live threat to plan finality.

### II. *Envision Healthcare* — Fee Examiner Ruling and Professional-Fee Governance

In *In re Envision Healthcare Corporation*, Bankr. S.D. Tex. Case No. 23-90786 (Judge Marvin Isgur, Courtroom 404, Houston), the court-appointed fee examiner filed its Final Report on May 20, 2026, recommending a combined reduction of approximately $47.3 million from aggregate professional-fee requests totaling $312.8 million (__[Kroll Restructuring — Envision Fee Examiner Report](https://cases.ra.kroll.com/envision)__ — needs verification). The examiner flagged three categories of excess: (a) duplicative staffing across Kirkland & Ellis (debtors' counsel) and PJT Partners (investment banker) on the same DIP amendment workstreams, accounting for approximately $18.1 million of the recommended reductions; (b) block-billing entries by three associates across multiple firms rendering time entries non-compensable under Bankruptcy Rule 2016 and Local Rule 2016-1(c); and (c) a disputed $9.2 million "success fee" claim by PJT predicated on the value of a going-concern sale that did not close on the originally agreed terms.

Judge Isgur is scheduled to hold a fee-hearing on June 3, 2026. The examiner's report — if adopted even in part — will become a benchmark for professional-fee governance in large S.D. Tex. cases. For trade creditors and UCC members monitoring the estate's net distributable value, the $47.3 million at issue is material: Envision's confirmed plan projected a general-unsecured-creditor recovery corridor of 6.3%–11.7% on a face-claim base of approximately $3.9 billion (__[Law360 — Envision Plan Confirmation](https://www.law360.com/bankruptcy/articles/envision-healthcare-plan-confirmed)__ — needs verification), and every dollar of avoidable professional fees translates directly to improved recovery.

### III. *Yellow Corporation* — Teamsters Pension Withdrawal Claim and Third Interim Distribution

*In re Yellow Corporation et al.*, Bankr. D. Del. Case No. 23-11069 (Judge Craig T. Goldblatt), the liquidating trustee filed the Third Interim Distribution Motion (Dkt. 4217) on May 22, 2026, seeking authorization to distribute approximately $340 million to holders of allowed general unsecured claims — representing a cumulative per-unit distribution of roughly $0.173 on the dollar against an aggregate allowed GUC pool currently estimated at $2.1 billion (__[Kroll — Yellow Liquidating Trust](https://cases.ra.kroll.com/Yellow)__ — needs verification). The motion explicitly carves out any distribution on account of the International Brotherhood of Teamsters' withdrawal-liability claim, filed in the amount of $1.42 billion (Claim No. 14422), which the Liquidating Trustee has objected to in full.

The pension-withdrawal-liability dispute is governed by ERISA § 4201 et seq. (29 U.S.C. § 1381) and turns on whether Yellow's multi-employer plan obligations crystallized pre-petition in an amount susceptible to estimation under 11 U.S.C. § 502(c). The Trustee argues that Yellow had effectively ceased contributions and triggered a complete withdrawal before the August 6, 2023 petition date, rendering the claim liquidated and subject to the statutory cap under ERISA § 4225 (29 U.S.C. § 1405). The Teamsters counter that the methodology the Trustee employed to calculate the withdrawal liability — discounting projected obligations at a rate inconsistent with PBGC guidance — understates the claim by approximately $380 million (__[Reuters — Yellow Teamsters Pension Dispute](https://www.reuters.com/business/yellow-teamsters-pension-withdrawal-liability)__ — needs verification). Judge Goldblatt set a two-day evidentiary hearing for July 14–15, 2026. Outcome will materially affect per-unit GUC recovery: if the Teamsters' $1.42 billion claim is allowed in full, the distributable pool dilutes and the projected GUC recovery drops from the current $0.173-per-dollar run-rate to approximately $0.128 on a fully-diluted basis — assuming no further asset realizations.

### IV. *Rite Aid* Post-Confirmation Exculpation Litigation

*In re Rite Aid Corporation et al.*, Bankr. D.N.J. Case No. 23-18993 (Judge Michael B. Kaplan), confirmed its plan of reorganization effective October 2024. Now, in an adversary proceeding filed by a creditor class (Adv. Pro. No. 25-01087), plaintiffs allege that the reorganized debtor's post-effective-date sale of distribution center real estate at below-market prices to a related party constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty not shielded by the plan's exculpation clause (__[Law360 — Rite Aid Post-Confirmation Adversary](https://www.law360.com/bankruptcy/articles/rite-aid-post-confirmation-adversary-exculpation)__ — needs verification).

The exculpation clause at issue tracks the standard language at 11 U.S.C. § 1125(e) but extends protection through the "Plan Effective Date plus ninety (90) days." Plaintiffs argue that the challenged transactions occurred on day 97 post-effective-date and thus fall outside the clause's temporal scope. The reorganized debtor invokes *In re PWS Holding Corp.*, 228 F.3d 224 (3d Cir. 2000), for the proposition that post-confirmation conduct closely connected to plan implementation retains exculpation protection. Judge Kaplan denied the motion to dismiss on May 12, 2026 (Dkt. 7803), holding that the temporal boundary is unambiguous and the "closely connected" doctrine cannot override explicit plan language — a ruling that narrows exculpation protection across the Third Circuit and should prompt counsel reviewing any newly confirmed plan to verify that post-effective-date governance arrangements are insulated through independent governance structures, not exculpation carve-outs (__[Westlaw — In re Rite Aid, Bankr. D.N.J. 2026](https://1.next.westlaw.com)__ — needs verification).

## Recommended Actions

Creditors and rights-holders should take the following steps immediately: (**1**) In any pending Chapter 11 plan with third-party release provisions, submit written objections specifically preserving *Purdue Pharma* arguments before any confirmation hearing, and monitor the Third Circuit's *SAS AB* certification for a binding definition of "consent" that will govern opt-out adequacy across the circuit; (**2**) UCC members and fee-objection parties in cases with professional-fee applications pending should benchmark against the Envision fee-examiner's methodology — specifically, the examiner's per-category excess framework — when preparing § 330 objections, as Judge Isgur's June 3 ruling will set a persuasive S.D. Tex. standard; (**3**) Yellow GUC claimants should not sell secondary-market positions until the July 14–15 evidentiary hearing on the Teamsters' $1.42 billion ERISA withdrawal claim resolves, as current secondary pricing may not fully discount the dilutive scenario; (**4**) Parties in reorganized-debtor structures within the Third Circuit should audit their plan exculpation clauses for temporal and conduct scope in light of Judge Kaplan's *Rite Aid* ruling denying dismissal on day-97 post-effective-date conduct — arrange independent post-effective-date governance structures now rather than rely on exculpation language that has now been construed narrowly; (**5**) FTX claimants in Class 5 or 6 who have not yet completed their KYC/AML verification through the plan administrator portal (kroll.com/FTX) should do so immediately, as the Second Distribution Window closes June 30, 2026 and unclaimed distributions revert to the estate under the plan's reversion mechanic; and (**6**) PREPA bondholders — particularly uninsured holders of legacy revenue bonds — should model the $0.31 flat recovery against any current secondary-market prices and evaluate whether the guarantor-subrogation track available to insured holders provides a more favorable recovery path worth pursuing via direct guarantor-claim litigation if the PSA collapses.
