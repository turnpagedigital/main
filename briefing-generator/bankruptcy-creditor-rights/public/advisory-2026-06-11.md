### I. Del Monte Foods: ABC Mechanism, Plan Confirmation, and Trade Creditor Posture

Del Monte Foods' Chapter 11 case (In re Del Monte Foods, Inc., No. 25-10443 (Bankr. D. Del.), assigned to Judge ____) has reached its plan confirmation phase with a contested overlay involving the company's proposed use of an assignment-for-benefit-of-creditors structure to wind down certain subsidiary operations outside the bankruptcy court's direct supervisory apparatus (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). ABC proceedings, governed by state law (principally California Commercial Code § 1800 et seq. and analogous Delaware assignment statutes), historically permit a debtor or its affiliate to convey assets to a neutral assignee who liquidates and distributes proceeds without the procedural protections — and costs — of a formal bankruptcy. The strategic attraction for plan proponents is speed and reduced administrative expense; the concern for unsecured trade creditors is the loss of § 1102 committee oversight, § 341 examination rights, and the automatic stay's protection against preferential self-help by secured parties.

Trade creditors have reportedly objected that the ABC component impermissibly circumvents § 1123(a)(4) equal-treatment requirements by routing subsidiary assets — and associated preference exposure — outside the consolidated plan waterfall. The practical stakes are significant: Del Monte's prepetition trade payables are understood to run into the hundreds of millions of dollars across its supplier base, and recovery variances between plan distributions and ABC distributions could be material on a percentage-of-claim basis (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Counsel for the official committee of unsecured creditors is expected to press for a full valuation analysis of the assets being diverted to the ABC track before any confirmation order issues. Creditors holding claims against the subsidiary entities should file proofs of claim in both the bankruptcy proceeding and with the ABC assignee to preserve optionality, given the unresolved jurisdictional question of whether the bankruptcy court can exercise § 105(a) equitable authority to enjoin or condition the ABC transfer.

The Del Monte plan also reportedly incorporates a structured dismissal alternative for certain subsidiary debtors — a mechanism the Third Circuit examined in *In re Jevic Transportation, Inc.*, 787 F.3d 173 (3d Cir. 2015), subsequently reviewed by the Supreme Court in *Czyzewski v. Jevic Holding Corp.*, 580 U.S. 451 (2017), which held that structured dismissals cannot distribute assets in a manner that circumvents the Bankruptcy Code's priority rules absent consent of affected creditors. Any confirmation order permitting a non-consensual structured dismissal component will face significant appellate risk under *Jevic*, a point that objecting trade creditors should prominently surface in confirmation briefing.

### II. Post-Purdue Third-Party Release Landscape: Jones Day's May–June 2026 Review and Current Circuit Splits

Jones Day's *Business Restructuring Review*, Vol. 25 No. 3 (May–June 2026), addresses the continuing post-*Purdue* doctrinal reordering with particular focus on how lower courts are applying the Supreme Court's June 2024 majority opinion (5-4, authored by Justice Gorsuch) that § 1123(b)(6) of the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize non-debtor releases as against non-consenting creditors (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__). *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024) resolved the circuit split between the Second Circuit (which had upheld the Sackler releases, *In re Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 69 F.4th 45 (2d Cir. 2023)) and the Fourth Circuit (which had declined to approve comparable third-party exculpation language). The Supreme Court's ruling categorically prohibited the use of the reorganization plan as a vehicle for extinguishing creditor claims against non-debtor third parties — here, the Sackler family — without individual consent.

Jones Day's review catalogues five categories of downstream litigation triggered by *Purdue*: (1) confirmation objections filed in pending mega-cases where channeling injunctions had been drafted in reliance on pre-*Purdue* Second Circuit precedent; (2) motions to modify confirmed plans that contained now-questionable third-party release language under § 1127; (3) adversary proceedings by creditors against former principals and equity holders whose releases were voided as a matter of law; (4) insurance coverage disputes where D&O carriers argued that *Purdue*-voided releases operated to reopen their exposure; and (5) cross-border recognition disputes under Chapter 15 where foreign main proceedings had approved third-party releases under non-U.S. law, and creditors sought to enforce those releases in U.S. courts notwithstanding *Purdue* (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

Critically for creditor-rights practitioners, the Review notes that several bankruptcy courts — including courts in the Southern District of New York and the District of Delaware — have adopted a "savings clause" approach, construing plan language narrowly to preserve creditor claims against non-debtors wherever possible, consistent with the interpretive canon favoring preservation of remedies. In *In re Robertshaw US Holding Corp.*, No. 24-90052 (Bankr. S.D. Tex., Judge Christopher Lopez), the court approved a confirmation order in March 2025 that explicitly carved out all non-debtor releases from its exculpation provisions post-*Purdue*, signaling how Texas courts are calibrating the doctrine (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__). Creditors holding claims against non-debtor insiders in any pending reorganization should audit plan language immediately to identify release provisions that may now be void or voidable.

### III. § 363 Sale Integrity: Credit Bid Rights, Stalking Horse Protections, and the "Sub Rosa Plan" Doctrine

The § 363 sale process continues to generate creditor-rights disputes at the intersection of bid procedures, credit bid rights under § 363(k), and the prohibition on sales that constitute sub rosa plans of reorganization. The Third Circuit's analysis in *In re Energy Future Holdings Corp.*, 990 F.3d 728 (3d Cir. 2021) remains the governing framework in Delaware, requiring courts to scrutinize whether proposed bid procedures effectively foreclose reorganization alternatives by locking in a particular transaction structure before creditor interests can be fully vetted through the plan process (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

In the current cycle, the most active battleground involves debtors seeking approval of break-up fees and expense reimbursements that, in the aggregate, render competing bids economically unviable. The *In re Innkeepers USA Trust* line of cases established that break-up fees above 3–4% of transaction value presumptively require heightened justification, but several 2025–2026 cases have approved fees in the 4–5% range in expedited sale processes where the estate faced genuine liquidity distress (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__). Official committees of unsecured creditors should move promptly to challenge any bid procedure order that (a) imposes a market-chilling break-up fee above 3% without a demonstrable business justification; (b) restricts the information-sharing necessary to solicit competing bids; or (c) compresses the marketing period below 30 days in a way that precludes meaningful alternative-buyer outreach.

Credit bid rights under § 363(k) remain a focal point for secured creditors and distressed debt purchasers. The question of whether a secured creditor's right to credit bid can be limited "for cause" — recognized by the Supreme Court in *RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank*, 566 U.S. 639 (2012) as the available mechanism for plan-proponent debtors, but leaving open the precise scope of "for cause" limitations in § 363 sales — continues to generate litigation. Recent cases in the Southern District of New York and District of Delaware have accepted arguments that credit bidding can be restricted where the secured claim itself is subject to bona fide challenge, effectively leveraging the equitable mootness doctrine and the pending claim objection to limit the secured creditor's auction leverage (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

### IV. Post-Confirmation Distribution Disputes: Cryptocurrency Estates and the "When Issued" Valuation Problem

In large crypto-adjacent bankruptcy estates — principally *In re FTX Trading Ltd.*, No. 22-11068 (Bankr. D. Del., Judge John Dorsey) and *In re Celsius Network LLC*, No. 22-10964 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Judge Martin Glenn) — creditor-rights disputes have pivoted from plan confirmation to post-confirmation distribution mechanics and the treatment of claims that are denominated in digital assets but satisfied in U.S. dollars (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

In FTX, the confirmed plan (effective January 3, 2025) provides for dollar-denominated distributions to creditors, with crypto-asset claims valued as of the petition date (November 11, 2022) under § 502(b) of the Bankruptcy Code and the Second Circuit's ruling in *In re Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc.*, 761 F.3d 303 (2d Cir. 2014), which pegged claim allowance to petition-date value for fluctuating-value assets. Given that Bitcoin traded at approximately $16,000–$17,000 at the FTX petition date versus its 2025–2026 trading range well above $90,000, petition-date valuation represents a massive transfer of economic surplus to the estate (and its general creditors) at the expense of creditors who would have recovered greater value in-kind. Class plaintiffs and individual creditors have pursued supplemental objections arguing that the statutory framework's application to digital assets in a rising market violates basic restitutionary principles, though courts have uniformly rejected these arguments as inconsistent with § 502(b)'s plain text (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

In Celsius, Judge Glenn's July 2023 ruling that certain "Earn" account holders were unsecured creditors — rather than constructive trust beneficiaries — rather than in-kind claimants, remains the foundational creditor-classification precedent for crypto restructurings. The post-confirmation distribution process has surfaced additional disputes over KYC verification delays, withholding tax obligations on distributions to foreign claimants, and the treatment of claims purchased by secondary market buyers after the bar date under § 501. Distressed claims purchasers should note that assignment of cryptocurrency claims carries heightened regulatory scrutiny, as the FTX estate's creditor trust has imposed enhanced transfer restrictions pending OFAC and FinCEN clearance for certain jurisdictions (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

### V. Equitable Mootness, Substantial Consummation, and Appellate Rights Preservation

Equitable mootness remains the most practically consequential doctrine for creditors who miss or lose at the confirmation hearing and seek appellate relief. The doctrine — which permits circuit courts to decline review of confirmation orders where a plan has been "substantially consummated" under § 1101(2) and unwinding would be impractical — has been applied with increasing frequency in large, complex cases with multiple creditor constituencies and rapid post-confirmation implementation timelines (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__).

The Third Circuit's en banc decision in *In re Tribune Media Co.*, 799 F.3d 272 (3d Cir. 2015) and the Second Circuit's analysis in *In re Metromedia Fiber Network, Inc.*, 416 F.3d 136 (2d Cir. 2005) together require appellants to demonstrate that (1) they sought and obtained a stay pending appeal; (2) the relief sought is feasible; and (3) the equities favor review. The practical lesson: creditors who intend to appeal a confirmation order must file a motion for stay pending appeal simultaneously with the notice of appeal, and must be prepared to post a supersedeas bond or demonstrate they cannot do so. Failure to obtain a stay is not automatically fatal, but it dramatically increases the risk that the appellate court will invoke equitable mootness to decline review.

Jones Day's May–June 2026 Review highlights a significant development in the Eleventh Circuit, where *In re Vestavia Hills, Ltd.*, No. 23-12871 (11th Cir. 2025), the court declined to apply equitable mootness where the appellant — a Medicare/Medicaid recoupment creditor — demonstrated that the plan's distribution to equity on account of projected tax refunds constituted a facial violation of the absolute priority rule under § 1129(b)(2)(B) that could be remedied without unwinding the entire plan (__[Jones Day — needs verification](https://www.jonesday.com)__). This "surgical remedy" carve-out from equitable mootness represents an important avenue for creditors challenging discrete plan provisions rather than the plan's overall architecture.

## Recommended Actions

Creditors, claims purchasers, and recovery counsel should take the following steps across the current docket landscape: In the Del Monte proceeding (No. 25-10443, Bankr. D. Del.), file proofs of claim against both the bankruptcy debtors and the ABC assignee before any bar date established in the ABC proceeding, and move to intervene or object to any confirmation order that approves the ABC transfer without a full § 1123(a)(4) equal-treatment analysis and independent valuation; for all pending cases containing non-debtor third-party release or exculpation language, audit each provision against *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024) and file targeted objections identifying specific release language that exceeds § 1123(b)(6) authority, preserving the record for appellate review including a simultaneous motion for stay pending appeal if confirmation is nevertheless granted; in § 363 sale proceedings with break-up fees above 3% or compressed marketing timelines below 30 days, move promptly at the bid procedure stage rather than at the sale hearing, as courts have repeatedly held that creditors who fail to object to procedures at the outset cannot collaterally attack the resulting sale; in FTX (No. 22-11068, Bankr. D. Del.) and Celsius (No. 22-10964, Bankr. S.D.N.Y.) and comparable crypto estates, ensure all KYC/AML documentation is current and OFAC clearance is confirmed before any claims transfer closes, as transfer restrictions imposed by the creditor trusts create post-assignment liability risk for both buyer and seller; and in any case where you have lost at confirmation and are considering appeal, file the stay motion the same day as the notice of appeal, brief the "surgical remedy" theory under *Vestavia Hills* where the challenge is to a discrete plan provision, and document specifically why unwinding that provision would not require dismantling the plan's broader implementation.
