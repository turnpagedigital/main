### BlockFills (★ KEY FOCUS) — Sale Closes; Post-Confirmation Recovery Clock Starts

The dominant development this week is the consummation of the Keyrock-BlockFills asset sale, converting what had been a dual-track restructuring into a confirmation-and-wind-down posture. 
Keyrock has completed its acquisition of BlockFills' institutional trading and brokerage operations.
 
According to a bankruptcy filing, Keyrock agreed to pay $3.25 million for substantially all of BlockFills' assets, while assuming certain liabilities, equity interests, customer relationships, and proprietary technology.
 
A Delaware bankruptcy judge agreed to approve the $3.25 million sale of cryptocurrency financial technology firm BlockFills to a Belgian digital asset investment group as BlockFills prepared for a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing.
 The sale approval occurred at the June 16 hearing before the Honorable Thomas M. Horan (Bankr. D. Del. No. 26-10371-TMH), and the transaction has now closed — meaning the estate's primary operating asset is transferred and the remaining case work centers on plan confirmation, liquidating trust mechanics, and creditor recovery quantum.

The creditor posture here is structurally grim. 
The first-day record identifies roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and about $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.
 A $3.25 million gross sale price against $145 million in general unsecured claims implies a fractional recovery — almost certainly sub-3 cents on the dollar from sale proceeds alone — before any estate causes of action or residual asset liquidation. The plan's value to general unsecured creditors will therefore depend almost entirely on (a) the liquidating trust's pursuit of pre-petition fraudulent-transfer and preference claims under 11 U.S.C. §§ 547–548, (b) professional fee management between now and plan effective date, and (c) resolution of the contested customer-property question.

### BlockFills — The Property-of-the-Estate Question: Carried Forward as Live Risk

The customer-property dispute that animated the first weeks of this case has not been mooted by the sale — it will define how much is available for distribution under the liquidating trust. The facts established in the first-day record remain materially adverse to customers asserting bailment or trust arguments.


At the first-day hearing, debtors' counsel confirmed in open court that customer funds "have always been commingled with company funds and other customer funds," and that the Debtors' position is that all such funds constitute property of the estate.
 
The BlockFills Client Agreement contains three separate provisions explicitly disclaiming any fiduciary relationship, and the agreement authorizes BlockFills to transfer margin to exchanges and clearing houses at its sole discretion, with customers ranking "only as a general creditor" upon third-party default.
 
As to commingling, on January 16, 2026, Alberta transferred 40.001 BTC and 650,050 USDC to BlockFills; these assets were swept from the deposit wallet within minutes and the wallet was empty within 1.5 hours. On February 11, 2026, the Debtors' representatives disclosed to clients that digital assets were "not segregated per client," "not segregated on separate wallets per customer," and were commingled into "one balance sheet."


This fact pattern maps almost precisely onto the Celsius/Mashinsky saga and the HKCFI's analysis in Gatecoin (HKCFI 914): where there is no segregation, no identifiable trust res, and an express contractual disclaimer of fiduciary duty, most courts applying 11 U.S.C. § 541 have held that commingled assets constitute estate property. On that theory, customers hold general unsecured claims — not a priority property right — and participate in whatever distributable value the liquidating trust generates after secured claims and administrative expenses. The customer plaintiffs in *Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd.* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:26-cv-01672, filed Feb. 27, 2026) and *1548199 Alberta Ltd. et al. v. Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc.* (N.D. Ill. No. 1:26-cv-02451, filed Mar. 5, 2026) remain subordinated to the automatic stay; their TRO-based asset-freeze remedies do not survive as estate-property claims in bankruptcy without an adversary proceeding — and as noted in the Stretto special report, 
the objectors have not filed an adversary proceeding as required by Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 7001(b) for determination of estate-property rights.

### Background on BlockFills' Liquidity Cascade

For institutional creditors who joined the case after the March filing, the causal chain is now well-documented. 
The company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius.
 
Despite mounting balance sheet pressures, the Company paid approximately $12 million in employee bonuses in 2024 against only $900,000 in adjusted profits — a disparity that contributed to the approximately $77–80 million balance sheet deficit that predated the February 2026 market crash.
 
On February 2, 2026, Bitcoin dropped below $80,000 for the first time since April 2025, and BlockFills temporarily suspended certain deposit and withdrawal activity that day in response to liquidity pressure and customer withdrawal requests.


The $12 million bonus-vs.-$900K-profit disparity, combined with the FRCP 7001(b) posture of the customer plaintiffs, suggests the Committee's FTI-led investigation will focus on whether those 2024 bonus payments constitute preferential transfers or fraudulent conveyances — a meaningful potential recovery avenue for unsecured creditors under 11 U.S.C. §§ 547–548.


The Committee members are: SBI VC Trade Co., Ltd.; Dominion Capital LLC; Karol Przybytkowski; Jeffrey Brandt; Tod Skarecky; Energy Conversion Group; and Fuel Labs, Inc.
 
The Committee retained FTI Consulting as financial advisors, effective April 1, 2026.
 FTI's parallel work on the valuation of estate causes of action will be the key driver of ultimate creditor recovery — not the $3.25 million sale price.

### FTX Recovery Trust — Fifth Distribution Imminent (July 31 Deadline)

The FTX estate (Bankr. D. Del. No. 22-11068) is entering its fifth distribution cycle, now directly relevant to claim-trading market participants who purchased BlockFills-era client positions. 
FTX Trading Ltd. and the FTX Recovery Trust announced that the anticipated record date for the next distribution was June 16, 2026, for holders of allowed FTX claims and interests, with the Next Distribution expected to commence on July 31, 2026.
 
For transferred claims, distributions will only be made to the transferee holder of an allowed claim that is processed and reflected on the official register of claims as of the June 16, 2026 record date, where the 21-day notice period has elapsed without objection.



As part of the ongoing claims reconciliation process, FTX filed an amended notice with the Bankruptcy Court seeking approval to reduce the disputed claims reserve by approximately $600 million, decreasing the reserve from $2.4 billion to $1.8 billion; if approved, the adjustment would make additional cash available for creditor distributions.
 
Distributions began in February 2025 with approximately $1.2 billion paid to Convenience Class claimants, followed by larger institutional payouts in May 2025, a third round totaling roughly $1.6 billion last September, and a fourth round launched on March 31.


For secondary-market claim holders, the practical constraint is mechanical: the record date has already passed (June 16). If transfers were not registered and the 21-day objection window cleared before that date, the transferee is not a payee in the July 31 round. That is not an emergency — the estate will run further distribution rounds — but it confirms that the liquidity premium on FTX claims has now normalized into an administrative-calendar arbitrage, not a bankruptcy-outcome bet.

### Cross-Docket Pattern Recognition: Commingling as the Load-Bearing Legal Question

This beat's central analytical thread — whether customer crypto deposits are property of the estate or held in trust — ran through Celsius, Voyager, and BlockFi, and it runs unchanged through BlockFills. The fact pattern across these cases is converging toward a consistent doctrinal outcome: absent express segregation obligations, contemporaneous wallet-level tracing, and a TOS that affirmatively creates a trust or bailment, U.S. bankruptcy courts applying § 541 treat commingled crypto deposits as estate property. The Celsius TOS litigation and the HKCFI's *Gatecoin* analysis (HKCFI 914) both reached similar conclusions under different legal regimes. BlockFills' Client Agreement — with its explicit "general creditor" ranking clause and its documented sweeping of deposited assets within 90 minutes — is arguably the cleanest § 541 fact pattern yet. For creditors hoping to assert trust or bailment claims, the absence of a pending adversary proceeding under FRBP 7001(b) is a procedural bar that compounds the substantive headwinds.
