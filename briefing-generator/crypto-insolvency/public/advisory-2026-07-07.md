### BlockFills (Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc.) — Sale Approved, Confirmation Looming, § 541 Question Unresolved

The arc of this case is now clear, and it is not favorable to unsecured creditors at recovery multiples. 
On March 15, 2026, four Reliz debtor entities filed voluntary Chapter 11 petitions, jointly administered as Case No. 26-10371 before Judge Horan in the District of Delaware.
 
Reliz reported $50 million to $100 million in assets against $100 million to $500 million in liabilities.
 
The first-day record identifies roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and approximately $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.


The genesis of the insolvency is instructive. 
The company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius.
 
On February 19, CoinDesk reported BlockFills had incurred approximately $75 million in losses and was seeking a buyer; on February 25, CoinDesk confirmed the former CEO's departure, reporting that founder Nicholas Hammer had allegedly urged some clients to withdraw assets before the platform froze deposits and withdrawals.


Those pre-petition withdrawal dynamics produced two adversary-adjacent customer lawsuits with immediate TRO consequences. 
Two customer lawsuits followed the suspension: Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd., S.D.N.Y. Case No. 1:26-cv-01672 (filed February 27, 2026), where on March 3, 2026 Judge Mary Kay Vyskocil issued an ex parte TRO freezing 70.56 BTC (~$4.8 million) belonging to Dominion, barring BlockFills from moving assets outside the United States, and ordering an accounting and segregation of customer funds.
 
The second action, 1548199 Alberta Ltd. and Robert J. Bertram v. Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. et al., N.D. Ill. Case No. 1:26-cv-02451 (filed March 5, 2026), asserted similar fraud-based claims against multiple debtor entities and named Interim CEO Perry, former CEO Hammer, and President Wallace; a TRO was also granted there.


The § 541 commingling admission made at the first-day hearing is the single most consequential legal fact in this case. 
At the first-day hearing, debtors' counsel confirmed in open court that customer funds "have always been commingled with company funds and other customer funds," and that the Debtors' position is that all such funds constitute property of the estate.
 That admission effectively forecloses a Celsius-style trust argument under the ToS unless the Committee can distinguish the applicable customer agreements on their face — the same statutory battleground that consumed years of litigation in *In re Celsius Network* (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. Case No. 22-10964). The unsecured creditors here never got that fight adjudicated because the case moved straight to a § 363 sale process.

**The § 363 Sale Track.** 
Reliz operated under final cash-collateral authority while pursuing a dual-track restructuring built around a sale process and a plan path, with a sale hearing set for June 16, 2026 and confirmation targeted for July 13, 2026.
 The auction process was turbulent. 
The docket reflects a notice of cancellation of auction, a notice of further rescheduled auction, and a notice of rescheduled auction before the May 28, 2026 sale hearing date.
 
Keyrock ultimately agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and IP; Keyrock confirmed on May 26, 2026 that it had been declared the Successful Bidder in the official bankruptcy court filing.



The June 12 agenda confirmed that the sale of substantially all assets was going forward at the June 16 hearing, while a protective-order matter was adjourned and sale objections remained from the Chicago Blackhawks Hockey Team and Oracle, per Dkt. 424.
 Judge Horan approved the sale at the June 16 hearing. The $3.25 million headline price against roughly $145 million in unsecured obligations implies asset coverage of roughly 2.2 cents on the dollar on the sale proceeds alone — the plan will need to layer in adversary-proceeding recoveries, claim liquidation, and insider fraudulent-transfer actions to move that number.

**Adversary Proceedings and Committee Activity.** 
The Debtors filed Adv. Pro. No. 26-50230 (TMH) against Artha Investment Partners LLC, with a summons issued April 9, 2026.
 
On March 27, 2026, the U.S. Trustee for Region 3 filed the Notice of Appointment of the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors at Dkt. 104; Committee members include SBI VC Trade Co., Ltd., Dominion Capital LLC, Karol Przybytkowski, Jeffrey Brandt, Tod Skarecky, Energy Conversion Group, and Fuel Labs, Inc.
 
The Committee retained FTI Consulting as its financial advisor effective April 1, 2026, authorized at Dkt. 264.


**Plan Mechanics and Confirmation Timeline.** 
On April 7, 2026, the Debtors filed the Joint Chapter 11 Plan at Dkt. 128 and the Disclosure Statement at a contemporaneous docket entry, with a hearing set for May 12, 2026.
 
Judge Horan granted conditional approval of the disclosure statement, deferring objections to the opt-out release mechanism for argument at confirmation.
 The Veritaglobal key-dates calendar shows the next hard court date as 
September 11, 2026 (4:00 p.m. ET)
 — which the tracker marks as the confirmation hearing, a slip from the original July 13 target.

That three-month slip from the original confirmation target is itself informative. It likely reflects extended balloting, plan objections from the Committee or individual creditors on the release mechanism, and the need to resolve outstanding adversary proceedings or at least establish their scope before any plan goes effective. Creditors should treat September 11 as a soft date until an order setting a final confirmation hearing is entered.

**Keyrock's Post-Acquisition Risk Profile.** The sale transfers customer lists, proprietary technology (Vision Trader platform), and substantially all operating assets. 
The acquisition came months after Keyrock raised a Series C round led by SC Ventures, Standard Chartered's venture capital arm, at a $1.1 billion valuation.
 What does not transfer is the litigation estate — adversary proceedings, fraudulent-transfer claims against insiders, and the unresolved Alberta/Dominion TRO litigation remain in the estate for the benefit of unsecured creditors. The plan's distribution mechanics, when filed in final form, will determine whether those recoveries flow pari passu to the general unsecured class or are carved out for specific claimants.

The Committee's liquidation plan, filed by Greenberg Traurig on January 30, 2025, remains the operative framework. 
Filed by Greenberg Traurig, LLP, the plan outlines a strategy to maximize value through a Liquidating Trust overseen by an independent trustee; the proposal came after Power Block Coin (operating as SmartFi) filed for bankruptcy on June 20, 2024.
 
The plan provides for the Liquidating Trustee to pursue potential legal claims, and the Committee's filing raises concerns about pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning insider transactions — including a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC, an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah, with the Committee expressing concern about the loan's unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property.


**SmartFi Account Classification Problem.** The underlying product taxonomy here mirrors the Celsius earn-account dispute. 
The Debtor's plan proposes to allow potentially eligible buyback-guarantee claimants to vote and either keep their tokens or, after an eligibility analysis, receive an allowed general unsecured claim in exchange for their tokens.
 
The precise nature and terms of the SmartINTEREST accounts remains unclear; the Court noted it was never presented with specific terms or an "agreement governing the SmartINTEREST accounts" beyond certain parts of the SmartFi Terms of Use.
 That ambiguity on account classification — bailment vs. loan vs. general unsecured — is the same § 541 question that drove the Celsius litigation, and it remains unresolved here, directly affecting whether SmartINTEREST depositors participate in the liquidating trust as secured or unsecured claimants.

### Statutory Framework: The Persistent § 541 and § 363 Tension in Crypto Estates

The three dockets tracked here — BlockFills, Power Block Coin, and the FTX/Genesis satellite litigation — collectively illustrate a recurring structural failure in institutional crypto: customer assets are commingled operationally but marketed as segregated or custodied, leaving the § 541 property-of-the-estate determination to be resolved in bankruptcy rather than proactively by contract or regulatory structure. In BlockFills, the debtor's own counsel conceded commingling at the first-day hearing. In Power Block Coin/SmartFi, the Court noted it lacked the contractual record to characterize SmartINTEREST accounts. In FTX/Celsius, years of litigation turned on whether account-holder agreements created a bailment or a debtor-creditor relationship.

The statutory stakes: if customer deposits are property of the estate under § 541, they flow into the general unsecured class and compete pari passu with trade creditors. If they are held in trust or bailment — the argument advanced in *Gatecoin, HKCFI 914* and partially in *In re Celsius* with respect to custody accounts — they are not property of the estate and must be returned in kind, prior to any general distribution. The BlockFills court never reached that question; the sale mooted the immediate issue but the plan's distribution mechanics will encode the debtor's answer, and the Committee retains standing to object if that answer undervalues the trust claim.
