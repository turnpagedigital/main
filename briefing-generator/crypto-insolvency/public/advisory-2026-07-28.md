# 🪙 Crypto Insolvency | Tuesday, July 28, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

### Lede: The July Cluster — Four Failures in Seven Days Crystallize a Structural Inflection

The past week produced the most concentrated burst of crypto-sector insolvency activity since the 2022 collapse wave. 
Movement Labs and Storj Labs filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, marking the third and fourth crypto-related company failures, respectively, in seven days as investor capital shifts heavily toward artificial intelligence.
 
Three centralized crypto exchanges — AscendEX, BitMEX, and BitMart — announced shutdowns in July 2026 alone.
 The distress pattern is structural, not idiosyncratic: 
collapsing retail trading volumes and rising regulatory costs are squeezing smaller crypto firms, and analysts say only large, well-capitalized exchanges with strong compliance, transparent reserves, and diversified services are likely to survive as retail speculation fades and rules like the EU's MiCA take hold.
 None of these failures, taken individually, reshapes creditor-rights doctrine — but the clustering matters for recovery posture, claim-trading dynamics, and the question of whether institutional counterparty exposure is now a systemic rather than case-by-case concern.

### BlockFills / Reliz Technology Group Holdings — Post-Confirmation Posture (★ KEY FOCUS)

The BlockFills case, which had been the most analytically significant open docket on this beat since its March 15 petition, appears to have cleared its confirmation milestone — though the docket's last recorded filing was July 24 (Docket Entry 538) with no new entries in the 72 hours before this run. The trajectory is clear from the record. 
On May 28, 2026, Judge Horan entered the order approving the disclosure statement, scheduling the confirmation hearing, and establishing procedures for objections, claim objections, temporary allowance, solicitation packages, and voting deadlines (Docket No. 388).
 
The voting deadline and confirmation objection deadline were set at July 1, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET, and the confirmation hearing was scheduled for July 13, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET.


The underlying capital structure remains the controlling frame for recovery analysis. 
The capital structure is concentrated in customer and trade exposure rather than traditional funded debt; the first-day record identifies roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and approximately $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.
 
Reliz Technology Group Holdings, Inc. and three affiliated entities filed for Chapter 11 protection on March 15, 2026 (the "Petition Date") in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, reporting $50 million to $100 million in assets and $100 million to $500 million in liabilities.


The structural ambiguity at the center of this case — the one that echoes Celsius and every other post-2022 crypto brokerage insolvency — is the § 541 property-of-the-estate question. 
Four debtor entities operating as BlockFills are pursuing a dual-track restructuring through a customer-led NewCo transaction and a competitive Section 363 sale process, while the central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remains contested.
 The debtors' liquidity crisis traces directly to cascading counterparty failures: 
the company attributes its liquidity crisis to an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy, alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius, compounded by stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials.


Pre-petition litigation adds complexity for any potential plan injunction. 
In the weeks immediately preceding the Chapter 11 filing, certain debtor entities and current or former directors and officers were named in two lawsuits asserting allegations including misappropriation of customer funds, conversion, breach of contract, and other fraud-based claims: *Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd.*, Case No. 1:26-cv-01672 (S.D.N.Y., filed Feb. 27, 2026)
 and 
*1548199 Alberta Ltd. and Robert J. Bertram v. Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.*, Case No. 26-cv-02451 (N.D. Ill., March 5, 2026).
 
The courts in both matters granted temporary restraining orders that restricted the debtors' ability to conduct operations and exercise control over certain assets; the automatic stay afforded by Chapter 11 was intended to halt further litigation and prevent individual customers from pursuing assets outside the bankruptcy process.


The Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors retained FTI Consulting as financial advisor effective April 1, 2026, per Docket No. 264. (__[Veritaglobal.net — Docket No. 264](https://veritaglobal.net/blockfills/document/2610371260429000000000006)__)

The docket is quiet this week — no new entries in 72 hours as of the July 27 sync. My read: the case is either in the post-confirmation implementation phase or, if confirmation encountered obstacles at the July 13 hearing, in a brief adjournment posture. ### Storj Labs — New Filing: Bk N.D. W.Va., Case No. 5:26-bk-00512 (July 26, 2026)

The most material new development this cycle is the Storj Labs Chapter 11 filing. 
Storj Labs, Inc. voluntarily filed Chapter 11 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Northern District of West Virginia, Case No. 5:26-bk-00512 (Bk N.D. W.Va.), to address certain legacy obligations while preserving Storj's ongoing business operations; the company will continue operating in the ordinary course throughout the Chapter 11 process.


The judge assignment and first-day counsel are not yet confirmed in available public records. The debtor's stated rationale: 
the financial difficulties trace to legacy liabilities connected to previous business operations; Kaloyan Raev, director of software engineering, characterized the current business as operationally sound and appropriately sized, but burdened by historical financial obligations.
 
Prior to this bankruptcy petition, Storj Labs had raised approximately $35 million in capital.


The reorganization framework is noteworthy — and legally novel enough to watch closely. 
The restructuring plan proposes sharing ownership of the reorganized company among management, investors, and token holders, who typically receive nothing in a Chapter 11 process.
 The mechanics are still undefined: 
management has announced intentions to develop a framework enabling STORJ token holders to acquire equity stakes in the post-reorganization entity, with a proposed ownership structure potentially including existing management, current investors, community stakeholders, and new capital partners — but details regarding eligibility criteria, token snapshot requirements, lockup provisions, or the percentage of equity allocated remain undisclosed.


The Inveniam backstory is relevant to the § 363 sale question. 
Inveniam announced its plan to acquire Storj Labs in October 2025 — roughly nine months before the Chapter 11 filing; under the original agreement, Storj would continue as a separate legal entity operating as an Inveniam subsidiary, with existing customer, supplier, and community relationships preserved — and that acquisition framework now runs through the bankruptcy court.
 
Parent company Inveniam continues to support the business, while Storj is focusing on its core offering and disposing of earlier acquisitions and non-essential operations as part of the restructuring process.


Token market impact: 
following the announcement, the STORJ token plunged as much as 16% to $0.06, per CoinGecko.
 The creditor pool composition is not yet public — the first-day schedules have not been surfaced in available records. Any creditor with an executory contract, node-operator payment obligation, or token-related claim should treat this as a new active matter. 
Any restructuring plan requires approval from creditors and final authorization from the bankruptcy court.

### Movement Labs — Subchapter V Filing (Del., July 15, 2026): Small Case, Big Signal


Court records show the voluntary petition was filed on July 15, 2026, under Subchapter V — a restructuring process designed for qualifying small businesses — reporting assets ranging from $100,001 to $500,000 and liabilities of up to $10 million, with hundreds of potential creditors listed.
 The balance sheet is small; the governance backstory is not. 
Binance said the market maker rapidly sold 66 million MOVE tokens, or about 5% of the total supply, contributing to a sharp decline in the token's price; Coinbase also suspended MOVE trading after saying the token no longer met its listing standards.
 
The largest creditor is co-founder Rushi Manche, with a claim of more than $1.6 million; he retained a 34.25% stake in the company.
 That insider-creditor dynamic — where the founder holds both a major claim and an equity interest — creates obvious § 502 and § 1129 confirmation pressure points. 
Creditors have until September 14, 2026, to file claims, and the court expects a Movement Labs restructuring plan by October 13, 2026.

### Exchange Wind-Downs: BitMEX and BitMart — Insolvency Risk vs. Orderly Closure

Both BitMEX and BitMart have explicitly represented solvency, which distinguishes them from the bankruptcy filings above — but the AscendEX situation is a cautionary counterexample. 
BitMEX and BitMart both explicitly say they are solvent and are announcing orderly wind-downs where customer assets exceed liabilities and users can still withdraw.
 
BitMEX announced on July 23 that it will permanently close on September 23 after 11 years of operation, with daily volume reportedly down to roughly $400,000.
 
Its parent HDR Global Trading says the platform is not insolvent, customer assets exceed liabilities, and the closure follows a strategic review that came after approximately $200 million in regulatory penalties and a sale process that produced no buyer.


The AscendEX closure is the more troubling data point for creditors. 
AscendEX shut down on July 1 after failing to secure authorization under the EU's MiCA regulation — though on-chain investigator ZachXBT had flagged withdrawal delays and near-empty hot wallets weeks before the closure — and as of late July, AscendEX users still have no guarantee they'll recover their full balances.
 The gap between a represented solvent wind-down and the actual recovery experience for retail users is precisely the issue that the Celsius and Voyager cases litigated at length under § 541 and § 363. Creditors with balances on any winding-down platform should initiate withdrawal processes immediately and not rely on platform representations absent independent verification.

### Pattern Recognition: The § 541 / Customer-Property Question Across the Cluster

The through-line across this week's new filings and the BlockFills posture is the same legal question that defined the 2022–2024 wave: are customer deposits property of the debtor's estate under 11 U.S.C. § 541, or do they travel outside the estate via trust, bailment, or constructive trust theories? In BlockFills, the dual-track plan attempts to resolve this by routing customer assets into a NewCo structure rather than litigating the § 541 boundary directly. In Storj, the token-equity conversion proposal creates a novel wrinkle: token holders are being offered equity in the reorganized entity rather than cash recovery — a structure that has no established precedent in crypto Chapter 11 cases and will require careful scrutiny under § 1129(a)(7) (best-interests test) and § 1129(b) (cramdown requirements) before confirmation. Creditors with fiat or crypto deposit claims should not assume that an equity conversion offer satisfies their entitlement under the absolute priority rule absent full disclosure of the post-confirmation entity's value.
