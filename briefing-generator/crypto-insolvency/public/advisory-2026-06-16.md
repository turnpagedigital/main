## Analysis & Developments

### LEDE — BlockFills/Reliz § 363 Sale Hearing: Today's Delta

The most consequential development in the crypto-insolvency docket today lands squarely in *In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.* (d/b/a BlockFills), Case No. **26-10371 (TMH)**, Bankr. D. Del., before the Honorable **Thomas M. Horan**. Brussels-based Keyrock was acquiring bankrupt crypto trading and lending firm BlockFills for $3.25 million, subject to court approval — and today was the moment of reckoning. The June 12 hearing agenda (Dkt. 424) confirmed that the sale of substantially all assets was going forward at the June 16 hearing, while a protective-order matter was adjourned and sale objections remained from Chicago Blackhawk Hockey Team and Oracle. Law360 reported this morning that BlockFills received court approval for the $3.25 million Chapter 11 sale — the first major § 363 disposition in the 2026 crypto-bankruptcy cycle to reach the finish line in the District of Delaware.

The sale architecture deserves careful parsing. According to the bankruptcy filing, Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property. Keyrock's spokesperson confirmed that, as set out in the official bankruptcy court document filed on May 26, 2026, Keyrock SA was declared the "Successful Bidder" for certain assets of Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. and its affiliated debtors. The auction was cancelled — notices of cancellation preceded the rescheduled May 28 auction, which was itself further rescheduled to May 26 when Keyrock emerged as the sole qualified bidder at $3.25 million — a figure that sits at the extreme low end of the $50–$100 million asset range the debtors disclosed at petition. The four debtors filed voluntary Chapter 11 petitions on March 15, 2026; the cases are jointly administered under Case No. 26-10371 before Judge Thomas M. Horan in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware.

The recovery math for general unsecured creditors is sobering. The debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations. The $3.25 million sale proceeds, net of administrative claims and professional fees, represent a recovery of approximately two cents on the dollar against those obligations before accounting for the liquidating trust's pursuit of additional claims. The filing was supported by a pre-negotiated term sheet with an Ad Hoc Group of the company's largest customers, providing the framework for a reorganization plan centered on the creation of a new operating entity and a liquidating trust. The Verita (f/k/a KCC) claims portal confirms a bar date of **September 11, 2026 (4:00 p.m. ET)** for non-customer creditors — counsel should advise any institutional counterparty to file promptly. ### Structural Context: Why the BlockFills Collapse Matters

Chicago-based BlockFills — operated by Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. — filed for Chapter 11 on March 15, 2026 in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, listing estimated assets of $50 million to $100 million and liabilities between $100 million and $500 million. The proximate causes of the collapse were layered and precedential for institutional crypto lenders: on February 11, BlockFills abruptly suspended client deposits and withdrawals, citing "recent market and financial conditions," and later disclosed that customer assets had been commingled with company funds on a single balance sheet, resulting in an estimated $77 million shortfall as of year-end 2025. The primary cause of the collapse was roughly $75 million in lending losses tied to declining collateral values during a market downturn.

The commingling disclosure is the fulcrum of the residual legal exposure. The central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remains contested — a question that will likely survive the § 363 sale and be litigated in the liquidating-trust adversary proceeding context. This is the same structural fault-line that animated the *Celsius* and *FTX* proceedings: under 11 U.S.C. § 541(a), property of the estate is broadly defined, but customer-asset segregation arguments under § 541(b) or constructive-trust theories under applicable state law cut against that breadth. The Special Committee formed on March 12, 2026 — comprised of a disinterested director — was vested with authority to investigate, release, or settle potential claims, including those arising from the commingling. (__[Chapter11cases.com — BlockFills Dual-Track Restructuring](https://chapter11cases.com/blogs/news/blockfills-crypto-brokerage-pursues-dual-track-restructuring-in-delaware)__)

The firm's balance sheet was eroded by cascading counterparty exposure: an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy, alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius. The principal outstanding on BlockFills' Celsius promissory notes as of the petition date was approximately $4.8 million, on which BlockFills had not made a payment since August 2025. Compounding these balance-sheet pressures were stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials. These facts establish the classic pattern of institutional crypto insolvency: lending-desk losses accumulated during a downturn, commingled with customer assets, triggering a run when markets cracked in February 2026. BlockFills represents the first major crypto bankruptcy filing of 2026.

### Keyrock Acquisition: Strategic Calculus and Residual Uncertainty

The acquisition will give Keyrock access to BlockFills' institutional client network, which includes hedge funds, asset managers, market makers, and mining companies. The firm reported trading volume north of $60 billion in 2025 — a 28% increase year-over-year — and served roughly 2,000 institutional clients, ranking it among the more active institutional lending and borrowing desks. For Keyrock, the $3.25 million price tag acquires infrastructure and client relationships at salvage-sale valuation. The move follows a strong series of growth steps for Keyrock: it raised a Series C led by SC Ventures (Standard Chartered's VC arm) at a $1.1 billion valuation, and last autumn acquired Luxembourg fund manager Turing Capital as part of a push into asset and wealth management.

Post-closing regulatory approval remains an independent gate. Keyrock confirmed its successful-bidder status but noted that parties continue to collaborate on the administrative process and that "final completion of the transaction remains subject to final court approval and the appropriate regulatory approvals referenced in Keyrock's bid." The applicable regulatory clearances likely implicate European MiCA authorization requirements given Keyrock's Belgian domicile, as well as any U.S. CFTC registration or exemption that BlockFills maintained for its derivatives-eligible-contract-participant business under 7 U.S.C. § 2(e).

The structural risk inherent in the deal is not trivial. Keyrock is effectively inheriting a client base that experienced the catastrophic failure of BlockFills' lending and borrowing services — integrating these clients involves overcoming immense reputational damage and legal complexity surrounding the initial bankruptcy filing. Objections from Chicago Blackhawk Hockey Team and Oracle — confirmed as live as of the June 12 agenda (Dkt. 424) — signal that at least two material creditors challenged the sale terms or proposed assumption-and-assignment economics. The resolution of those objections at today's hearing will determine whether any cure obligations are baked into the sale order, with downstream consequences for the estate's distributable cash.

### Power Block Coin, LLC — Ongoing Liquidation Track (Case No. 24-23041-CDP, Bankr. D. Utah)

The parallel Utah proceeding, *In re Power Block Coin, LLC* (d/b/a SmartFi), remains on its liquidation trajectory before the Honorable **Cathleen D. Parker** in the Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah. Power Block Coin LLC filed a voluntary Chapter 11 petition on June 20, 2024; the case is being represented by Parsons Behle & Latimer. The tracked-case ground truth confirms docket ID 68873409 with no new filings in the 72 hours preceding today's advisory — the most recent entry is Docket No. 519 (filed June 9, 2026), indicating a measured pace consistent with a case approaching distribution readiness or final trustee administration.

The case's macro posture was shaped by the Unsecured Creditors' Committee's January 30, 2025 liquidation plan, filed by Greenberg Traurig, LLP. The plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million. A key component involves the appointment of a disinterested Liquidating Trustee to manage the company's assets, including pursuing potential legal claims and overseeing distributions to creditors. The committee's filing raised concerns about the company's pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets and questioning several insider transactions, including a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC — an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah — with concerns about its unsecured status and recent encumbrances placed on the underlying property.

Creditors in Power Block Coin should monitor activity around the Celsius Network preference claims (asserted in excess of $133 million, as described in the early-case Subchapter V eligibility litigation) and any adversary proceedings initiated by the Liquidating Trustee. The May 7, 2026 Docket No. 502 — a notice mailing including Celsius Network LLC c/o David Proman in Miami — suggests Celsius-related administration remains live and active. ### Sector-Wide Observations: Crypto Insolvency Landscape, Mid-2026

The BlockFills sale approval and the Power Block Coin liquidation track collectively illustrate the maturation of the 2026 crypto-insolvency cycle. Unlike the *FTX* (Bankr. D. Del., Case No. 22-11068 (JTD)) and *Celsius* (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Case No. 22-10964 (MG)) mega-cases that consumed 2022–2024 with billion-dollar estates and multi-year contested confirmation battles, the 2026 cycle features mid-tier institutional lenders with $100–$500 million liability stacks moving through § 363 processes at compressed timelines — petition to sale approval in under 95 days in the BlockFills case. The structural throughline is identical: commingled assets, lending-book losses tied to crypto-market drawdowns, and a race between customer-restitution advocates and general unsecured creditors over whether digital assets segregated in custody are estate property under § 541.

The Keyrock/BlockFills sale also highlights the emergence of well-capitalized European digital-asset market-makers as acquirers of distressed U.S. institutional crypto infrastructure. With MiCA now fully operative across EU member states and providing regulatory clarity that U.S. equivalents still lack, firms like Keyrock can offer foreign-regulated-entity status as part of their acquisition thesis — a competitive advantage over domestic bidders whose CFTC or state-level money-transmitter compliance posture may be less settled.
