## Analysis & Developments

**BlockFills § 363 Sale Cleared; Plan Confirmation Set for September — The Key Open Question Survives**

The week's principal delta in this beat is the June 16 sale approval in *In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc., et al.* (BlockFills). 
A Delaware bankruptcy judge on Tuesday agreed to approve the $3.25 million sale of cryptocurrency financial technology firm BlockFills to a Belgian digital asset investment group as BlockFills prepares for plan confirmation.
 The buyer is Brussels-based Keyrock SA, a market-maker and digital-asset services firm that was declared the successful bidder following a competitive process. 
According to the bankruptcy filing, Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and will assume "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, some of its equity interests, customer lists, and its proprietary technology and intellectual property.
 Closing remains subject to regulatory approvals referenced in Keyrock's bid. 
The acquisition comes months after Keyrock raised a Series C round led by SC Ventures, Standard Chartered's venture capital arm, at a $1.1 billion valuation.


The sale approval is significant procedurally, but it does not resolve the estate's central creditor-rights dispute. 
From the outset, four debtor entities operating as BlockFills have pursued a dual-track restructuring through a customer-led NewCo transaction and a competitive Section 363 sale process, while the central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remains contested.
 At the first-day hearing, debtors' counsel stated on the record that 
customer funds "have always been commingled with company funds and other customer funds," and that the Debtors' position is that all such funds constitute property of the estate.
 That concession — made voluntarily, in open court — mirrors the dynamic that consumed 18 months of litigation in the Celsius cases. Here, the record starts from an even weaker position for customers: BlockFills was a pure institutional platform with no retail depositor class to generate political pressure, and its institutional counterparties entered under negotiated master agreements that may cut either way on the property-of-the-estate analysis under 11 U.S.C. § 541.

The procedural calendar is now clear. 
On May 28, 2026, the Bankruptcy Court entered the order approving the Disclosure Statement and scheduling the hearing on confirmation of the Amended Joint Chapter 11 Plan (Docket No. 388).
 
The confirmation hearing is set for September 11, 2026, at 4:00 p.m. ET.
 Claims traders and creditors holding positions in the $145 million general unsecured pool now have a fixed confirmation target and must assess ballot strategy against that timeline.

**BlockFills: Balance Sheet, Litigation Background, and Claim Posture**

The origin story matters for claim valuation. 
BlockFills filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy to pursue a customer-led reorganization after a liquidity crisis triggered by the February 2026 crypto market crash, compounding counterparty losses, a failed mining venture, and lawsuits alleging misappropriation of customer assets.
 
Reliz Technology Group Holdings, Inc. and three affiliated entities filed for Chapter 11 protection on March 15, 2026 in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, reporting $50 million to $100 million in assets and $100 million to $500 million in liabilities.
 
The Debtors report approximately $145 million in general unsecured obligations.


The balance-sheet deterioration was triggered in sequence. 
The company attributes its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius.
 
Compounding these balance sheet pressures were stranded capital from a failed 2022 mining hardware deployment and delayed 2024 financials.
 
The company disclosed losses of approximately $75 million leading up to the filing.


Two pre-petition lawsuits created the immediate crisis. 
The first, *Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd.* (S.D.N.Y., Case No. 1:26-cv-01672, filed February 27, 2026), alleged ongoing misappropriation and fraudulent concealment of customer assets. On March 3, 2026, Judge Mary Kay Vyskocil issued an ex parte temporary restraining order freezing 70.56 BTC (approximately $4.8 million) belonging to Dominion, barring BlockFills from moving assets outside the United States, and ordering an accounting and segregation of customer funds.
 
The second, *1548199 Alberta Ltd. and Robert J. Bertram v. Reliz Technology Group Holdings Inc. et al.* (N.D. Ill., Case No. 1:26-cv-02451, filed March 5, 2026), asserted similar claims against multiple Debtor entities and named individuals including Interim CEO Perry, former CEO Hammer, and President Wallace. A temporary restraining order was also granted.
 The automatic stay imposed at filing halted both actions, channeling all asset-tracing and misappropriation claims into the bankruptcy estate.


The filing is supported by a pre-negotiated term sheet with an Ad Hoc Group of the company's largest customers that provides the framework for a reorganization plan centered on the creation of a new operating entity and a liquidating trust.
 That structure — operating entity to Keyrock; residual estate into a liquidating trust — is now confirmed by the § 363 sale order. For creditors not in the Ad Hoc Group, the critical variable is how the liquidating trust's distributable value compares against the $145 million in general unsecured claims once the Keyrock $3.25 million proceeds and any remaining estate assets are tabulated. The gap between reported assets ($50M–$100M) and the sale price ($3.25M) for "substantially all" operating assets signals that most of the asserted asset value was in goodwill, client relationships, and technology — all of which transferred to Keyrock. What remains in the estate for unsecured creditors depends heavily on how the estate resolves the commingling issue and whether any assets are successfully traced and segregated as customer property.

**FTX Recovery Trust Drops $1.15 Billion Clawback Against Genesis Digital Assets — With Prejudice**

A significant development on the FTX recovery front closed this week. 
The FTX Recovery Trust voluntarily dismissed its lawsuit against Bitcoin mining company Genesis Digital Assets Ltd., dropping its attempt to recover $1.15 billion in funds it alleged were fraudulently transferred by FTX's co-founder. The trust dropped all claims with prejudice, according to a Monday filing in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware.
 The case caption is *FTX Recovery Trust v. Genesis Digital Assets Ltd.*, Bankr. D. Del., No. 1:25-ap-52358, dismissal filed June 15, 2026. 
The trust is represented by Sullivan & Cromwell LLP and Landis Rath & Cobb LLP; Genesis Digital was represented by Latham & Watkins LLP and Young Conaway Stargatt & Taylor LLP.


The underlying theory had been substantial. 
The lawsuit alleged Genesis Digital, along with its affiliates and co-founders, received commingled and misappropriated funds from FTX co-founder Sam Bankman-Fried between August 2021 and April 2022.
 
The trust alleged that Bankman-Fried used his personal hedge fund, Alameda Research LLC, to purchase several tranches of shares in the Bitcoin mining company at heavily inflated prices.
 
The trust claimed the mining company's co-founders, Rashit Makhat and Marco Krohn, personally benefited, selling more than $550 million worth of their own shares to Alameda in the process.
 
Genesis Digital, a Cyprus-based company headquartered in Dubai, had moved to dismiss the lawsuit in January.


A with-prejudice dismissal — as opposed to without prejudice — forecloses re-filing and is typically a sign of a negotiated resolution, a judgment that the case was not winnable, or both. 
Counsel for the FTX trust did not immediately respond to a request for comment about why it dropped the suit; counsel for Genesis Digital also did not immediately respond.
 No settlement terms have been publicly disclosed. The trust should be expected to address this in its next status report to the Delaware court. For the FTX creditor population, the loss of a $1.15 billion recovery target — even a contingent one — modestly narrows the ceiling on aggregate distributions. 
With the latest payout, FTX creditors have received approximately $10 billion in returns since bankruptcy proceedings began.
 The GDA action was one of the larger remaining recovery vectors; its closure is a material data point for claim-trading pricing on residual FTX positions.

**Pattern Recognition: The Commingling Concession and Its Precedential Weight**

Three of the six matters this beat tracks — BlockFills, Celsius, and the earlier Voyager proceedings — share a common structural failure: customer assets were commingled with operating funds, and the debtors argued at the outset that commingled assets constitute property of the estate under 11 U.S.C. § 541. The outcomes have been mixed. In Celsius, the court ultimately found that certain custody assets were not property of the estate, but that "Earn" account assets — held under terms-of-service language that purported to transfer title — were. BlockFills' first-day concession puts all customer assets into the estate at the opening position; the absence of a retail depositor class may reduce the political pressure that shaped the Celsius outcome, but institutional creditors typically have better-resourced counsel to litigate the issue.

The statutory question is straightforward to state: does the debtor's contractual right to use and commingle customer assets (as would be found in a typical prime-brokerage or lending agreement) cause legal title to those assets to pass to the debtor under applicable state law, making them "property of the estate" under § 541(a)(1)? Or do trust, bailment, or agency concepts preserve customer ownership? The answer turns on the specific terms of BlockFills' master trading and lending agreements — documents that are in the case record but whose key provisions have not yet been publicly litigated in this case. Creditors and their counsel should obtain and review those agreements before the September 11 confirmation hearing, as the plan's treatment of customer claims will almost certainly incorporate the Debtors' § 541 position.

For offshore analogs: the Hong Kong Court of First Instance's 2023 ruling in *Gatecoin* (HKCFI 914) found that cryptocurrency held in an exchange wallet was held on trust for customers — but that analysis turned on specific language in Gatecoin's user agreement that is not confirmed to be present in BlockFills' institutional master agreements. The BVI and Cayman liquidation frameworks that governed Three Arrows Capital applied similar tracing principles under their respective insolvency statutes (BVI Insolvency Act 2003; Cayman Companies Act Part V), but those offshore entities had no equivalent § 541 construct to navigate.
