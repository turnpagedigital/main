II. BlockFills / In re Reliz Technology Group Holdings — Post-Sale, Post-Confirmation Posture

On March 15, 2026, each of the four Debtors filed voluntary petitions for relief under Chapter 11; the cases are jointly administered under Case No. 26-10371, before Judge Thomas M. Horan in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware.

The debtors reported $50 million to $100 million in assets and $100 million to $500 million in liabilities.

The first-day record identifies roughly $145 million of general unsecured customer and trade obligations and approximately $4.8 million outstanding under Celsius secured promissory notes, with Celsius asserting first-priority liens.

The § 363 sale closed on July 16. Keyrock completed its acquisition of BlockFills' institutional digital asset trading and brokerage business on July 16, 2026; the deal follows BlockFills' Chapter 11 case and expands Keyrock's regulatory footprint into the Cayman Islands and the United Kingdom.

Per bankruptcy filings, Keyrock agreed to a purchase price of $3.25 million and assumed "substantially all" of BlockFills' assets, certain liabilities, equity interests, customer lists, and proprietary technology and intellectual property.

The deal folds in a CIMA-registered entity in the Cayman Islands, acquired outright, plus an FCA-authorized entity in the United Kingdom still awaiting regulatory approval.

The $3.25 million sale price against ~$145 million in general unsecured claims signals a thin recovery pool. The filing was supported by a pre-negotiated term sheet with an Ad Hoc Group of the company's largest customers that provides the framework for a reorganization plan centered on the creation of a new operating entity and a liquidating trust. Confirmation was originally calendared for July 13, 2026. The Confirmation Objection Deadline was July 1, 2026 at 4:00 p.m. ET; the Confirmation Hearing was set for July 13, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET. The docket logged six new entries in the 72 hours through July 29 (CourtListener Docket ID 72494188, fetched July 30, 2026) — the substance of those entries has not been publicly described in available sources and should be reviewed directly.

The core structural dispute — whether customer digital assets held at BlockFills constitute estate property — was flagged at filing and remains the central creditor-rights question. Four debtor entities operating as BlockFills pursued a dual-track restructuring through a customer-led NewCo transaction and a competitive Section 363 sale process, while the central question of whether customer digital assets constitute estate property remained contested. Resolution of that question will determine whether institutional counterparties are general unsecured creditors at pennies on the dollar or retain priority property claims. The Celsius/Glenn precedent (Earn account assets = estate property under the ToS) is the analytical anchor, but BlockFills served a different institutional counterparty profile — hedge funds, broker-dealers, and investment managers — whose agreements may support different property characterizations under §§ 541 and 362.

The company attributed its liquidity crisis to cascading exposure from several counterparty defaults — specifically an $8.5 million impairment in the Babel Finance bankruptcy and a $12 million settlement stemming from the AEXA bankruptcy — alongside an adverse arbitration obligation requiring payments to Celsius. Two pre-petition misappropriation lawsuits also preceded the filing: certain Debtor entities and current or former directors and officers were named in two Customer Actions asserting misappropriation of customer funds, conversion, breach of contract, and fraud-based claims — including Dominion Capital LLC v. Reliz Ltd., Case No. 1:26-cv-01672 (S.D.N.Y., Feb. 27, 2026). These adversarial threads will likely resurface in the liquidating trust's causes-of-action schedule.

V. Cross-Cutting: Valuation-Date Disputes and the § 547 Preference Frontier

Crypto-industry bankruptcies increasingly involve preference actions where account-holders received or moved value shortly before the insolvency event. Valuation questions are acute: which date governs — the petition date or the withdrawal date? And if the transferred value was in a volatile token, how should gains or losses be measured for purposes of a clawback? These issues challenge traditional avoidance frameworks and force adaptation by trustees and creditors alike.

The Celsius damages-framework ruling of July 13, 2026 — now confirmed by multiple whitelisted sources — is the most consequential recent ruling on this question. A New York bankruptcy judge established a first-of-its-kind framework for valuing cryptocurrency in Celsius Network avoidance actions, a ruling that White & Case says will guide nearly 1,000 pending cases and hundreds of defaults. The framework's methodological choices (petition-date pricing vs. distribution-date pricing; dollar-denominated vs. in-kind recovery) will directly govern the quantum of clawback exposure for the hundreds of defendants still in active litigation. Claims-purchasers pricing Celsius avoidance-defendant positions need to update their models.

The GENIUS Act's customer-property rule for stablecoin reserves ( signed July 18 ) does not apply retroactively to the existing insolvency cohort, but it will materially change the opening posture of any crypto-platform insolvency filed after enactment — including platforms that commingled customer stablecoin balances with operating capital.
