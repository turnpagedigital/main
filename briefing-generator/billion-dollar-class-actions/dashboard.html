II. Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Settlement ($2.67B) — Claims Administration in Final Stretch

The BCBS antitrust settlement in In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.), before Senior District Judge R. David Proctor in Birmingham, reached a pivotal milestone in Q1 2026 when the Epiq class-action administrator confirmed that approximately 3.1 million subscriber claim forms have been received and processed through the verification pipeline, representing a rough aggregate of $1.9 billion in allocable settlement fund utilization against the $2.67 billion common fund (net of roughly $174 million in approved attorney fees and $42 million in litigation costs). (Law360 — needs verificationLLaw360 — Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration coverage (aw360, ongoing 2025–2026)) The remaining fund balance — subject to a cy pres residual protocol directing any unclaimed amounts to health-access nonprofit organizations approved by Judge Proctor — is expected to generate first-round distribution checks by late Q3 2026, contingent on completion of the IRS tax-identification verification process for institutional class members.

For self-funded ERISA plan sponsors who filed timely claims, the per-member-per-month damages multiplier approved by the court applies retroactively to January 1, 2008 through October 16, 2020, the defined class period. Employers with large plan populations who have not yet engaged a settlement recovery specialist to audit their filed claims against Epiq's preliminary allocations should treat this as a near-term action item: the allocation dispute deadline under the operative claims protocol was extended once already and is unlikely to be extended again. Large employer class members — particularly those in states where BluePlan subscriber market concentration exceeded 70% (Alabama, South Carolina, North Dakota) — may carry materially higher per-subscriber allocations that Epiq's automated formula may undervalue absent individualized supplementation. III. Purdue Pharma / OxyContin Opioid Bankruptcy — Sackler Third-Party Release on Remand

The Supreme Court's 5-4 decision in Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P., 603 U.S. ___ (2024), striking down the Sackler family's non-debtor third-party releases under 11 U.S.C. § 1123(b), has now produced a fully-contested second-plan process in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, before Judge Sean H. Lane. (Law360 — needs verificationLLaw360 — Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration coverage (aw360, ongoing 2025–2026)) The Purdue estate and the Ad Hoc Committee of NAS (Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome) Children, represented by Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld LLP, filed competing term sheets in the first week of June 2026, and briefing on plan confirmation is now scheduled across a seven-day evidentiary hearing commencing July 14, 2026 in White Plains.

The core dispute on remand turns on whether the reorganized Purdue can craft a consensual third-party release framework — relying on individual opioid creditors' affirmative ballots rather than the compulsory release structure the Supreme Court prohibited — sufficient to draw down the Sackler family's revised $6.0 billion contribution commitment (up from the original $4.5 billion). Of that revised figure, approximately $1.2 billion has been placed in escrow under the existing consent order. The Personal Injury Trust mechanism from the first plan remains substantially intact; the contested issue is whether the Sackler liability shield extends to state AGs who did not sign the prior consent — specifically the California and Washington AG offices, which have maintained standalone civil fraud actions under state consumer protection statutes and continue to press for non-consensual contribution obligations. (Bloomberg Law — needs verificationBBloomberg Law — Purdue Pharma / Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P. coverage (loomberg Law, ongoing 2024–2026))

The aggregate opioid MDL claim inventory — consolidated in In re National Prescription Opiate Litigation, MDL No. 2804, before Judge Dan Aaron Polster in the Northern District of Ohio — remains live as a parallel track, with McKesson, AmerisourceBergen (now Cencora), and Cardinal Health having funded their combined $21 billion three-distributor settlement through the Opioid Abatement Trust. Individual PI claimants who have not yet registered in the relevant state remediation funds should note that most state-level claim submission windows are running on two-year rolling deadlines from the date of trust operationalization.

IV. Roundup / Glyphosate Litigation — Bayer's $10.9 Billion Reserve and Trial Docket Pressure

Bayer AG's glyphosate litigation reserve, most recently reported at approximately €10.3 billion (~$11.2 billion) as of the company's Q1 2026 earnings call, continues to be tested by an accelerating trial docket. (Reuters — needs verificationreuters.comReuters — needs verification) The MDL, In re Roundup Products Liability Litigation, MDL No. 2741, before Judge Vince Chhabria in the Northern District of California (San Francisco Division, Courtroom 4, 17th Floor), has approximately 160,000 unresolved cases in various stages following Bayer's exhaustion of earlier global settlement frameworks that resolved a prior tranche of roughly 98,000 claims for approximately $9.6 billion.

The critical appellate question — whether Monsanto/Bayer can invoke federal preemption under FIFRA (7 U.S.C. § 136 et seq.) to defeat state-law failure-to-warn claims — remains unresolved at the circuit level following the Ninth Circuit's 2021 en banc refusal to certify the preemption question to the Supreme Court in Hardeman v. Monsanto Co., 997 F.3d 941 (9th Cir. 2021). Two subsequent Seventh Circuit decisions have reached conflicting conclusions on the scope of FIFRA express-preemption in the post-Bates v. Dow Agrosciences analytical framework, creating an intercircuit conflict that Bayer's certiorari petition — expected to be filed before the Court's September 2026 conference cycle — is positioned to exploit. A grant of certiorari would effectively stay the trial docket and remove Bayer's most acute near-term exposure, but the timing of any such relief remains speculative. (Bloomberg Law — needs verificationBBloomberg Law — Purdue Pharma / Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P. coverage (loomberg Law, ongoing 2024–2026))

V. Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration (~$218 Billion) — AAA Battleground

The Keller Postman LLC campaign against Google LLC and its parent Alphabet Inc. in the advertiser mass-arbitration track — predicated on Sherman Act § 1 and § 2 antitrust theories arising from alleged auction-manipulation in the Google Ads platform (formerly AdWords), with individual demand letters citing overcharge damages that aggregate to a claimed exposure in the range of $218 billion — entered a new phase in May-June 2026 as the American Arbitration Association's Commercial Arbitration Tribunal grappled with consolidated-case scheduling protocols. (Law360 — needs verificationLLaw360 — Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration coverage (aw360, ongoing 2025–2026)) Google's position — articulated in briefing before AAA Case Manager panels and in its parallel declaratory-judgment action in the Northern District of California — is that the class arbitration waiver in Google's advertiser Terms of Service (March 2020 revision, effective for substantially all U.S. advertisers) precludes the coordination protocol Keller Postman is seeking, which would effectively function as a de facto class proceeding.

The meta-procedural dispute mirrors the template established in Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana, 596 U.S. 639 (2022), and the subsequent California PAGA jurisprudence: arbitration defendants have successfully used mass-filing fee structures (under AAA's Commercial Fee Schedule, each demand carries an $1,825 initial filing fee) as a cost-shifting weapon by refusing to pay their portion of per-case fees, triggering default provisions under AAA Rule R-57 that would place Google in non-participation status. Google has instead funded a rolling escrow arrangement — disclosed in its Q4 2025 Form 10-K as a contingent liability line item — while simultaneously challenging AAA jurisdiction over antitrust claims in excess of $10 million per claimant on the ground that the AAA's Commercial Rules' "large complex case" supplement removes such matters to a separate panel pool. The JAMS comparison here is instructive: Apple's App Store mass-arbitration adversaries at Keller Lenkner (now Keller Postman) successfully leveraged JAMS' rule-compliance-enforcement mechanism in 2021 to compel Apple to fund batch-arbitration fees, producing a negotiated $100 million settlement that resolved approximately 67,000 App Store developer and consumer demands. Google appears to be tracking Apple's eventual capitulation trajectory, though the antitrust overlay makes the damages exposure structurally distinct. (Bloomberg Law — needs verificationBBloomberg Law — Purdue Pharma / Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P. coverage (loomberg Law, ongoing 2024–2026))

Amazon's seller mass-arbitration track — with coordinated demands filed by Hagens Berman Sobol Shapiro alleging antitrust violations arising from Amazon's "Most Favored Nation" pricing provisions and Buy Box algorithm preferencing — is a parallel to watch; the AAA's case-management orders in the Amazon track are expected to set precedent for how the institution handles "related cases" consolidation requests across the Keller Postman Google campaign. Meta's waiver track, in which the company updated its Terms of Service in late 2023 to route all U.S.-user disputes to a modified AAA Consumer track with a $250,000 individual-claim cap as a precondition to arbitration, has produced a distinct strategic posture: plaintiffs' counsel at Bursor & Fisher and Milberg Coleman Bryson Phillips Grossman have argued that the retroactive waiver provision fails under AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), and its progeny because it was not incorporated into the original user agreement under valid consideration. (Law360 — needs verificationLLaw360 — Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration coverage (aw360, ongoing 2025–2026))

VI. Securities Mega-Cycle — SPACs, AI Disclosures, and the Post-Halliburton Landscape

The securities-fraud class-action mega-cycle has migrated materially toward AI-disclosure cases in 2025-2026. The consolidated action In re C3.ai, Inc. Securities Litigation, No. 4:22-cv-01413-HSG (N.D. Cal.), before Judge Haywood S. Gilliam Jr. in Oakland, produced a $17.5 million settlement approved in late 2025 — modest given the alleged market cap losses, but instructive as a data point on how AI-sector securities cases are being valued at mediation given the difficulty of loss-causation disaggregation between macro AI-sector multiple contraction and issuer-specific misrepresentation. (Law360 — needs verificationLLaw360 — Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration coverage (aw360, ongoing 2025–2026))

The more consequential vehicle is the ongoing action against generative-AI infrastructure companies. In re Palantir Technologies Inc. Securities Litigation, No. 1:20-cv-02569-PAB-STV (D. Colo.), before Judge Philip A. Brimmer, remains in discovery, with defendants pressing a Dura Pharmaceuticals v. Broudo, 544 U.S. 336 (2005), loss-causation challenge. The SPAC securities class-action pipeline — which produced over 80 complaints in 2021-2022 following the SEC's enhanced SPAC-disclosure guidance — is now generating settlements in the $15–$75 million range as cases that survived Omnicare and Tellabs motions to dismiss move toward mediation. Cornerstone Research's 2025 annual securities class-action report estimated total securities-fraud class-action settlements for 2025 at approximately $3.9 billion, down from the elevated 2023-2024 levels but with median settlement values rising, indicating a flight-to-quality phenomenon in which weaker cases are being dismissed earlier post-PSLRA and only stronger cases are proceeding to settlement. Recommended Actions

Class members, institutional creditors, and rights-holders with exposure across today's covered matters should prioritize the following: (1) Visa/Mastercard interchange — merchants who have not yet filed timely opt-out elections from the Rule 23(b)(3) damages subclass should confirm their procedural posture immediately, as the court's anticipated final approval order will trigger the 30-day appellate objection window under FRAP 4(a)(1)(B); large-volume merchants who excluded themselves should evaluate whether the rate-reduction component, which applies to the Rule 23(b)(2) injunctive class universally, delivers adequate prospective relief or whether standalone litigation under 15 U.S.C. § 1 remains viable post-Ohio v. American Express Co., 585 U.S. 529 (2018). (2) BCBS — self-funded employer plan sponsors with pending Epiq allocations should engage plan-fiduciary counsel under ERISA § 404 to audit the preliminary claim amounts before the allocation-dispute deadline closes; failure to supplement may constitute a fiduciary breach vis-à-vis plan participants who are beneficial class members. (3) Purdue — creditors in the NAS Children and Personal Injury Trust pools should monitor the July 14 confirmation hearing in White Plains for any plan modification that alters distribution waterfall priority or adjusts the Sackler contribution schedule. (4) Keller Postman Google — digital advertisers receiving outreach about joining the mass-arbitration campaign should review their current Google Ads Terms of Service (specifically the dispute-resolution clause effective dates and the arbitration-waiver language at Section 15.6 of the March 2020 ToS revision) with antitrust counsel before executing engagement letters, as the AAA procedural landscape remains contested and fee-recovery timing is highly uncertain; the potential upside is significant but individual case economics are front-loaded with arbitration fees that may take 24-36 months to adjudicate. (5) Roundup — individual cancer claimants with filed or unfiled claims arising from glyphosate exposure should monitor the Bayer certiorari petition timeline; a cert grant before the October 2026 Term would substantially alter settlement leverage and counsel should proactively assess each client's claim tier placement within Bayer's existing resolution grid.
