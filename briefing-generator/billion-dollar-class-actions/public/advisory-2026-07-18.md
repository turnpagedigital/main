### I. *Hasson v. Comcast Cable Communications* — Fee Motion Filed, Final Approval Hearing Rescheduled to August 5


*Hasson v. Comcast Cable Communications LLC*, No. 2:23-cv-05039-JMY, is pending in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, arising out of an October 2023 cybersecurity breach of Comcast's Xfinity platform.
 
On July 17, 2026, class counsel filed a fee motion urging the court to award approximately $39 million — one-third of the $117.5 million settlement fund — describing the outcome as an "extraordinary result achieved."
 The case is before 
Hon. John M. Younge.


The one-third ask warrants scrutiny. The settlement class is large — 
31.6 million customers who received individual notifications of the October 2023 breach
 — and the fund is correspondingly significant, but a 33% fee on a $117.5 million common fund sits at the high end of what circuit precedent comfortably supports above the $100 million threshold, where courts applying a lodestar cross-check routinely compress the percentage toward 25% or below. Counsel will need a robust lodestar multiplier, consolidated docket complexity (the settlement resolves 24 related Eastern District actions consolidated in March 2024), and strong claims-rate data to anchor the request.

On the settlement timeline: 
the final approval hearing has been rescheduled to August 5, 2026, and the claim deadline extended to September 14, 2026.
 
The August 5 hearing is set for 12:30 p.m. ET at the James A. Byrne U.S. Courthouse, 601 Market Street, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19106.
 
The opt-out and objection deadline has already passed — it was July 1, 2026.
 That window is now closed, which crystallizes the class composition and caps upward exposure from opt-outs ahead of the hearing.

Recovery structure: 
the $117.5 million fund covers cash payments to class members, reimbursement for documented out-of-pocket losses and lost time, identity defense and restoration services, notice and administrative costs, and court-approved attorneys' fees and expenses.
 
Class members with documented losses may seek up to $10,000; those without documentation qualify for an alternative $50 flat payment.
 
The underlying breach exploited a vulnerability known as "Citrix Bleed" (CVE-2023-4966), a flaw in Citrix NetScaler products that permitted hijacking of active user sessions.


The denominator math here is consequential. On a $117.5 million fund serving 31.6 million class members, a flat-payment $50 draw would exhaust the entire net fund if a meaningful fraction of the class files — underscoring why settlement administrators and courts track claims rates closely. With the claim window open through September 14, 2026, counsel and claimants should monitor whether any objectors preserved their objection to the fee request before the July 1 deadline and whether any appeal risk materializes after final approval.

### II. Social Media Addiction MDL — Judge Rogers Rejects Both Penalty Anchors One Month Before AG Trial


A California federal judge overseeing the upcoming state-AG trial over social media addiction claims against Meta took issue with both sides' "extreme" penalty estimates during a pretrial hearing Friday, July 17, characterizing the states' $1.4 trillion proposal as "unreasonable" but Meta's $4 million estimate as "not even a slap on the hand."



The Social Media Addiction MDL is overseen by Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in the Northern District of California.
 
The case is *In Re: Social Media Adolescent Addiction/Personal Injury Products Liability Litigation*, MDL No. 3047.
 The defendants include Meta Platforms, TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat. 
The proceeding also involves YouTube Inc. and TikTok Inc. in the Northern District of California.


Friday's exchange is strategically significant. The court's explicit rejection of the $1.4 trillion ceiling — which California, Colorado, Kentucky, and New Jersey advanced as their penalty computation — signals that Judge Rogers is unlikely to instruct the jury on a per-violation penalty theory that would produce headline exposure anywhere near the state-side estimate. 
Meta had argued that the states were seeking more than $1 trillion in civil penalties based on "unmoored" calculations; the states maintain Meta knowingly designed addictive features that harmed children and teens.
 The court's simultaneous rejection of Meta's $4 million floor suggests she views both anchoring strategies as tactical rather than analytically grounded, and is likely to issue pretrial instructions constraining the penalty framing. That is a meaningful development for settlement valuation: if the jury's penalty reference range is judicially narrowed, the magnitude of a post-trial damages award — and therefore defendants' settlement urgency — shifts accordingly.


A June 25, 2026 ruling upheld a landmark $6 million jury verdict against Meta and YouTube, with the court rejecting their attempts to overturn the decision or win a new trial.
 That bellwether outcome, however modest in absolute dollar terms, established that Section 230 does not categorically immunize platform design choices — a predicate ruling that the AG trial will build on. 
As of July 2026, at least 2,893 claims are pending in the MDL.


The absence of a global settlement offer — despite this volume and trial proximity — may reflect defendants' read that Judge Rogers' pretrial management is running against the states on the penalty theory, reducing settlement urgency in the short term. That calculus could shift rapidly if August trial evidence lands badly.

### III. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* — $1.5 Billion Settlement, Non-Class Counsel Fee Dispute Active

The $1.5 billion AI-copyright settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal., Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín), remains the largest confirmed AI-copyright settlement on the docket and continues to generate post-approval litigation. Per the tracked-case ground truth, docket entry 679 filed July 13, 2026 is Anthropic's opposition brief to an objection, the most recent filing as of this advisory. 
Anthropic has argued that the $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel as part of the $1.5 billion settlement is "far too high," contending there is "scant justification" for the request.


The underlying litigation arc: 
in June 2025, Judge William Alsup — the original presiding judge — issued a pivotal summary judgment ruling holding that Anthropic's use of lawfully acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and protected by fair use, but simultaneously ruling that Anthropic's creation and retention of a "central library" comprised in part of pirated works was not transformative and constituted infringement.
 
By mid-July 2025, a class of U.S. copyright holders whose works Anthropic downloaded from shadow libraries was certified — potentially numbering in the millions — with statutory copyright damages reaching up to $150,000 per infringed work, extending theoretical exposure into the hundreds of billions of dollars.



On September 5, 2025, the parties filed a motion in which Anthropic agreed to pay at least $1.5 billion plus interest, with Anthropic also agreeing to destroy the original files of works obtained from pirated sites and torrent downloads.
 
The settlement provides approximately $3,000 per work (prior to deduction of costs, fees, and expenses) and establishes a $1.5 billion settlement fund.


The non-class counsel fee dispute — Anthropic's July 13 opposition at ECF No. 679 — is the live question. Where multiple plaintiffs' counsel firms contributed to a settlement fund without being designated lead class counsel under Rule 23, courts apply a common-benefit analysis; the $75 million request (~5% of fund) on top of class counsel's own fee petition will require a detailed allocation hearing before final distribution commences.
