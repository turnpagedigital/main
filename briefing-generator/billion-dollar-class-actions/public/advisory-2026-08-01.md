## Analysis & Developments

Two developments broke in the last 48 hours worth tracking. The Second Circuit issued its opinion in the phenylephrine MDL on July 30, delivering a partial revival that narrows — but does not extinguish — consumer claims against CVS, Walgreens, Target, Procter & Gamble, and Walmart. The same day, Judge Rodgers in the Depo-Provera MDL issued a procedural order appointing three replacement pilot plaintiffs, a quiet but consequential move that preserves the post-settlement scaffolding needed to resolve out-of-program claims. ### Phenylephrine MDL — Second Circuit Partially Revives "Maximum Strength" Claims | *Yousefzadeh v. Johnson & Johnson Consumer Inc.*, No. 24-3296 (2d Cir. July 30, 2026)


The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated nearly one hundred class actions and transferred them to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York
, where Judge Brian M. Cogan had dismissed the complaint in full. 
The district court held that the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act expressly preempted the state law claims because the drugs' labels complied with FDA requirements, and that the plaintiffs lacked standing to bring the RICO claim.



On July 31, 2026, the Second Circuit — Judges Raggi, Chin, and Pérez — reinstated in part the class action, ruling that while most claims are preempted because manufacturers adhered to FDA labeling requirements, certain defendants falsely advertised their products as superior to competitor oral nasal decongestants, and revived those comparative-advertising claims.


The practical split: 
the panel revived claims alleging that makers misled consumers by labeling their products as "maximum strength," while finding the bulk of the remaining claims were preempted by federal regulation.
 Named defendants include 
CVS Health Corp., Target Corp., The Procter & Gamble Co., and Walmart Inc.


The preemption bar is consequential. The FDA, notwithstanding mounting scientific consensus that oral phenylephrine is no more effective than a placebo, 
did not remove oral phenylephrine's designation as an effective decongestant under its regulations
 — and that regulatory inaction is what shields the efficacy-fraud theory. What survives is the comparative-strength track: the allegation that calling a phenylephrine product "Maximum Strength" is false specifically because pseudoephedrine-based competitors demonstrably outperform it. 
The argument is that the "Maximum Strength" labeling is false and misleading because phenylephrine is not as strong as other over-the-counter decongestants such as pseudoephedrine-containing products, as demonstrated by studies finding pseudoephedrine to be superior.


For practitioners: the revival is narrow. The decision puts comparative-advertising claims back before Judge Cogan on remand in MDL No. 3089 (E.D.N.Y.) but leaves the efficacy-is-zero theory preempted. Class certification on the surviving theory will require clean comparative-market evidence — not just the FDA's advisory-committee declaration that phenylephrine is placebo-equivalent. Watch for defendants to argue that the surviving claim is uncertifiable given individual purchase and reliance variance.

### Depo-Provera MDL — New Pilot Plaintiffs Appointed Post-Settlement | *In re: Depo-Provera (Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate) Products Liability Litigation*, MDL No. 3140, No. 3:25-md-03140 (N.D. Fla.)


On July 29, 2026, the Florida federal judge overseeing MDL-3140 approved three new pilot plaintiffs
 — a procedural step that, read alongside the June settlement order, confirms the court is threading two tracks simultaneously: executing the global program for eligible claimants while preserving a trial-ready posture for those who fall outside it.

The background: 
on June 15, 2026, Judge M. Casey Rodgers entered an order confirming that Pfizer and the plaintiffs' leadership team reached a global settlement in principle in the federal Depo-Provera meningioma MDL.
 
Pfizer, Pharmacia LLC, and Pharmacia & Upjohn Co. LLC reached an agreement with plaintiffs' lead counsel that would let eligible MDL plaintiffs resolve their claims — terms have not been disclosed and the deal is not yet finalized.
 
The resolution arrived unusually early — before any ruling on Pfizer's federal-preemption motion, before the Daubert ruling, and before any bellwether trial.



As of the July 2026 JPML report, 5,830 cases were pending (5,916 total filed).
 
The settlement does not include cases filed in state court, most of which are filed in New York and Delaware.


The July 29 pilot-plaintiff appointment matters for a specific structural reason: 
the agreement, once finalized, will not end the entire MDL — only plaintiffs who meet the eligibility criteria agreed by the parties will have the opportunity to resolve their claims.
 The court needs a functioning bellwether structure for out-of-program cases, which explains why Judge Rodgers is seating new pilots even as settlement documentation proceeds. The December 7, 2026 trial date in *Toney v. Pfizer* remains vacated; the new pilots are the replacement test cases if global terms collapse or if a material cohort opts out.


Lead counsel Christopher Seeger of Seeger Weiss LLP, co-lead counsel Bryan Aylstock of Aylstock Witkin Kreis & Overholtz, and co-lead counsel Ellen Relkin of Weitz & Luxenberg continue steering the litigation.


The Daubert hearing — reset from June 24–26 — 
is now set for July 27, 2026 in Pensacola and remains on calendar because it stays relevant for any cases that fall outside the settlement agreement.
 Whether that hearing proceeded as scheduled on July 27 is unconfirmed; no ruling or continuance order has surfaced in the public record as of this writing.

Claims denominator note: with 5,830 federal cases and an undisclosed aggregate, per-claim valuation remains opaque. The state-court carve-out — 
Delaware has 340 cases, New York has 119, California has 91, and Minnesota has 72
 — means a material population of claimants will litigate independently and may face different preemption exposure depending on the Daubert outcome.

### Structural Signal — Private Securities Litigation Filling the SEC Void


The SEC is drastically reducing enforcement of the federal securities laws even as it encourages companies to block private securities litigation, including through forced arbitration.
 
From 2019 to 2025, SEC investor distributions averaged $685 million annually, while private securities class action settlements recovered an average of $3.6 billion annually before accounting for attorneys' fees — meaning private recoveries were on average 5.2 times as large as SEC distributions, a ratio that reached 11.5:1 in 2025.


The implication for this desk: enforcement retraction compresses the parallel-track pressure that historically prompted earlier corporate settlements. In markets where SEC parallel proceedings accelerated resolution — as they did in the securities-fraud cycle covering Enron, Madoff, and the 2008 cohort — the absence of that pressure extends litigation timelines and may elevate per-claim settlement values for classes that reach resolution. Watch how this dynamic plays out in the pending securities MDLs (Bartz, Purdue adjacents, BCBS) as the private enforcement gap widens. (__[Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/securities-law/private-investors-pick-up-slack-for-plummeting-sec-enforcement)__)
