### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* — Final Judgment Entered; Fee Award Cut to $101.5 Million; Distribution Pending

**Case:** *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal., San Francisco Div.) (CourtListener Docket ID 69058235); Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín.


On July 20, 2026, Judge Martínez-Olguín granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement and entered final judgment in *Bartz v. Anthropic*.
 
The court issued a 23-page order granting final approval, resolving outstanding administrative details, and ruling on attorneys' fees.


The settlement fund is, by any measure, a historic result. 
Bloomberg reported that *Bartz v. Anthropic* is "the largest copyright class action settlement in US history," paying "about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works," with claims filed for 440,490 works downloaded by Anthropic from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror.
 
The agreement requires Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from those pirate sites, and to destroy all original files of torrented works and any copies derived from them. In aggregate, the court found this to be the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77%.


The claims-rate figure deserves attention. 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate and 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works, or approximately 0.03% of the total number of works in the class.
 That opt-out fraction is negligibly small. A claims rate above 90% is extraordinary in class action practice — it compresses per-claim recovery (there is no low-filer windfall here), but it also validates the settlement's adequacy finding and sharply limits the appellate exposure that typically attaches to objector campaigns predicated on low class participation.

The fee ruling was the live issue at the May fairness hearing and received extended treatment in the July 20 order. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then trimmed the request to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million in attorneys' fees.
 
An additional $122 million of the settlement money will go toward attorneys and litigation costs in total.
 The court's lodestar cross-check drove the reduction: 
the approved fee works out to approximately $2,953 per hour across 34,381.6 hours of attorney time the court credited.


On the merits of the objections: 
Judge Martínez-Olguín overruled all 53 objections filed with the court, finding the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," negotiated at arm's length, with relief that is adequate for the class and equitable among class members.
 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, reminding those individuals that precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.
 
That is already happening — the *Chicken Soup for the Soul* and *Carreyrou* suits are separate individual actions — and the court's approval order may prompt others to follow.


**Distribution:** 
The Authors Guild has stated it looks forward to commencement of the distribution process so authors can start getting paid, noting that a specific date for payouts has not yet been announced.
 The absence of a payment date at this stage is not unusual — the Special Master dispute-resolution process must be operationalized first — but claimants should monitor the settlement administrator's site for disbursement scheduling within the coming weeks.

**What this closes and what it leaves open.** The final judgment ends Anthropic's liability exposure for the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy claim, but 
Anthropic, in agreeing to the settlement, denied any wrongdoing, and the infringement question on AI training was not adjudicated.
 
Judge William Alsup, before the class was certified, held that AI companies can use legally purchased books for training — not illegal ones — a ruling that applies only to the named plaintiffs, not the certified class.
 The fair-use-for-training question therefore remains unresolved as a matter of class-wide precedent and will drive the litigation posture in the parallel suits (OpenAI, Meta, and others) that have yet to reach settlement.

### II. *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee* (MDL 1720) — Second Distribution Approved; September 2026 Payout Target

**Case:** *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 1720, No. 05-MD-1720 (E.D.N.Y.); Judge Brian Cogan.

Two parallel tracks are running simultaneously in this litigation, and they should not be conflated.

**Track 1 — Damages Class (historical fund, 2004–2019 transactions):** 
On May 26, 2026, Class Counsel filed a motion requesting court approval of a second initial distribution of settlement funds; on June 15, 2026, the Court approved the motion.
 
The second initial distribution is anticipated to occur in September 2026.
 This is the existing $5.5+ billion damages class settlement covering merchants that accepted Visa and Mastercard between January 1, 2004 and January 25, 2019. The claim deadline passed years ago; current work is purely administrative — processing the population of claims that cleared the first distribution cut but were not yet ready for payment. Merchants who filed valid claims before the prior deadline and have not yet received full payment should verify their claim status through the official settlement website.

**Track 2 — Injunctive Relief Class (revised settlement, 2025–2026):** 
On June 9, 2026, Judge Cogan granted preliminary approval to the revised Visa and Mastercard injunctive-relief settlement; prior versions of this settlement had been rejected, and the revised proposal announced in November 2025 is now moving through the court process.
 
The current preliminary-approved terms include a 10 basis-point reduction in the average effective U.S. credit interchange rate for five years, a 1.25% cap on standard U.S. consumer credit card interchange rates, and more flexibility around credit card surcharging.
 
The $38 billion figure circulating in press coverage is not a cash fund for payouts — it represents cumulative merchant savings on interchange rates over a five-year period.
 
Preliminary approval is not final approval; objections, procedural delays, additional appeals, and further court review remain possible before any rate changes go into effect.
 
The June 9 ruling followed an April 27 hearing at which Judge Cogan heard objections from several large merchants, including an attorney for Walmart, that do not support the settlement.


**Denominator math for Track 1 claimants:** The September 2026 second distribution is a partial payout. Claimants who held back or whose claims were not cleared in the first wave should not assume full resolution in September — a third distribution covering disputed or audited claims is likely to follow.

### III. Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Active Filing Stage

The campaign that Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026 has moved from intake to active arbitration filing. The mechanics and theory of recovery bear close attention.


Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 The two predicate rulings are Judge Amit P. Mehta's September 2025 liability finding in the D.C. search monopoly case and Judge Leonie M. Brinkema's ad tech liability finding in the Eastern District of Virginia.


The claimed $218 billion recovery figure is based on calculations from an economist Keller Postman has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly rulings.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.
 Keller Postman is proceeding under the FAA and Google's standard advertiser agreements, which contain mandatory arbitration clauses — the same clause structure that plaintiff-side firms have converted from a defendant liability shield into a volume-claim vehicle in prior campaigns.


Keller Postman's Ashley Keller previously architected the Amazon Alexa mass arbitration campaign that prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands, and the TurboTax campaign that ran parallel to FTC enforcement and resolved in a confidential settlement alongside a $141 million FTC fund.
 The Google campaign follows the same playbook: aggregate sufficient demand volume to make individual adjudication economically nonviable for the defendant and force a global negotiated resolution.


In addition to representing private claimants in the arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel case.
 That AG parallel track matters for leverage — it creates both public-record pressure and an overlapping damages theory that Google cannot extinguish through a private arbitration settlement alone. 
For major advertisers — including some with hundreds of millions of dollars in annual Google ad spend — mass anonymous arbitration allows them to pursue settlements without running afoul of account etiquette or damaging a key media relationship.


The campaign remains pre-settlement and pre-award; no arbitral decisions have been published. The operative risk for Google is AAA filing-fee exposure at scale, which in prior campaigns has been sufficient on its own to drive settlement.

### IV. *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation* (MDL 2406) — Distribution Underway

**Case:** *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL 2406, N.D. Ala., Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP; Judge R. David Proctor.


The initial distribution of payments to Damages Class Members with valid claims began on May 11, 2026, in the settlement of *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, Ala. Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP.
 
BCBS agreed to a $2.67 billion antitrust settlement; roughly six million policyholders and businesses filed claims, with approximately $1.9 billion available for payouts after fees.



Final approval was granted by Judge Proctor in August 2022; appeals were filed through the Eleventh Circuit and ultimately to the U.S. Supreme Court, with the final legal hurdles cleared in 2024.
 
The settlement administrator began issuing payments on May 11, 2026, following a distribution motion filed April 23, 2026 and approved by the court on April 24, 2026.


The denominator math here produces thin per-claim recoveries. 
Approximately six million claimants share roughly $1.9 billion after fees, for an average payout of approximately $333.
 This is a claims-made settlement with no opt-out windfall — high claims volume compressed per-claimant recovery to near-nominal levels. The structural lesson for claimants in comparable antitrust matters: claims rate projections at the notice stage should drive realistic recovery expectations before filing costs are committed.

**Provider sub-fund note:** A separate provider-side sub-fund under an amended $2.8 billion total settlement covered health care providers with a claim deadline of July 29, 2025. That deadline has passed; no new provider claims are accepted.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and recovery counsel should move on three immediate items: (1) Monitor the *Bartz v. Anthropic* settlement administrator (CourtListener Docket 69058235; settlement site) for the distribution-commencement announcement — no payment date is yet set, but with final judgment entered July 20, 2026, disbursement scheduling should follow within weeks; the 92.77% claims rate means recoveries will be pro-rata against a fully subscribed fund, so no late-mover advantage exists. (2) *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee* (MDL 1720) claimants whose awards were not included in the first distribution should confirm claim status through the official settlement website ahead of the September 2026 second distribution; the June 15, 2026 court approval is clean and the payout window is firm. (3) U.S. businesses that purchased Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016 and have not yet evaluated the Keller Postman mass arbitration campaign should do so now — the intake window is open, filing is contingency-based, and the two predicate monopoly rulings (Mehta, D.C.; Brinkema, E.D. Va.) substantially de-risk the liability threshold; early filers may receive priority processing before the AAA's administrative queue lengthens as demand volume scales.
