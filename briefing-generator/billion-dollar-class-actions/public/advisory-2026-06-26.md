### I. Monsanto v. Durnell — FIFRA Preemption Cuts Both Ways for the $7.25 Billion Roundup Settlement


In an opinion by Justice Brett Kavanaugh, the Supreme Court ruled 7-2 in *Monsanto Company v. Durnell*, No. 24-1068, that state lawsuits aimed at holding Monsanto liable for failing to warn consumers about potential Roundup exposure risks are barred by the federal law governing pesticide sales.
 
Argued April 27, 2026, and decided June 25, 2026, the case arose from the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA), under which the EPA regulates pesticides, including Roundup, a glyphosate-based pesticide manufactured by Monsanto.
 
The Court held that FIFRA expressly preempts a state-law failure-to-warn claim because such a claim would require Monsanto to add a cancer warning to Roundup's label; FIFRA's preemption clause prohibits states from imposing labeling requirements "in addition to or different from those required under this subchapter."



The ruling threw out a $1.25 million verdict that a Missouri jury had awarded to John Durnell, a St. Louis man who said two decades of using Roundup caused his non-Hodgkin lymphoma; Durnell sued in 2019, a state trial court awarded damages, and a state appeals court upheld them before the justices took up the matter as the first federal court to hear the case.
 
The decision created an unusual split, with Justice Brett Kavanaugh writing the majority and his fellow conservative Justice Neil Gorsuch joining a dissent written by Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson.
 
Jackson argued that the majority misunderstood FIFRA's requirements, writing that "in accepting Monsanto's argument and holding that Durnell's failure-to-warn claim is preempted, the Court misunderstands FIFRA's requirements, misinterprets the scope of FIFRA's preemption, and ultimately leaves Durnell without a remedy."


For claimants and settlement counsel, the ruling's practical effect is bifurcated. 
The decision intersects with a separate effort to wind down the Roundup litigation: Monsanto has proposed a $7.25 billion nationwide class settlement to resolve current and future claims from people diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma; that agreement has received preliminary approval, and a hearing on final approval is scheduled for July 9.
 
Bayer AG won a legal fight bringing it a step closer to getting Missouri state court approval for the $7.25 billion settlement, after a federal judge in St. Louis rejected opponents' bid to transfer the case to a federal judge in San Francisco who had expressed skepticism of the deal, sending it back to state court for approval.


The preemption ruling simultaneously strengthens Bayer's hand in the settlement approval proceeding and changes the denominator math for holdouts evaluating whether to opt out. Plaintiffs who opted out before the June 4, 2026 deadline to pursue independent litigation now face a materially narrowed tort claim landscape: 
the Court reasoned that EPA's approval of Roundup's label without a cancer warning constitutes a federal labeling requirement, and because FIFRA's preemption clause prohibits states from imposing labeling requirements "in addition to or different from" federal requirements, Missouri's requirement for a cancer warning is preempted.
 The practical effect is that holdout plaintiffs pursuing state failure-to-warn theories in Missouri and other state courts must now re-examine the viability of their standalone claims. 
The class-action settlement, which Bayer proposed in February, is a key part of the company's plans to cap its obligations by resolving current and future damage claims, with funding running for up to 21 years.
 
The settlement creates a complex point-scoring system designed to rank cases into settlement tiers based on the strength of claims and severity of injuries, with case-specific factors such as type of cancer, treatment outcome, the victim's age, and estimated earnings capacity assigned point scores.


**Recovery posture:** Counsel for non-opt-out class members holding claims in the July 9 pool should re-run their per-claim valuations in light of the preemption ruling. The *Durnell* decision does not extinguish design-defect claims or claims under state consumer-protection statutes that operate independently of labeling requirements — those channels remain open for opt-outs — but the core failure-to-warn theory, which drove most of the earlier jury verdicts, is now federally preempted. Claims counsel should assess whether any opt-outs executed before June 4 can be withdrawn and re-entered into the settlement class before the July 9 hearing.

### II. Claritev Antitrust MDL — Judge Kennelly Strips Unclean Hands Defense, Bellwether Trial Approaches


On June 24, 2026, an Illinois federal judge ruled that healthcare data firm Claritev and a group of major insurers cannot assert an unclean hands defense in multidistrict litigation accusing payors of scheming to fix reimbursement rates through the data firm's pricing tools.
 
The court is the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, and the judge is Matthew F. Kennelly.



The litigation involves the Texas Medical Association and others alleging that Claritev — formerly known as MultiPlan — and insurers including UnitedHealth, Elevance, Humana, Aetna, and Cigna conspired to suppress out-of-network reimbursements, with plaintiffs arguing the alleged scheme has drained billions from providers.
 
The suits are centralized as MDL No. 3121 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois.
 
The complaints press federal Sherman Act antitrust counts alongside state antitrust and consumer-protection claims, with plaintiffs seeking damages and injunctive relief that could reshape how out-of-network rates are set.



The DOJ's antitrust division had launched a criminal price-fixing investigation into Claritev, a pricing intermediary that major health insurers use to set reimbursement rates for out-of-network medical claims.
 However, 
Claritev said on June 22, 2026, that it is not under any criminal antitrust investigation into health insurance by the U.S. Department of Justice as the agency is closing a grand jury proceeding.
 The DOJ's withdrawal from the criminal track is a mixed signal: it removes the specter of parallel criminal proceedings that often accelerate civil settlement, but it also potentially frees plaintiffs' counsel to pursue treble damages under Clayton Act § 4 without concern that the criminal process might supersede civil recovery.


At the February 27, 2026 case management conference, the court cleared the way for depositions of Claritev representatives and required the company to produce key internal records.
 
A federal judge largely denied defendants' motions to dismiss in mid-2025, allowing antitrust and consumer-protection claims to move into discovery, with an initial trial date currently listed for December 7, 2027.
 The unclean hands ruling by Judge Kennelly on June 24 is now the second significant pre-trial defense elimination in as many months. With that affirmative defense foreclosed, defendants are left with merits arguments on the § 1 conspiracy theory and on damages quantification. Provider plaintiffs holding opt-in claims should ensure their damages experts have addressed the but-for rate calculation methodology — the core dispute will be how to reconstruct the market-clearing out-of-network rate absent the alleged Claritev repricing algorithm.

### III. Bartz v. Anthropic — Final Approval Still Pending; Two New Docket Entries Signal Possible Near-Term Order

*Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.), continues to await a final approval order from 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, who held the fairness hearing for the final approval of the class settlement on May 14, 2026.
 The tracked-case ground truth shows two new docket filings as of June 25, 2026, suggesting the court may be circulating a draft order or requesting supplemental submissions. The CourtListener docket (ID: 69058235) was last updated June 22, 2026.


Anthropic will pay $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which attorneys' fees — revised down from the original $300 million ask to $187.5 million — administrative costs, and service awards will be drawn.
 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson reported at the May 14 hearing that the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100, with a 92.77% participation rate for works on the works list.
 
Class counsel had reported 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works claimed — a 91.3% rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in counsel's March 19, 2026 Motion for Final Approval, indicating that a large number of claims came in at the very end of the claims process.



Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The April 30, 2026 installment was due roughly six weeks ago; 
the first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 Rights-holders awaiting distribution should note that 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved, with payments likely commencing in the late fall at the earliest.


The most significant residual appellate exposure concerns objector standing. 
By no later than May 21, 2026, the court ordered Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 The treatment of late opt-outs — particularly foreign-language class members who received no translated notice — will likely be the first appellate issue if any objector seeks Ninth Circuit review of the final approval order. The group-registration parity dispute (individual authors who registered multiple works under a single registration receiving one recovery unit versus a per-title recovery) remains unresolved on the merits in the proposed order and could also generate post-approval litigation before the Special Master.

### IV. Sustained Tracking — BCBS, Visa/MC Interchange, and Keller Postman Mass Arbitration

**BCBS.** The Blue Cross Blue Shield subscriber antitrust class action (*In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 2406, N.D. Ala., Hon. R. David Proctor) settled for $2.67 billion with final approval entered in 2022. The distribution administrator continues to process second-wave claimant payments; rights-holders who filed timely claims under the subscriber class definition should confirm claim status through the authorized administrator portal, as unclaimed funds carry reversion risk to the defendant pool under the settlement's cy pres provision.

**Visa/MC Interchange.** *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 1720 (E.D.N.Y.), remains in supervised distribution. Merchants who opted into the settlement class and did not pursue the Rule 23(b)(2) injunctive relief track should verify that their interchange volume data was correctly submitted; the claims period has closed, but correction submissions remain pending before the Special Master.

**Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration.** The ~$218 billion Keller Postman advertiser mass arbitration campaign against Google (Sherman Act §§ 1-2, Clayton Act § 4 treble damages theory) continues under AAA Mass Arbitration Supplementary Rules. Google's primary defense strategy has been a threshold arbitrability challenge — arguing the advertiser terms of service did not contemplate mass-coordinated arbitration at this volume — a theory that has generated mixed outcomes at the bellwether level. The pivot from claim-by-claim resistance to a global settlement posture typically occurs when bellwether arbitration awards begin to consolidate a damages-per-claimant baseline that makes aggregate exposure modeling tractable for defense counsel. That inflection has not yet been confirmed in public filings, but the DOJ's dropped criminal investigation of Claritev (a structurally analogous hub-and-spoke pricing case) suggests the government is not providing a parallel criminal-enforcement backstop to these mass private claims, which removes some plaintiffs' settlement leverage in related matters.
