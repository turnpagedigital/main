### Bartz v. Anthropic PBC — 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) | Final Approval Limbo, Docket Activity July 1


Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín held the final-approval fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* on May 14, 2026, in the Northern District of California.
 
She took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench and indicated that short orders might follow quickly.
 As of the CourtListener docket pull dated July 2, 2026, 
the docket was last updated July 2, 2026 at 5:11 a.m.
 — and the tracked-case ground truth registers two new filings dated July 1 — but no final approval order is yet reflected in publicly accessible RECAP records. The settlement therefore remains pending, now roughly seven weeks past the fairness hearing. That gap is not typical; orders of this complexity and political weight (seven objectors spoke at the hearing) routinely take longer, but the extended silence warrants monitoring.

The settlement's headline terms are 
a $1.5 billion fund, with plaintiff counsel originally seeking 25% — $375 million — in fees.
 
Class counsel subsequently revised that fee request down from $300 million to $187.5 million at the final-approval hearing
 — a meaningful concession that, if approved at the reduced figure, represents roughly 12.5% of the gross fund. 
Anthropic separately challenged a $75 million non-class-counsel fee request as having "scant justification."


The claims math is now settled and striking. 
Class counsel reported to the court that 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a 91.3% rate — a figure that vastly surpassed the 54% rate reported in the March 19 motion for final approval, indicating a large volume of last-minute filings.
 
That high claim rate means per-work payouts will be closer to the original $3,000-per-work baseline rather than the higher figures that a lower claim rate would have produced.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed the projected payout at approximately $3,100 per work.


Anthropic's payment schedule matters for distribution timing: 
the fund is being paid in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved
 — meaning the September 25, 2026 installment may arrive before distributions flow to claimants if there is appellate activity. The Authors Guild projected 
the settlement administrator would calculate distributions around June 11, 2026, with disbursement beginning June 2026 or later, subject to change.
 Both dates have slipped absent the final approval order.

The opt-out wave has generated its own litigation. 
Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action, *Cruz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:26-cv-04482 (N.D. Cal.), demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works.
 That case is active in parallel. 
For many authors implicated in *Bartz*, the AI training litigation is not ending — it is shifting, with dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft and active putative class actions against Meta.


**Practitioner note on fee trajectory:** The reduction from the initial $300 million fee ask to $187.5 million at the hearing, alongside Anthropic's challenge to the $75 million non-class-counsel request, signals the court is conducting a serious lodestar cross-check. 
Susman Godfrey and Lieff Cabraser reported approximately 26,000 hours worked in their fee petition
 — at an effective blended rate the court will scrutinize against the percentage-of-fund result. If the court awards $187.5 million (12.5%), that is still a structurally important data point for fee benchmarking in future AI copyright settlements.

### Keller Postman v. Google — Mass Arbitration Campaign | ~$218B Claimed Exposure + July 1 TOS Shift

The largest mass arbitration campaign by dollar value now has a direct counter-move from the defendant. 
Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding the company engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem.
 
In April 2026, Keller Postman's Ashley Keller began filing claims against Google/Alphabet on behalf of advertisers, with potential recovery estimated at $218 billion or more.


The legal predicate is robust: 
federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) ruled in favor of the DOJ, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
Federal antitrust law provides for treble damages; under Keller Postman's theory, a 10% overcharge claim translates into a potential recovery of up to 30% of the advertiser's total Google Ads spend during the relevant period.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade
, providing the denominator against which those percentage claims are sized.

The structural pressure point: 
in mass arbitration, the leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger — not from any single damages number.
 
Due to contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, these claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation.


**The July 1 TOS move is the sharpest development this week.** 
Google's updated Ads Terms of Service took effect July 1, 2026.
 
The new terms allow for batch arbitration of identical or nearly identical claims, replacing the previous framework that handled each claim individually.
 
The new terms include a 30-day window to opt out of the arbitration clause via a web form at ads.google.com/nav/arbitration; if an advertiser opts out, it retains the right to bring disputes in court; if it misses the window, it is bound by the arbitration clause.
 This is a direct structural response to Keller Postman's campaign: Google is attempting to consolidate thousands of individual arbitrations into batches — reducing per-claim forum fees and the cost leverage that makes mass arbitration economically coercive. 
The TOS update was explicitly described as a response to the May 2026 mass arbitration filing, with Google framing it as "being proactive about how we settle things with advertisers."


For current Google advertisers: the 30-day opt-out window to preserve court access runs through July 31, 2026. 
Keller Postman has cautioned that deadlines vary by state and that earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.
 
In addition to private arbitration claimants, Keller Postman represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel action.
 The interplay between the new batch-arbitration terms, the existing mass-filing campaign, and the state-level parallel track will be the defining procedural battleground in this campaign through the balance of 2026.

### In re Lemonade, Inc. Data Disclosure Litigation — S.D.N.Y. | Claims Window Open Through September 8


Lemonade, Inc. and Lemonade Insurance Agency, LLC have agreed to a $10,500,000 class action settlement over a data exposure involving Lemonade's online insurance quote platform, affecting approximately 190,000 people whose personal information — including driver's license numbers — was exposed between April 2023 and September 18, 2024.
 
The consolidated lawsuit, *In re Lemonade, Inc. Data Disclosure Litigation*, is pending in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, asserting claims for negligence, violation of the Driver's Privacy Protection Act, and violation of New York General Business Law Section 349.



Class members with documented financial losses may claim up to $10,000; class members without documentation may receive approximately $55, subject to deductions for attorneys' fees and costs, which could account for up to one-third of the fund.
 The per-capita undocumented recovery of ~$55 against a $10.5 million fund across 190,000 potential claimants is thin — and the typical claims-rate dynamics apply: low participation means higher effective per-claim recovery for those who do file. 
The final approval hearing is scheduled for September 10, 2026.
 
The deadline to opt out is August 7, 2026.
 
Claim forms may be submitted online or by mail, postmarked by September 8, 2026.
 
The claims administrator is Kroll Settlement Administration LLC.


At $10.5 million, this settlement is below the $1B tracking threshold but is notable as a clean Driver's Privacy Protection Act / data-breach hybrid in SDNY — a forum where fee awards and claims-rate norms will be watched by parallel data-breach litigants.

### Broader Field: Opt-Out Dynamics, Fee Benchmarks, and the AAA Mass-Arbitration Rules Environment

Three structural points frame the current environment.

**Opt-out campaigns as a separate recovery track.** The *Cruz v. Anthropic* filing by 28 opt-outs from *Bartz* is the most visible recent example of a post-settlement individual rights-holder track. Statutory copyright damages — up to $150,000 per infringed work — make individual opt-out litigation potentially more valuable per plaintiff than a $3,100 class share, 
given that statutory damages can theoretically reach into the hundreds of billions of dollars in aggregate.
 The trade-off: litigation risk, duration, and the absence of guaranteed recovery. For rights-holders in other AI copyright cases (OpenAI, Meta) who receive opt-out notices, the *Bartz* opt-out track is the current reference model for pricing that decision.

**AAA mass-arbitration rules and the batching debate.** 
Courts in 2025 established clearer guidelines: bellwether provisions are more likely to be upheld when they resolve claims in concurrent batches rather than indefinitely sequential rounds, toll statute of limitations periods, and do not bind non-participating claimants to outcomes.
 
The Ninth Circuit struck down Live Nation's New Era ADR clause (*Heckman*, 2024) and a provision that bound non-participants (*Pandolfi v. AviaGames*, August 2025), while upholding JAMS consolidation authority (*Jones v. Starz*).
 Google's new July 1 batch-arbitration clause is an attempt to thread this needle — structuring consolidation in a way that survives *Pandolfi* — but its validity against currently enrolled Keller Postman claimants (who filed under the prior TOS) is genuinely unsettled and is a near-term litigation question.

**Fee-award trajectory.** The revised Bartz fee ask — $187.5 million on a $1.5 billion fund, or 12.5% — is on the low end of percentage-of-fund awards in mega-settlements, likely as a tactical accommodation to limit objector ammunition. 
Class action fee awards can reach up to 33% or more.
 GCs and claims administrators tracking the *Bartz* fee order (once entered) should expect it to serve as a ceiling-versus-floor reference in the parallel OpenAI and Meta author-copyright cases, which will reach settlement or trial over the next 18 to 24 months.
