### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Still Pending; Fee Request Cut Nearly in Half

The procedural posture remains where it has been for seven weeks: 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the Northern District of California held the fairness hearing on May 14, 2026 and took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench.
 The court has not issued a written final approval order as of the July 1, 2026 last docket entry at CourtListener docket ID 69058235.

The fee dispute is the most watched remaining variable. 
Authors who allege Anthropic pirated their work urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 That revision — from the original 25%-of-fund ask of $375 million to $187.5 million — is a meaningful concession, likely driven by the unusual participation rate and judicial scrutiny of the lodestar cross-check. 
The plaintiff lawyers had initially sought 25% of the fund, or $375 million — a sum that illustrates the business model of class action litigation — while administrative costs by JND will likely cost several million dollars, and service awards are $50,000 per named plaintiff, totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives.


On the claims side, the denominator math is now largely fixed. 
Class counsel filed papers showing that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate — vastly surpassing the 54 percent rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval, indicating a large volume of claims came in at the very end.
 
The high claim rate means that forecasts of a larger per-work payout based on the March 19 filing will not materialize, and the actual payout will likely end up closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.
 Class counsel confirmed at the fairness hearing a figure of approximately $3,100 per work. (__[chatgptiseatingtheworld.com, May 15, 2026](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/05/15/final-approval-of-class-settlement-hearing-in-bartz-v-anthropic-recap/)__)

The hearing itself was procedurally notable. 
The judge heard from seven objectors, each speaking for two minutes (except attorney James Bartolomei, who received five minutes to address several objectors collectively), and questioned class counsel — Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman — on the service awards, cost reserve, attorneys' fees, and fee multiplier.
 
Post-hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered Anthropic by May 21, 2026 to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and ordered plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.


The pending approval order is the sole remaining gate before 
Anthropic is required to destroy the pirated copies and pay $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.
 Anthropic's installment schedule runs across four tranches — 
October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The second installment has already passed, meaning the approval order, if and when issued, will govern the third and fourth payments. Any appeal of a final approval order tolls distribution; the September 25, 2026 installment is at risk if an objector notices an appeal before then.

What the settlement does not resolve is equally important for claimants in parallel AI copyright proceedings. 
The scope of the settlement has been widely misunderstood: Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training. Judge Alsup ruled in June 2025 that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and nothing in the settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
What the court left unresolved was Anthropic's potential liability for downloading and storing files from two shadow libraries — Library Genesis (LibGen) and Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi).
 The training-use fair-use holding will be cited aggressively by defendants in the OpenAI, Meta, and Apple consolidated actions now proceeding in parallel.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Claims Filing Underway; $218B Theoretical Pool; Remedies Ruling Still Pending


Keller Postman, the Chicago-based litigation firm, announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings that found the company engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem.
 This is the largest mass arbitration campaign ever filed by dollar value.

The legal theory rests entirely on existing liability findings rather than contested facts. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) ruled in favor of the U.S. government
 on search and ad tech monopoly counts respectively. 
The $218 billion damages figure is based on calculations from an economist the firm retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established liability.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade, and experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs.


The structural mechanics of this campaign track Keller Postman's established playbook. 
Because of contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, these claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation; Keller Postman is coordinating thousands of individual claims on behalf of businesses nationwide, allowing each claimant to pursue recovery based on its own advertising spend.
 
The firm is pursuing individual arbitration claims coordinated at scale, with potential recovery estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, and treble damages available under federal antitrust law (Clayton Act § 4).



In addition to representing private claimants in arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel case.
 The dual-track structure — private arbitration plus state AG enforcement — creates overlapping pressure on Google's negotiating position at the remedies phase. 
Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad tech case is expected in 2026, and Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines.
 The remedies order in the Mehta (D.D.C. search) case remains on appeal, with Google having filed in January 2026 to pause data-sharing mandates. (__[ppc.land, May 17, 2026](https://ppc.land/mass-arbitration-targets-google-over-alleged-billions-in-ad-overcharges/)__)


Many large advertisers — Keller confirmed he represents multiple major businesses with hundreds of millions in annual Google media spend — have hesitated to be the public face of anti-Google litigation, but in mass anonymous arbitration, big brands can pursue settlements without running afoul of delicate account relationships.
 That dynamic is structurally important: the anonymity of private arbitration removes the hold-out problem that typically suppresses participation in high-profile commercial disputes.

The timing constraint worth flagging: 
Keller Postman cautions that deadlines vary by state and that earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.
 For businesses that purchased Google ads post-August 2016 and have not yet filed, the clock is running on a state-by-state basis. Under Clayton Act § 4, the limitations period is four years from discovery of injury, but state law claims may run shorter depending on the jurisdiction.

### In re Lemonade Inc. Data Disclosure Litigation, No. 1:25-cv-04106 (S.D.N.Y.) — New Settlement Now in Active Claims Phase

This is the week's new entrant. 
Lemonade Insurance Co. agreed to pay $10.5 million to resolve claims that it failed to prevent a data breach affecting its online insurance quote system, covering consumers who received notice that their personal information — including driver's license numbers — was compromised between April 2023 and September 2024.



The class action alleged that Lemonade's online quote platform automatically populated consumers' private information, namely driver's license numbers, when basic identifying information was entered, and that cybercriminals exploited this vulnerability to obtain consumers' driver's license numbers over 17 months.
 The class is estimated at approximately 190,000 affected individuals. (__[classaction.org](https://www.classaction.org/news/10.5m-lemonade-settlement-resolves-class-action-lawsuit-over-17-month-data-incident)__)


The complaint alleges negligence and violations of consumer protection and privacy laws, including the New York General Business Law and the federal Drivers' Privacy Protection Act (DPPA).
 
Class counsel plans to seek attorneys' fees of up to one-third of the settlement fund
 — approximately $3.5 million — leaving a net fund of roughly $7 million before administration costs.

The per-claimant economics matter here. 
Settlement class members who file a valid, timely claim form can receive up to $10,000 for documented losses incurred on or after April 1, 2023, and a pro rata cash fund payment.
 
Class members who cannot document losses may receive approximately $55 in cash payments, though that figure is dependent on the number of claims filed and deductions for attorneys' fees, which could take up to one-third of the fund.
 The $55 baseline undocumented-loss payment is characteristic of a low-per-claimant data breach settlement — claims rate will be the operative variable.

Key dates: 
The exclusion and objection deadline is August 7, 2026; the final approval hearing is scheduled for September 10, 2026; and class members must submit valid claim forms by September 8, 2026.
 
As part of the proposed settlement, Lemonade has agreed to implement business practice changes to prevent future disclosure of confidential consumer information.


This case is worth tracking at the macro level as a data point on DPPA-based class exposure. The Drivers' Privacy Protection Act is an increasingly active litigation vehicle against insurance and fintech companies whose quote-generation platforms create systemic data exposure risk — a pattern that will recur.

### Mass Arbitration Mechanics — AAA Volume and Structural Context


The AAA closed 26 mass arbitrations with 37,648+ claimants in H1 2025 alone, updated its Consumer and Employment Arbitration Rules effective May 1, 2025, and the Ninth Circuit upheld JAMS consolidation of 7,300 Starz claims in *Jones v. Starz Entertainment*.
 The Ninth Circuit also separately found a bellwether provision unconscionable where non-participants were bound without notice — *Pandolfi v. AviaGames* (Aug. 2025). Both rulings tighten the permissible scope of defendant-side procedural defenses.


Corporate defendants have attempted to fight back with bellwether provisions, fee non-payment (Samsung), and by suing plaintiffs' firms (Sega, Tubi), but most of these defenses have failed or backfired — Samsung's fee refusal opened federal court instead of closing the case, and the plaintiff bar's structural advantage remains intact heading into 2026.


The next wave of mass arbitration campaigns is being assembled around privacy claims. 
BIPA (biometric), VPPA (video privacy), and CIPA (California wiretapping) claims are fueling the next round, with every app, streaming service, and healthcare platform with a tracking pixel a potential target.
 The Keller Postman Google campaign — built entirely on pre-established antitrust liability findings rather than contested facts — may prove the template for similar advertiser-side campaigns if other ad networks face monopoly findings in ongoing DOJ enforcement actions.
