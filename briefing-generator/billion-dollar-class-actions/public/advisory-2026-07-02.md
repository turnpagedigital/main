### Bartz v. Anthropic — Docket 676 and the Approval Bottleneck

The most active matter on this desk this week is *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) before Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín held the fairness hearing on May 14, 2026, and issued a post-hearing order directing Anthropic to file, by May 21, a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 
The bulk of the court's attention at that hearing was directed not at the merits of the objections but at the structure of attorneys' fees and expense accounting — specifically the proposed fee multiplier, the $15 million cost reserve, and an accounting line that appeared to move money between class counsel and non-appointed counsel.



Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench.
 As of the most recent CourtListener sync (June 25, 2026), the docket reflects a filing at Entry 676 dated June 30, 2026 — characterized as objections to a June order setting deadlines/hearings — confirming that post-hearing activity is ongoing and a final approval order has not yet been publicly docketed. The settlement status remains: final approval pending.

The economic stakes of that approval order are substantial. 
Anthropic will pay $1.5 billion into a settlement fund; class counsel initially sought 25% ($375 million) in fees, a request that was subsequently revised.
 
Class counsel revised their fee request downward — from $300 million to $187.5 million — ahead of the fairness hearing.
 The per-work payment figure, however, turned on the final claims rate. 
Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19 motion for final approval, eleven days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 
At the fairness hearing, class counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work in the class would be approximately $3,100.
 That figure is a function of the 91.3% claims rate compressing the per-work denominator; had participation remained at 54%, the per-work figure would have been materially higher.

The fee architecture warrants close attention. 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 That staggered schedule means any appellate challenge to the final approval order — filed within the standard 30-day window after entry — could delay the second and third installment drawdowns and push actual distribution well into 2027. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 The settlement website had projected distribution calculations by June 11, 2026 and payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later — both contingent on final approval having been entered. The absence of a publicly filed final approval order as of this run means the disbursement schedule remains conditional.

Structurally, the objector posture is thin but not without appellate relevance. 
The handful of objectors who appeared raised concerns — most prominently that the Works List excludes works that should have been included due to group registration practices, pseudonyms, or other registration formalities, and that the per-work payment is small relative to the statutory damages ceiling.
 Seven objectors appeared; one attorney, James Bartolomei, spoke for five minutes on behalf of a group. That participation level is low enough that a Rule 23(e) fairness challenge on appeal would face a high bar — but the fee-structure scrutiny the court itself applied creates at least one plausible appellate hook if the approved fee award departs materially from the revised $187.5 million request. 
If Judge Martínez-Olguín grants approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and to pay the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.


The downstream significance extends beyond this docket. 
For many authors implicated in Bartz, the litigation is not ending but shifting — to consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others. Several of those cases appear to be adopting the strategy that proved effective in Bartz: rather than challenging AI training as such, they focus on the underlying piracy and on infringing copies.
 The $3,100-per-work settlement value, achieved on a piracy theory rather than a training-use theory, sets a floor for comparable negotiations.

### BCBS Subscriber Settlement — Mid-Stream Distribution, Tier 2 Now Flowing

*In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala., Judge R. David Proctor) has moved from litigation into active distribution mechanics. 
A motion to distribute settlement funds was filed on April 23, 2026, approved the next day, and the settlement administrator JND Legal Administration began issuing checks to approved claimants on May 11, 2026.



The $2.67 billion settlement in MDL No. 2406 is one of the largest healthcare antitrust settlements in U.S. history; final court approval came in August 2022 and distributions have been ramping up through 2026.
 The fund available for claimants is reduced by fees: 
the $2.67 billion fund is now estimated at approximately $1.9 billion after attorneys' fees and administrative costs, to be paid to approximately 6 million policyholders who filed claims.



Tier 2 (employee) notification emails started June 1 for the first batch — approximately 15% of validated Tier-2 claims — with payments arriving four to eight weeks after notification. Tier 3 (employer) payments have not yet started.
 
The Eleventh Circuit denied the final pending appeal on the Tier-2 subclass methodology, clearing the path for full Tier-2 distribution to begin in Q2/Q3 2026.
 For the parallel provider settlement: 
that fund is a $2.8 billion cash payment with injunctive relief that economists have valued at a minimum of $17.3 billion, with average administrative cost savings of $7.55 per BlueCard claim.
 
Approximately 6,500 provider organizations opted out — less than 1% of a class exceeding one million — including Mayo Clinic, Providence, University of Michigan Health, and AdventHealth, many of which have filed their own antitrust lawsuits seeking treble damages.


The BCBS distribution is the clearest current illustration of the claims-rate / per-claim recovery relationship: at 6 million claimants against a ~$1.9 billion net fund, average payouts run approximately $333, with individual variation driven by coverage tier, premium amounts, and coverage duration. Sophisticated claimants — small employers with multi-year group plan coverage — are seeing payouts in the $8,500–$15,000 range on Tier 3 election, which has not yet commenced.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Claims Filed, $218B Exposure

The most consequential new mass-arbitration campaign on the desk is now in intake. 
Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding the company engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem.


The legal predicate is clean: two separate bench trial findings, both final. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. government on antitrust grounds.
 The liability findings are not themselves on appeal for purposes of private recovery; Google's advertiser contracts contain mandatory individual arbitration clauses, which Keller Postman is weaponizing as the vehicle for recovery rather than trying to circumvent. 
Arbitration clauses in advertisers' contracts effectively prevent damages via class action litigation — but claimants can join together for a group arbitration, which is usually handled by a single law firm and gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations.



The $218 billion aggregate figure is based on calculations from an economist Keller Postman has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade; experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs.
 
Potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law — Clayton Act § 4.
 The eligible period covers Google advertising purchases made August 2016 onward.

The structural dynamic is central to understanding the risk profile. 
Many large advertisers — including major brands with hundreds of millions in annual Google media spend — may be hesitant to be publicly associated with anti-Google litigation; mass anonymous arbitration allows them to pursue settlements without risking that relationship.
 That behavioral incentive pushes intake toward institutional advertisers, not just SMBs — which materially increases the aggregate exposure. 
In addition to representing private claimants in the arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel case.
 State AG involvement strengthens the liability narrative in any global settlement discussion.

The FAA mechanics are worth tracking. There is no pending motion to stay or compel that has been publicly filed; Google's position in arbitration-fee allocation disputes in prior Keller Postman campaigns (DoorDash, Postmates) has been to resist mass-filing fee demands at AAA/JAMS. If Google seeks a similar procedural defense here — arguing that mass arbitration filing fees must be borne individually — it will likely face the same estoppel arguments Keller Postman deployed successfully against DoorDash. 
Keller Postman's prior Amazon Alexa campaign prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands — a structural capitulation that remains the ceiling-case outcome for this campaign.

### Fee Trajectory and Denominator Mechanics — Cross-Case Note

The fee tension visible in Bartz — class counsel's initial $375 million (25%) ask revised to $187.5 million (12.5%) before the fairness hearing — is consistent with a FRCP 23(h) pattern emerging in mega-fund settlements where courts apply a percentage-of-fund method with a lodestar crosscheck. At a $1.5 billion fund, a 25% award would be $375 million; the percentage producing $187.5 million is 12.5%, which is at the low end of what circuits have approved for pure percentage awards in funds above $500 million. The court's scrutiny of the fee multiplier and the cost-reserve transfer suggests the final awarded fee may land somewhere between $187.5 million and $225 million — and any upward departure would attract appellate attention given the objector record.

In the BCBS settlement, the $2.67 billion fund was reduced to approximately $1.9 billion net of fees and administration — a blended fee-and-cost drag of roughly 29%, consistent with historical large-fund averages. The lesson for claimants evaluating optionality in the Google advertiser arbitration: the Clayton Act § 4 treble-damages multiplier is the structural feature that makes individual recovery economically meaningful even after contingency fees, because the pre-treble overcharge estimate (up to 30% of ad spend) is trebled before fees are applied, not after.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and claimants with active positions across these matters should calibrate timing and posture as follows: In *Bartz*, the dispositive near-term event is entry of the final approval order — monitor the docket at CourtListener No. 69058235 for Judge Martínez-Olguín's order, which will trigger Anthropic's obligation to fund the second installment (September 25, 2026) and start the 30-day appellate window; any objector-driven Rule 23(e) appeal should be assessed for likelihood of success against the thin objector record and the court's own fee-scrutiny, which reduces the risk of a "sweetheart deal" reversal. In BCBS MDL 2406, Tier-2 claimants who received June 1 notification emails should confirm receipt and dispute any discrepant premium-amount figures within the 30-day dispute window; Tier-3 employer claimants should prepare documentation now ahead of the September 2026 commencement window; opt-out providers who have filed or are considering individual antitrust actions should note that the Eleventh Circuit's Tier-2 methodology ruling eliminates one appellate avenue but does not resolve independent opt-out claims. In the Keller Postman / Google arbitration, businesses with material Google ad spend from August 2016 onward should assess whether execution of a contingency retainer before applicable state limitations periods run is warranted — Keller Postman's public guidance is that deadlines vary by state and earlier action increases the probability of a timely claim; the Clayton Act four-year limitations period under 15 U.S.C. § 15b runs from the last overt act, meaning spend through mid-2022 may be time-barred in the absence of a continuing-violation argument, and spend from 2022 forward is more defensible — counsel should perform the state-law and federal-law SOL analysis before intake.
