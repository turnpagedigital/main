### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Post-Hearing Limbo, Distribution Timeline, and the Attorney-Fee Compression That Matters


The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place on May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench, and indicated that short orders may follow as soon as the day of the hearing.
 No confirmed ruling has surfaced in the public record through July 3, 2026 — the CourtListener docket (Docket ID 69058235) was last updated July 2, 2026, and reflects a filing dated July 1, 2026 whose description remains unpopulated. The court has not yet issued a final approval order from the public record, and the status remains formally "Settlement — final approval pending" per the tracked-case ground truth.

The financial mechanics for claimants are material. 
Anthropic will pay $1.5 billion into the settlement fund; plaintiffs' counsel at Lieff Cabraser Heimann & Bernstein and Susman Godfrey initially sought 25% of the fund, or $375 million.
 That request was later revised downward: 
authors urged the court to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 The compression from $375 million to $187.5 million is significant — it effectively moves roughly $187.5 million from counsel to the class, improving per-work recovery at the margin.


The $1.5 billion minimum settlement is being funded by Anthropic in multiple installments; the first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 
Post-final-approval, the settlement administrator was scheduled to calculate distributions around June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later, subject to change.
 Given the court has not yet issued a final approval order (as of July 3), those dates have slipped.

The claims rate at close is the key valuation input. 
In an updated claims report filed April 16, class counsel reported claims for 440,490 of the 482,460 works on the Works List (Dkt. 643), up from the 54% reported in the March 19 motion for final approval.
 
The claims rate reached 91.3%.
 
The high rate of claim means that the forecasts about a bigger payout based on the March 19 report of 54% will not materialize, and the actual payout will likely end up being closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.
 The denominator math is now settled; the per-work recovery floor is roughly $3,000 before any fee-reduction dividend flows through.

The outstanding objector record raised several intraclass-fairness issues Judge Martínez-Olguín must resolve before entry of final judgment. 
The distribution plan systematically favors publishers over authors through the claims-made process and presumption that publishers share in authors' recovery; Professor Bishop noted that publishers could claim roughly 50% or more of the total settlement amount.
 
One objection reported that Elsevier proposed to take 88% of the per-work settlement amount for one book, leaving 12% for the author; the settlement's default split for non-education works is 50/50, but education works are subject to negotiation and arbitration governed by a Special Master process.
 
The group-registration issue: author Sandra Pinder holds rights to 40+ independently published novels registered under a single group copyright registration; under the settlement, each copyright registration number counts as one "claimable Work," meaning she receives one share rather than 40.


An additional exposure layer: 
the court gave a terse response to notice that a Canadian publisher's counsel contacted Anthropic looking for a better deal, saying the publisher could opt out but couldn't "seek a sweeter deal than other class members."
 That ruling forecloses the informal side-channel that several institutional rightsholders had explored, channeling any remaining dissatisfied parties toward the Ninth Circuit on appeal post-approval.

### In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation — MDL 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala., Judge R. David Proctor) — Mid-Wave Distribution, Residual Reserve Structure

The BCBS subscriber settlement has crossed into active distribution. 
On April 23, 2026, subscriber plaintiffs filed a motion in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama asking the court to approve a distribution plan for nearly 7.8 million claimants.
 
The initial distribution of payments to Damages Class Members with valid claims began May 11, 2026.



The total settlement fund is $2.67 billion; after approximately $667 million in attorney fees (25%) and roughly $100 million in administration costs, approximately $1.9 billion remains for distribution to roughly 6 million claimants.
 
Average payout is approximately $333.
 
Claimants with a calculated distribution of $200 or more will receive 90% in the initial round, with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution.



Payments began going out in May 2026 and are still being distributed in June 2026.
 
Payments are released in waves rather than on a single day, so claimants who filed a valid claim before the deadline may still be waiting.
 
As of the most recent update, there is no universal confirmed second BCBS settlement distribution date announced for June 2026; in some settlements, a residual or second distribution can happen later, after uncashed, returned, or failed payments are reconciled.


The reserve structure warrants attention: the 10% holdback on claims ≥$200 is standard for large-population distributions to absorb address failures, returned checks, and claw-back disputes. Claimants with disputed claim determinations received notice starting February 2026 and have a narrow window to contest data from the defendant-produced premium records before the residual pool is closed.

### In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation — MDL 1720, E.D.N.Y. — Second Distribution Approved for September 2026; ~96,000 Merchants Reinstated

This is the cleanest positive delta in the past two weeks. 
On May 26, 2026, class counsel filed a motion requesting court approval of a second initial distribution of settlement funds; on June 15, 2026, the court approved the motion, finding that the proposed distribution is in the best interests of eligible claimants while protecting those whose claims are not yet ready; the second initial distribution is anticipated to occur in September 2026.



Under the claims process, merchants are to receive settlement payments equal to their pro rata share of the settlement fund based on Visa interchange fees they paid relative to the fees paid by all merchants; roughly 96,000 merchants were excluded from the first round of payment because of "variances between the merchant names in the data and the claims submitted."



The $5.5 billion settlement applies to merchants that accepted Visa and Mastercard branded cards in the United States from January 1, 2004, to January 25, 2019; the claim filing deadline was February 4, 2025.
 
Class counsel has requested this second distribution because outstanding legal disputes regarding payment facilitators (PayFacs) and Branded Operators, and challenges to fee amounts or ownership of claims, have made full payment of all claims impossible at this time.


Separately, the original MDL 1720 opt-out track in S.D.N.Y. has reached its endpoint. 
Visa and Mastercard settled the final interchange fee claims from a group of about five dozen merchants; the last settlement came with Circle K parent Alimentation Couche-Tard; U.S. District Judge Alvin Hellerstein in the Southern District of New York formally closed the litigation, after Amtrak, Crate & Barrel, Dick's Sporting Goods, Nike, and other merchants settled their claims over the past six weeks.
 That closure eliminates the opt-out appellate overhang that had complicated the class-side distribution for years.

A structurally distinct proceeding warrants a note of caution: 
a revised Visa and Mastercard settlement cleared its first major court hurdle when a federal judge granted preliminary approval of a revised $38 billion settlement; if finalized, the deal would reduce average U.S. credit interchange rates by 10 basis points for five years.
 
This newer settlement, making headlines in June 2026, is an entirely different settlement from MDL 1720; the $38 billion figure represents cumulative merchant savings on interchange rates over a five-year period, not a cash fund.
 Practitioners should take care not to conflate the two proceedings for claims-triage purposes.

### Carry-Forward: Broader AI Copyright Litigation Pipeline and Structural Implications of Bartz


Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training; in June 2025, Judge Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and also concluded that Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired — and nothing about this settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
If Judge Martínez-Olguín grants final approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and pay the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.



There are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages — including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others; several of those cases appear to be adopting the strategy that proved effective in Bartz: rather than challenging AI training as such, they focus on the underlying piracy and on infringing copies.
 The Bartz settlement thus functions less as a comprehensive resolution of the AI copyright problem and more as a liability-isolation transaction: it walls off Anthropic's piracy exposure while leaving the fair-use question structurally intact for every other defendant.


Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 Given the number of objectors who appeared at the May 14 hearing — several raising Ninth Circuit due-process arguments about compressed notice timelines — the appellate exposure post-approval is real and should be priced into any timeline assumption.

Authors and publishers with confirmed claims in *Bartz v. Anthropic* (No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) should monitor the docket at CourtListener ID 69058235 for a final approval order from Judge Martínez-Olguín — the July 1 filing remains unverified in content and may be the order itself; if final approval issues, the 30-day objector appeal window under FRAP 4(a)(1)(B) begins running immediately, and any claimant who filed an objection and intends to appeal should be positioned to file notice in the Ninth Circuit within that window. Merchants with claims in MDL 1720 (*Payment Card Interchange*, E.D.N.Y.) who were excluded from the first distribution because of name-variance issues should confirm their eligibility for the September 2026 second distribution by cross-checking the class administrator's portal now, before the September disbursement date is set in stone. BCBS subscriber claimants (N.D. Ala., MDL 2406) who have not received their initial-distribution payment should use the official claim-status tool at bcbssettlement.com with their claim number; the 10% reserve is standard and does not indicate claim denial. Rights-holders in the parallel AI copyright pipeline — particularly those with works in the OpenAI, Meta, or Adobe proceedings — should note that the Bartz fair-use ruling on legally acquired books now provides defense counsel a well-developed affirmative-use playbook; the litigation leverage in those cases rests almost entirely on establishing that the defendant sourced training data from unauthorized repositories, mirroring the LibGen/PiLiMi theory that produced the $1.5 billion Bartz settlement.
