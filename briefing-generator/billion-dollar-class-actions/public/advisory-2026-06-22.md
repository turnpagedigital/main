### I. Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 — Under Submission, Distribution Timeline at Risk

The $1.5 billion settlement — the largest copyright class-action settlement in U.S. history — sits in post-hearing limbo as of this briefing. 
After a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement, but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect final approval relatively quickly.
 The case was reassigned from the retired Judge William Alsup to Judge Martínez-Olguín on December 31, 2025, and the fairness hearing was originally set for April 2026 before being rescheduled to May 14 at Courtroom 12, 19th Floor, 450 Golden Gate Ave., San Francisco. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín issued an order to move the fairness hearing to May 14, 2026 at 2:00 p.m., with the hearing held at the San Francisco Federal Courthouse, 450 Golden Gate Ave., Courtroom 12 – 19th Floor.


The headline claims economics: 
class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate — vastly surpassing the 54 percent rate reported in counsel's March 19, 2026 Motion for Final Approval, indicating that a large number of claims came in at the close of the March 30 deadline.
 By the May 14 hearing, lead counsel Justin Nelson reported 
the participation rate had climbed further to 92.77 percent, with no indication of any change to the per-work payout of approximately $3,000 to $3,100.


The fee dispute is the court's principal remaining concern. 
Class counsel urged final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 At the May 14 hearing, 
Judge Martínez-Olguín's questions focused on attorneys' fees and the structure of the cost reserve, rather than the merits of the settlement.
 Separately, 
Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel was too high, with "scant justification" for the request.


The objector posture: 
the judge heard from seven objectors at the hearing, each given two minutes, and also questioned class counsel about service awards, cost reserves, attorneys' fees, and the fee multiplier.
 The most substantive structural objection came from Professor Lea Victoria Bishop, who raised transparency concerns: 
she alleged that certain concerns raised by the original judge were not communicated to Judge Martínez-Olguín, who ordered her objection and those of other objectors unsealed to allow greater transparency before issuing a final ruling.



Judge Martínez-Olguín has put the brakes on the settlement, declining final approval and demanding more detail on how the money will reach claimants and how much will go to attorneys — leaving the settlement in limbo just weeks before payouts were expected to begin.


The installment structure creates its own pressure: 
Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 Installment 2 (April 30) is confirmed funded; Installment 3 falls in September 2026. A delayed final-approval order does not reverse the funding obligation, but it delays the distribution waterfall — administration costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments cannot flow until the order issues and any appeal period runs. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.


Benchmark relevance: 
the Bartz settlement has been treated by the industry as a template, and other AI labs and rights holders are quietly using it as a benchmark in their own negotiations — if Martínez-Olguín approves the deal as written, a wave of similar settlements in the OpenAI, Meta, and Microsoft cases would be expected; if she requires material changes, leverage in every pending AI training case shifts toward plaintiffs.

### II. BCBS Subscriber Distribution — *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL 2406, No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.)

Distribution of the $2.67 billion gross settlement (approximately $1.9 billion net after fees and administration) is live and progressing. 
Class counsel filed the motion for distribution of the net settlement fund on April 23, 2026; Judge Manasco approved the distribution order on April 24, 2026, with a target start date of May 11, 2026.
 
The distribution plan covers nearly 7.8 million claimants.



Payments began going out in May 2026 and are still being distributed in June 2026, released in waves rather than on a single day, so many claimants who filed valid claims before the deadline may still be waiting in June 2026.


The mechanics of pro-rata distribution are as follows: 
the administrator distributes on a pro rata basis, with each approved claimant's payment proportional to their recognized claim; claimants whose calculated payment falls at or below a $5 de minimis threshold will not receive an initial distribution; claimants with calculated distributions below $200 receive their full payment in the initial round; and claimants with distributions of $200 or more receive 90% in the initial round with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution.



Epiq Class Action Services issued an official statement that Tier-1 distributions began the week of May 19, with prepaid debit cards going first; estimated total Tier-1 wave completion is by end of July 2026.
 This is the capstone event for a case that has been in the pipeline since 2013: 
the case was filed in 2013, preliminarily approved in November 2020, finally approved in August 2022, and held up by appellate review through October 2024 before claim allocation, fraud screening, and payment-method processing finally cleared the way for distribution.


The practical watch item for Tier-1 recipients with claims of $200 or more: the 10% reserve creates a second distribution event that will require another court-supervised allocation order before residual funds are disbursed. No timeline for that second distribution has been confirmed. The claim-filing deadline passed November 5, 2021; no new claims are being accepted.

### III. Visa/Mastercard Interchange MDL — MDL 1720 (E.D.N.Y.); Damages-Class Release Under Attack

The Visa/Mastercard interchange litigation — the largest antitrust class action in U.S. history by fund size — has a fresh procedural development that carries material exposure for the networks. 
On April 21, 2026, three merchants that are members of the damages class filed a motion for partial summary judgment in MDL 1720 seeking a declaration that the forward-looking release in the Amended Settlement Agreement resolving the damages-class claims is invalid and unenforceable under federal law.
 The release — which would bar class members from asserting future claims based on post-settlement interchange conduct — has long been a flashpoint. A ruling that voids it would significantly reopen Visa's and Mastercard's exposure on the right side of the settlement date.

On Visa's own financial posture as of its most recent 10-Q: 
for the six months ended March 31, 2026, Visa recorded additional accruals of $894 million and deposited $625 million into the U.S. litigation escrow account to address claims associated with the interchange MDL.
 
The current U.S. litigation escrow account balance is approximately $650 million, and if there is no settlement of remaining claims, the range of damages exposure on currently unresolved claims is approximately $15–$20 billion through calendar year 2025, undiscounted and before any legal challenge to the claims.


The individual-merchant opt-out litigation has reached a conclusion in the S.D.N.Y. track. 
Visa and Mastercard settled the final interchange fee claims from a group of approximately five dozen merchants in recent weeks in an antitrust case set for trial, with the last settlement closing with Circle K parent Alimentation Couche-Tard; U.S. District Judge Alvin Hellerstein in the Southern District of New York formally closed that litigation, with Amtrak, Crate & Barrel, Dick's Sporting Goods, Nike, and others settling over the prior six weeks.
 That closure of the opt-out track does not resolve the damages-class action or the pending partial-summary-judgment challenge to the forward-looking release. 
On November 10, 2025, Visa and Mastercard entered into a superseding and amended settlement agreement to resolve the Injunctive Relief Class claims, and the Injunctive Relief Class plaintiffs filed a motion for preliminary approval.
 The injunctive-class approval track and the release-validity motion are now the two live exposures.

The claims-filing deadline for the damages class was February 4, 2025. 
The Class Administrator is currently reviewing submissions and will calculate the amount due to each approved class member based on valid claims; the court must approve the calculated amounts before distribution.
 No distribution timeline has been confirmed.

### IV. Keller Postman / Google Mass Arbitration — Advertiser Overcharge Claims, ~$218 Billion

This is the structurally most significant development in the mass-arbitration space since the Intuit / DoorDash wave. 
Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.


The theory rests entirely on liability already adjudicated: 
federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) ruled in favor of the DOJ, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
Keller estimates potential claims for online search and display ads could reach $218 billion or more, based on calculations from an economist his firm has hired.


The structural mechanics: 
many companies that purchased advertising through Google have sued for damages since the 2024 rulings, but advertiser contracts with Google require mandatory arbitration over legal disputes; mass arbitration — where 25 or more claims against the same company are pooled together — has become more common and provides greater likelihood of settlement awards for claimants.
 
The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most group proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.


The damages calculus: 
the estimated overcharge is 5–10% of ad spend; with treble damages under Clayton Act § 4, that translates to up to 30% of total Google Ads spend over the period — meaning a company with $100 million in Google Ads spend since 2016 could have a claim worth up to $30 million.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.



The $218 billion figure is an estimate, not a filed demand amount; in mass arbitration, the leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger, not from any single damages number.
 That fee-obligation dynamic — the mechanism that forced DoorDash and Intuit to settle at scale — is the practical lever here. Google's arbitration clauses trigger AAA or JAMS mass-arbitration supplementary rules at 25 or more simultaneous coordinated claims; at thousands of filings, per-claim administrative fees become a nine-figure liability in their own right, independent of the merits. 
Keller Postman cautions that deadlines vary by state, and that earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.



Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad-tech case remains pending; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines while the remedies phase concludes.
 A divestiture order in the ad-tech case would strengthen the damages theory substantially.
