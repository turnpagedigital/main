### I. Visa/Mastercard Interchange — In re Payment Card Interchange Fee & Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation: Preliminary Approval Granted June 9, E.D.N.Y.

On Tuesday, June 9, U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan in the Eastern District of New York granted preliminary approval to Visa's and Mastercard's revised $38 billion settlement with merchants who accused the card networks of charging too much to process payments on their credit cards — ruling nearly two years after a different judge rejected a proposed $30 billion settlement as too small.

Judge Cogan said the settlement covering more than 12 million merchants was "fair, reasonable, and adequate," and signaled that he was likely to eventually grant final approval. The procedural history here is critical for claims-recovery counsel: in rejecting the prior $30 billion accord in June 2024, U.S. District Judge Margo Brodie had found that fees would have still been above where they were absent any antitrust violations, and merchants would have remained stuck with the "Honor All Cards" rule. The revised settlement announced in November 2025 directly addressed both sticking points.

Two experts hired by the plaintiffs — Nobel Prize-winning economist Joseph Stiglitz and University of Washington professor Keith Leffler — said the changes could save merchants $38 billion by 2031 and provide $224 billion of benefits overall, including to consumers. The class-action suit filed by more than 12 million merchants in 2005 accused Visa and Mastercard of charging excessive processing fees; in 2025, the card networks charged merchants almost $119 billion in swipe fees.

The preliminary-approval ruling does not end the battle. Groups including the National Retail Federation — the world's largest retail trade group — also opposed the new settlement and plan further challenges. The NRF stated that "the proposed settlement offers no meaningful relief and leaves intact the underlying system that enables Visa and Mastercard to dictate the rules and costs that merchants and consumers must bear." The organization added that it looked forward to "participating in the next phase of the proceedings."

For counsel advising merchant-class members: the preliminary-approval order initiates the formal notice and opt-out window. Given the NRF's stated opposition and the prior rejection history in this docket, objections at the final-approval stage are near-certain. Practitioners should track whether the NRF or the Merchants Payments Coalition file formal objections before the final-approval hearing, and monitor whether Judge Cogan sets any supplemental briefing schedule analogous to Judge Martínez-Olguín's post-hearing orders in Bartz.

### II. Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Distribution Calculation Date Is Today; Final Approval Order Still Pending

The most operationally significant development in Bartz on this date is one that will largely occur off-docket: June 11, 2026 is the date on which the Settlement Administrator calculates distributions (subject to change), with payment disbursement expected to begin June 2026 or later. That calculation, administered by JND Legal Administration at AnthropicCopyrightSettlement.com, is itself contingent on the entry of a final approval order — which, as of this writing, has not yet been entered.

The chronology leading to today's inflection point: the fairness hearing in Bartz v. Anthropic took place on Thursday, May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California. After the 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expected the judge to give final approval relatively quickly.

The post-hearing procedural posture is defined by a targeted supplemental-briefing order: by no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic was permitted to file a supplemental brief of no more than 2 pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; by that same date, plaintiffs were to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026. The court stated it would not require anything further from objectors nor consider any further submissions from them. The final approval order has not yet been entered as of today's briefing window, which means distribution calculations scheduled for today remain subject to that contingency.

The claims-rate data at the hearing was extraordinary. Class counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work in the class will be $3,100, with a 92.77% participation rate for works in the works list. Class counsel had previously filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10 percent). The high rate of claim means the per-work payout will likely end up being closer to the original $3,000 per work figure rather than the elevated per-work amounts that were floated during the period of lower claiming activity.

Fee and cost structure: Anthropic will pay $1.5 billion into a settlement fund, with plaintiff lawyers seeking 25% of the fund — $375 million — in attorneys' fees, plus administrative costs through JND Legal Administration and service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives). Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.

The downstream litigation environment warrants close attention. Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — have filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. Anthropic, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors. There are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages — including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others.

### III. Keller Postman Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — ~$218 Billion Campaign in Motion

On May 11, 2026, Keller Postman announced it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings that found the company engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem. The campaign is structurally distinct from class litigation precisely because of a contractual barrier that has become a strategic asset for claimants: arbitration clauses Google has in its advertisers' contracts effectively prevent damages via class action litigation; instead, advertisers can join together to make claims as part of group arbitration, which gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations.

The two predicate liability rulings are foundational to the claims theory. Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business. According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade; experts in the federal antitrust cases estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs, creating the potential for substantial recovery under federal antitrust law.

The damages arithmetic: Keller estimates potential claims for online search and display ads could reach $218 billion or more, based on calculations from an economist his firm has hired. Potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law. Any business that purchased Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016 may be eligible to participate. While prior public litigation has focused largely on publisher-side claims, advertiser claims represent a significantly larger portion of the digital advertising market.

Critically, the ad tech remedies picture remains unsettled. Both the Department of Justice and Google filed competing remedies proposals in November 2025, with the government seeking forced divestiture of Google's AdX exchange and publisher ad server, while Google argued behavioral changes would suffice; Google concluded its advertising technology remedies trial on November 21, 2025, and a court ruling on remedies in that case remains pending. On the search side, Google filed an appeal in January 2026 seeking to pause data-sharing mandates imposed by the September 2025 remedies ruling in the Mehta case, adding further legal uncertainty to the regulatory picture. The still-pending remedies landscape means that advertiser-claimants filing now are doing so against a defendant simultaneously contesting the scope and form of structural relief — a posture that creates both pressure on settlement timing and strategic complexity in structuring individual arbitration filings.

In addition to representing private claimants in arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel case. The dual private-public posture historically accelerates settlement conversations. Practitioners tracking DoorDash and TurboTax precedents — the mass arbitration campaigns that established Keller Postman's template — should note that the Amazon Alexa campaign prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands, and the TurboTax campaign resolved in a confidential settlement alongside a $141 million FTC fund.

### IV. Broader Mega-Class Docket: Pay-to-Play Scrutiny and Lingering Structural Risks

The Reuters headline flagging pay-to-play claims in class action fee allocation — surfacing in the context of a pension fund rematch dispute — highlights a structural tension that practitioners managing large settlement classes cannot ignore in the current environment. Courts have grown increasingly scrutinizing of fee-allocation arrangements, lead-plaintiff selection processes, and the conflict between institutional investors (pension funds, sovereign funds) and law firms competing for leadership positions in securities mega-class actions. While the specific docket underlying the Reuters piece could not be fully extracted from the feed, the thematic signal is clear and consistent with what Judge Martínez-Olguín's own questioning in Bartz reflected: fee multipliers, cost reserves, and service-award levels are live judicial pressure points in every large class action at the final-approval stage.

This scrutiny is structurally relevant to the securities mega-cycle. Counsel tracking the Roundup litigation inventory should note that Bayer's global mass tort resolution strategy remains under development following adverse jury verdicts; in the current mega-class and mass-arbitration environment, defendants in the Visa/Mastercard posture who face both NRF-style institutional objectors and residual opt-out plaintiffs face the longest tail on claims resolution. The pay-to-play fee-allocation issue, once adjudicated, will create precedent governing how institutional investors may negotiate leadership-fee splits in future mega-settlements.
