### *In re: Fidelity Investments Data Breach Litigation*, No. 1:24-CV-12601-LTS (D. Mass.) — Final Approval Entered; Claims Deadline July 27


The case — *In re: Fidelity Investments Data Breach Litigation*, Case No. 1:24-CV-12601-LTS — is pending in the United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts.
 
U.S. District Judge Leo T. Sorokin granted preliminary approval, finding that the proposed nationwide class presents common legal and factual questions.
 
The settlement was approved at the final fairness hearing on July 9, 2026.


The fund is small — 
Fidelity agreed to pay $2.5 million to resolve claims it failed to prevent a 2024 data breach that compromised sensitive consumer information
 — but structurally the settlement follows the standard claims-made architecture. 
The class covers individuals who received a data breach notice from Fidelity informing them their financial account number and routing number were compromised between Aug. 17 and Aug. 19, 2024.
 
More than 160,000 people may be eligible for the settlement.
 The three-tier benefit structure: 
up to $5,000 reimbursement for documented out-of-pocket losses incurred between Aug. 17, 2024, and July 27, 2026; a pro rata cash payment of approximately $100 for those without proof; and two years of free credit monitoring from CyEx, including $1 million in fraud and identity theft insurance.
 
California class members can also receive an additional $50 CCPA payment.


The claims window is still open. 
Claimants who do not file by July 27, 2026 will not receive benefits or payments and will give up the right to be part of another lawsuit related to the legal claims in this case.
 
If there are no appeals, attorneys' fees and the lead plaintiffs will be paid first; the remaining funds will then be distributed among class members who submitted valid forms.


The collateral regulatory dimension is worth noting: 
the Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth William Galvin issued a $1.25 million fine against Fidelity Brokerage Services, finding that the company's insufficient enforcement of its own cybersecurity protocols allowed the breach to occur over a three-day period.
 
That regulatory penalty is separate from the class action settlement — it goes to the state, not to consumers.


At $2.5M total and over 160,000 potential class members, the arithmetic is tight. The pro rata payment floor of approximately $100 per claimant assumes a low filing rate; a high participation rate will compress individual recoveries proportionally. Claimants with documented losses above $100 should prioritize the documented-loss tier.

### *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Sub Judice; Late Opt-Out Now the Key Obstacle

The $1.5 billion Bartz settlement — the largest copyright class action settlement in U.S. history — remains under submission before Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín in San Francisco following the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing. No final approval order has issued.

The fairness hearing itself was largely uneventful. 
After a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly.
 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson confirmed the projected payout per work in the class will be $3,100.
 
The class participation rate for works on the works list reached 92.77%
 — a figure without precedent in a settlement of this scale; the median class-action claim rate is approximately 10%. 
The Judge heard from seven objectors, each of whom spoke for two minutes, except attorney James Bartolomei who was given five minutes to speak on behalf of several objectors.


Post-hearing, the court issued a sequencing order. 
By no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic was permitted to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; Plaintiffs were ordered to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 [Dkt. 646-1]; the Court stated it would not consider any further submissions from objectors.


The most recent docket activity is the late opt-out application. 
Music industry attorney Donald S. Passman filed an Application for Leave to Submit Late Request for Exclusion from Class Action Settlement, and in the alternative a Conditional Objection to Final Approval, supported by a Declaration and four exhibits, filed June 30, 2026 and entered July 1, 2026 (Dkt. 676).
 
In its supplemental filing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any record supporting relief.
 Class counsel's opposition to the Passman late opt-out (Dkt. 679) was filed July 13, 2026 — the last entry on the CourtListener docket as of this run. Judge Martínez-Olguín has not ruled.

The payment structure is installment-based and already partially funded: 
the $1.5 billion minimum settlement is being funded by Anthropic in multiple installments; the first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 
Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 Distribution to class members waits on final approval and any appeal period. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved; payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest.


One fee dispute remains live. 
Authors urged the court to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Separately, Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel is far too high, calling the request lacking in "scant justification."
 The fee-award decision will reduce the net distributable fund; at $187.5M in class counsel fees plus any non-class counsel award, the total fee load could reach 17–18% of the $1.5 billion face value before administration costs.

The key structural observation for rights-holders: 
for Anthropic, the settlement releases it from liability for past conduct in exchange for the $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies; it does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme and does not bind any other AI developer or any future case.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — $218B Demand; Arbitration Filings Underway

The largest mass arbitration campaign by claimed dollar value continues to ramp. 
Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist Keller Postman has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability.


The legal predicate rests on two distinct adverse findings: 
in August 2024, Judge Amit Mehta ruled that Google illegally maintained a monopoly in general search services and search text advertising; then, on April 17, 2025, a second federal court ruled against Google on ad tech antitrust grounds.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.
 
Potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law
 — i.e., Clayton Act § 4.

The arbitration-funnel mechanics are straightforward and worth understanding. 
Due to contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, these claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation; Keller Postman is coordinating thousands of individual claims on behalf of businesses nationwide, allowing each claimant to pursue recovery based on its own advertising spend.
 
Keller told Bloomberg in April 2026 that he had already signed up a "significant number" of advertisers and expected to file the first claims that week.
 
The firm cautions that deadlines vary by state, and that earlier action increases the probability of a successful claim being filed within applicable statutes of limitations.


One structural advantage of mass arbitration over class litigation: 
for many big advertisers — including multiple major businesses with hundreds of millions of dollars spent on Google media per year — there may be hesitation to be the face of an anti-Google movement; in mass anonymous arbitration, big brands can pursue settlements without running afoul of delicate account etiquette or ruining a key media relationship.


The campaign's scale precedent is also informative. 
Ashley Keller is the architect of some of the most significant mass arbitration campaigns in history — including the Amazon Alexa campaign that prompted Amazon to remove its mandatory arbitration clause entirely after 75,000 demands, and the TurboTax campaign that ran parallel to FTC enforcement and resolved in a confidential settlement alongside a $141 million FTC fund.
 The Google campaign, with $218 billion in aggregate claimed damages, eclipses both.

The proceedings are largely private. 
Because these proceedings take place in private arbitration, they have remained largely outside the public record.
 No public ruling or settlement announcement has issued as of this run.

### Broader Mass Arbitration Landscape — AAA Rule Updates, Bellwether Trends, Defense Counter-Moves


The AAA closed 26 mass arbitrations with 37,648+ claimants in H1 2025 alone; AAA updated its Consumer & Employment Arbitration Rules effective May 1, 2025; and the Ninth Circuit upheld JAMS consolidation of 7,300 Starz claims in *Jones v. Starz Entertainment*.


Defense postures are hardening. 
Companies are fighting back with bellwether provisions, fee non-payment strategies (Samsung), and suing plaintiffs' firms (Sega, Tubi); most of these defenses have failed or backfired — Samsung's fee refusal opened federal court instead of closing the case.
 
The plaintiff bar's structural advantage remains intact heading into 2026.


The next wave of mass arbitration candidates is taking shape: 
biometric data (BIPA), video privacy (VPPA), and California wiretapping (CIPA) are fueling the next wave; every app, streaming service, and healthcare platform with a tracking pixel is a potential target.

## Recommended Actions

*Bartz* claimants should treat the Passman late opt-out application (Dkt. 676, filed June 30, 2026) as a potential short-term speed bump — it does not threaten the settlement on the merits, but it gives Judge Martínez-Olguín a procedural reason to hold the final approval order until she resolves late-exclusion requests; rights-holders should not expect checks before late fall 2026 at the earliest, and the September 25, 2026 Anthropic installment ($450M, Schedule Payment 1) will not be distributable until after any appeal period clears. Fidelity class members with documented identity-theft losses should file under the Tier 1 documented-loss track before the July 27, 2026 deadline — the $100 pro-rata floor is modest, but the $5,000 documented-loss ceiling is meaningful for anyone who can show bank statements or fraud-remediation receipts; do not miss the deadline, which is firm and court-imposed. Advertisers who have spent material sums on Google Search or Display since August 2016 should evaluate the Keller Postman mass-arbitration campaign now: statutes of limitations vary by state, the Clayton Act § 4 treble-damages multiplier is substantial, and the federal monopoly findings eliminate the need to re-litigate liability; the principal residual risk is that Google's advertiser contracts' arbitration provisions face a challenge to their scope or enforceability, which could return some claims to federal court — a complication that would, ironically, favor claimants with larger individual damages exposure.
