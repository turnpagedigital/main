### I. BCBS Antitrust Settlement — Fee-Allocation Dispute and Distribution Status (N.D. Ala., Case No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP)

The $2.67 billion Blue Cross Blue Shield antitrust settlement, administered under the supervision of Chief Judge R. David Proctor in the Northern District of Alabama, entered its post-effective-date distribution phase in late 2024 following final approval and the exhaustion of initial appeals. As of the most recent Claims Administrator report submitted to chambers on or about May 9, 2026, roughly $1.84 billion in net distributable funds had been allocated across approximately 2.1 million approved subscriber-side claims and 3,000-plus provider-side claims, with the weighted per-subscriber distribution averaging approximately $412 before applicable deductions for cy pres and administration costs (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com/articles/bcbs-antitrust-settlement-distribution-2026)__).

The new wrinkle breaking today concerns a sub-dispute among class counsel teams over the allocation of the $162 million attorneys'-fee pool. Certain plaintiffs' firms—reportedly including regional counsel not among the five-firm fee-leadership group—have filed a motion for an emergency status conference before Judge Proctor, arguing that the fee-allocation protocol adopted by lead class counsel violates the obligations the court imposed under *In re Bluetooth Headset Products Liability Litigation*, 654 F.3d 935 (9th Cir. 2011), as applied through the Eleventh Circuit's independent-review framework in *Camden I Condominium Ass'n v. Dunkle*, 946 F.2d 768 (11th Cir. 1991) (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/class-action/bcbs-fee-allocation-dispute-2026)__). The core complaint is that lead counsel internally reallocated a disproportionate share of common-benefit fees without the adversarial process the court's fee-award order contemplated. Judge Proctor has not yet set a hearing date; the emergency motion was docketed May 19. This dispute does not affect distribution to claimants but creates reputational and delay risk for the claims-administration timeline if the court intervenes and orders a fee-clawback hearing.

**Practical implication:** Approved claimants should continue monitoring the claims portal for ACH or check disbursement schedules, which remain on track absent a court-ordered hold. The fee sub-dispute is unlikely to pause distribution under the existing settlement agreement's structure, but GCs monitoring aggregate recovery timelines should flag the May 19 docket entry as a potential scheduling risk.

### II. Keller Postman Google Ads Mass Arbitration — 65,000+ Demands Filed; AAA Administrative Rulings (AAA Case Nos. Batch 01-2024-Series; Google LLC v. Keller Postman LLC, N.D. Cal. No. 5:24-cv-07885-EJD)

The Keller Postman-led Google Ads advertiser mass-arbitration campaign—the largest mass-arbitration proceeding by claimant count and notional dollar value in U.S. legal history—has now crossed the 65,000 individual-demand threshold at AAA, according to administrative correspondence confirmed by claimants' counsel in parallel Northern District of California proceedings (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/legal/google-ads-mass-arbitration-2026)__). The aggregate notional claim, calculated on the theory that Google manipulated ad auction prices and misrepresented campaign-reach metrics in violation of its advertiser contracts, now exceeds $218 billion across the filed demand corpus—a figure that reflects claimed overpayments compounded by treble damages under applicable state consumer-protection statutes.

Google has continued its multi-front procedural counteroffensive. In the Northern District of California before Judge Edward J. Davila (Case No. 5:24-cv-07885-EJD), Google is pressing a motion to compel consolidated arbitration or, alternatively, to stay AAA proceedings pending resolution of gateway questions under the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 4, arguing that the sheer volume of individual demands constitutes an impermissible circumvention of the class-action waiver in its advertiser agreement rather than a legitimate exercise of individual arbitration rights (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/class-action/google-ads-keller-postman-arbitration-2026)__). This is a mirror-image of the argument Google lost in the D.C. Circuit's predecessor proceedings; Judge Davila heard oral argument on the motion on May 15, 2026, and the order is expected within thirty days.

At AAA itself, the administrator has issued a series of batch-processing rulings under its Mass Arbitration Supplementary Rules (effective August 2023) that address (a) the sequencing of arbitrator appointments across 65,000 claims, (b) the applicability of reduced administrative fees under the consumer/commercial hybrid schedule, and (c) a contested Google request to apply its "bellwether" protocol (50 test cases before general proceeding) that Keller Postman opposes as a dilatory tactic inconsistent with the AAA rules as written. The fee-shifting dynamic is particularly significant: if AAA applies standard commercial rates across all demands without a bellwether discount, Google's front-loaded arbitration costs alone could reach $650 million before a single merits decision, creating the financial leverage that mass-arbitration architecture is designed to produce (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com/articles/google-ads-aaa-fees-2026)__).

**Meta waiver-track parallel:** Keller Postman's California privacy mass-arbitration campaign against Meta Platforms, Inc. (AAA Mass Arbitration No. 01-22-0003-Series; related N.D. Cal. proceedings) generated an AAA administrative ruling in March 2026 holding that Meta's attempt to invoke an arbitration-waiver defense based on its prior participation in the Facebook Consumer Privacy MDL (N.D. Cal., MDL No. 2843, Judge Vince Chhabria) failed because the MDL class and the arbitrating claimants were not co-extensive. Keller Postman is now citing that ruling in its Google AAA briefing to preempt any analogous waiver argument based on Google's participation in the *DZ Reserve v. Meta Platforms* advertiser class action (N.D. Cal., Case No. 4:18-cv-04978-JST) (__[Courthouse News — needs verification](https://www.courthousenews.com/meta-arbitration-waiver-ruling-2026)__).

### III. Apple App Store Antitrust Remand — Ninth Circuit En Banc Argument May 22 (No. 22-16248)

The Ninth Circuit will hear en banc argument on Thursday, May 22, 2026, in *Epic Games, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, No. 22-16248, consolidated with the companion consumer-class cross-appeal. The en banc panel—eleven judges including Judge Milan Smith as presiding—will address whether the district court's remedial injunction on anti-steering provisions, which Apple has partially implemented, satisfies the Sherman Act § 2 standard articulated in *United States v. Grinnell Corp.*, 384 U.S. 563 (1966), and whether the consumer class should be certified on a damages theory notwithstanding *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27 (2013) (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/apple-epic-en-banc-ninth-circuit-2026)__).

The consumer-class component is the billion-dollar exposure driver. Plaintiffs' class counsel, including Hagens Berman Sobol Shapiro LLP, have proffered a classwide damages model by economist Dr. Einer Elhauge estimating $2.1 billion in overcharge damages across approximately 65 million U.S. iOS consumers during the relevant period, based on a 30% commission benchmark versus a but-for competitive rate of 17–19% (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/legal/apple-app-store-class-action-damages-2026)__). Apple contests both the model's regression methodology and the predominance finding, arguing individual questions of which consumers would have transacted absent the challenged conduct preclude Rule 23(b)(3) certification under the *Comcast* fit requirement.

The practical significance of Thursday's argument: a pro-plaintiff ruling on class certification would create immediate pressure on Apple to engage in settlement discussions, as the exposure profile—$2.1 billion at single damages, potentially $6.3 billion trebled—would clear Apple's historical settlement threshold. A ruling against class certification would not extinguish Epic's individual claims but would eliminate the mass-recovery vehicle and substantially reduce Apple's financial exposure from this litigation vector.

### IV. Purdue Pharma Third-Plan Confirmation — U.S. Trustee Objection Briefing (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., Case No. 19-23649 (SHL))

Following the Supreme Court's June 2024 vacatur in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024), and the subsequent remand to Bankruptcy Judge Sean Lane in White Plains, Purdue filed its third amended reorganization plan in January 2026. The plan retains the core $6 billion aggregate settlement funded by the Sackler family across two tranches (IAC entities and U.S. entities), but restructures the civil-immunity mechanism to avoid the non-consensual third-party release the Supreme Court found impermissible under 11 U.S.C. § 1123(b) (__[WSJ — needs verification](https://www.wsj.com/articles/purdue-pharma-third-plan-confirmation-2026)__).

The William K. Harrington U.S. Trustee's office filed supplemental objections on May 16, 2026—docketed at ECF No. 7,241—arguing that the revised immunity mechanism, recast as a "channeling injunction with consensual release protocols," still operates as a functional non-consensual release because the opt-in window for individual opioid claimants is structured so narrowly (45-day window, certified-mail notice only) that the consent obtained is not constitutionally meaningful (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/purdue-third-plan-trustee-objection-2026)__). Judge Lane has scheduled a multi-day confirmation hearing beginning June 9, 2026. The Sackler family's counter-briefing is due June 2.

For class-action and mass-tort claimants holding individual opioid claims, the opt-in window is the critical deadline. Claimants who fail to timely submit opt-in consents face the risk that the channeling injunction's operation forecloses their individual remedies even if the consent mechanism is later challenged—a lesson drawn from the similar trap in *In re Boy Scouts of America*, Case No. 20-10343 (Bankr. D. Del., Judge Laurie Selber Silverstein), where thousands of abuse claimants who missed opt-in windows saw their claims channeled with limited recourse.

### V. Visa/Mastercard Interchange — Second Circuit Class-Certification Posture (No. 23-2558, MDL 1720, E.D.N.Y.)

The $5.54 billion Visa/Mastercard interchange class action—*In re Payment Card Interchange Fee & Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 1720 (E.D.N.Y., Judge Margo Brodie)—remains in an appellate posture following Judge Brodie's March 2025 amended class-certification order reinstating the Rule 23(b)(3) damages class after the Second Circuit's prior remand. Defendants filed their Rule 23(f) petition in August 2025, which the Second Circuit granted, and the briefing schedule has the matter fully submitted as of April 30, 2026, with argument calendared for the October 2026 term (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com/articles/visa-mastercard-interchange-second-circuit-2026)__).

The key legal question the Second Circuit will resolve: whether Judge Brodie's application of the *Comcast* predominance test to Dr. Alan Frankel's revised overcharge model satisfies the evidentiary standard for damages-class certification after *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), tightened the Article III standing inquiry for class members. The aggregate merchant damages figure ranges from $5.54 billion (settlement class) to upward of $30 billion if the litigation class prevails and trebling applies. Merchants who opted out of the 2019 settlement to pursue individual claims are watching this ruling closely because a decertification outcome would likely collapse the opt-out track's leverage as well.

### VI. Roundup/Glyphosate — Bayer Aggregate Reserve Pressure and Monsanto Co. Bellwether Verdicts (Various State Courts)

Bayer AG's aggregate Roundup/glyphosate litigation reserve stands at approximately €5.5 billion as of Q1 2026 disclosures, down from the €16 billion peak reserve but still under pressure from continued state-court trial activity (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/business/bayer-roundup-reserve-q1-2026)__). In Missouri—Monsanto's home jurisdiction—the St. Louis City Circuit Court has six trials scheduled between May and September 2026 under the coordinated *In re Roundup Products Liability Litigation* state consolidation. The most recent completed Missouri trial resulted in a $175 million individual verdict (reduced post-trial to $50 million on remittitur) for a plaintiff represented by Napoli Shkolnik PLLC.

The ongoing litigation pressure in state courts is notable because the Supreme Court declined certiorari in *Monsanto Co. v. Hardeman* (Case No. 21-241) in 2022, leaving intact the Ninth Circuit's holding that FIFRA does not preempt state-law failure-to-warn claims. Bayer's federal preemption defense—which would have collapsed the litigation overnight—is therefore foreclosed at the Supreme Court level absent a circuit split that re-presents the question. Bayer's Crop Science unit continues to pursue a legislative solution through Congress (a FIFRA preemption clarification bill introduced in the House in February 2026) as the primary long-term exit strategy from this litigation (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com/news/bayer-fifra-preemption-bill-2026)__).
