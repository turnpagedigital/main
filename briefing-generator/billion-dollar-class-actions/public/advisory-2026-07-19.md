# 💰 $1B+ Class Actions & Mass Arb | Sunday, July 19, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**Today's lede covers three active pressure points:** (1) the *Hasson v. Comcast* fee motion now sitting in Judge Younge's queue ahead of the rescheduled August 5 final approval hearing — the $39.2M fee ask is the single largest variable still compressing net recovery for ~35 million class members; (2) *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.), where final approval remains pending before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín following her May 14 fairness hearing, with the late-opt-out dispute and fee-multiplier scrutiny still unresolved as of the most recent docket activity on July 13, 2026; and (3) the Keller Postman Google advertiser mass arbitration campaign, which formalized in May 2026 and is the largest-by-dollar-value mass arbitration ever filed, anchored to two established federal liability rulings. Each carries a distinct denominator problem for claimants: Comcast's claims-made structure means every incremental filer dilutes the ~$50 flat-payment pool; Bartz's 92.77% claim rate has already collapsed per-work recovery to approximately $3,100; and the Google campaign's per-claimant upside hinges entirely on individual ad-spend documentation — no class-wide average will hold.

### *Hasson v. Comcast Cable Communications, LLC* — Fee Motion Pending; Final Approval August 5


*Hasson v. Comcast Cable Communications, LLC*, No. 2:23-cv-05039-JMY, is pending in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania before Hon. John M. Younge.
 
The Court is scheduled to hold a final approval hearing on August 5, 2026 at 12:30 p.m. ET at the James A. Byrne U.S. Courthouse, 601 Market Street, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19106.
 That date is itself a product of a schedule modification: 
a final approval hearing was originally scheduled for July 7 but has been moved to August 5, 2026.



More than 35 million current and former Xfinity customers have until August 14, 2026 to claim their share of a $117.5 million settlement tied to one of the largest consumer data breaches disclosed in the past two years.
 — though that deadline too was extended. 
The claim deadline has been extended to September 14, 2026, and the final approval hearing has been rescheduled for August 5, 2026.


The fee motion is the most consequential pending submission. 
Class Counsel will ask the Court to approve attorneys' fees up to one-third of the Settlement Fund — approximately $39,170,000 — plus reimbursement of litigation expenses; the motion for fees was due May 11, 2026 and any award is paid from the $117.5 million fund.
 
The $117.5 million fund will be reduced by up to roughly $39.2 million in attorneys' fees, about $7.3 million in administration costs, and service awards of $5,000 for each of the 11 class representatives.
 
The remainder is what claimants split.
 That arithmetic leaves roughly $71 million in net distributable proceeds across a class of up to 35 million eligible members — making the denominator the decisive variable for per-claimant recovery.


The amended consolidated complaint asserts 23 causes of action, including violations of the federal Cable Communications Policy Act (47 U.S.C. § 521), state consumer protection statutes, negligence, unjust enrichment, and breach of implied contract.
 
Co-Lead Counsel are Gary F. Lynch of Lynch Carpenter LLP and Norman E. Siegel of Stueve Siegel Hanson LLP; over twenty related cases were filed across federal courts in Pennsylvania, Florida, Illinois, South Carolina, Nevada, and Minnesota, then consolidated before Judge Younge as Master File No. 2:23-cv-05039-JMY.


The settlement's benefit structure is claims-made, not pro-rata off a fixed class: 
eligible claimants can receive a flat cash payment of roughly $50 with no documentation, or up to $10,000 in reimbursements for documented losses and lost time.
 
Each of the 11 class representatives — Patricia Andros, Michelle Birnie, Jessica Durham, Ryan Emmett, Vince Estevez, Alexander Nunn, Steven Prescott, Robert Smith, Veronica Verdier, Marcia Proto Wilson, and Jodi Wolfson — may receive a $5,000 service award subject to Court approval, totaling $55,000.


The operative fee standard will be percentage-of-fund against a lodestar cross-check. One-third of $117.5 million is on the high end of the range courts approve in large consumer data-breach settlements — most cluster between 25% and 30% — and Judge Younge may apply downward pressure consistent with Third Circuit norms. If the court reduces the fee request even to 30%, approximately $3.5 million more flows to class members. The opt-out deadline of July 1, 2026 has now passed, 
as class members had to submit exclusion requests by that date through the opt-out portal.
 Any opt-out tally submitted to the court before August 5 will inform Judge Younge's adequacy analysis under FRCP 23(e)(2).

### *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Final Approval Still Pending; Fee Scrutiny Active; Docket Entry 679 (Jul. 13)


Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement in the *Bartz v. Anthropic* copyright infringement lawsuit.
 
In a supplemental filing ordered after the fairness hearing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs from the proposed $1.5 billion settlement, arguing that the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any record supporting relief.
 
Final approval remains pending.


The docket confirms ongoing activity. Per the tracked-case ground truth, Docket Entry 679, filed July 13, 2026, is Anthropic's opposition to objections — the most recent filing as of this run. The case is *Bartz et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417, before Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín, N.D. Cal. (San Francisco, now relocating to Oakland effective May 26, 2026). 
Effective May 26, 2026, Judge Martínez-Olguín's courtroom and chambers moved to the Ronald V. Dellums Federal Building and United States Courthouse, 1301 Clay Street, Oakland, CA 94612.


The economics at this stage are fixed. 
Class counsel in the *Bartz v. Anthropic* lawsuit reported that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate.
 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson reported that the payout per work in the class will be $3,100.
 That figure collapsed from earlier projections when the March 30, 2026 deadline produced a late surge: 
the final claims rate vastly surpassed the 54 percent rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed on March 19, 2026, indicating that a large number of claims came in at the very end of the claims process.


The fee question is the remaining live variable before the court. 
Plaintiff lawyers are seeking 25% of the fund, or $375 million — a sum that illustrates the business model of class action litigation — plus administrative costs, service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff, and Special Master fees.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín questioned Class Counsel Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman about various amounts sought for service awards, cost reserves, attorneys' fees, and the fee multiplier.
 The 25% request on a $1.5 billion fund will almost certainly draw lodestar scrutiny, and any reduction in the multiplier shifts dollars to the per-work distribution.


Judge Martínez-Olguín put the brakes on what would be the largest copyright class action settlement in U.S. history, declining to grant final approval and demanding more detail on how the money will actually reach claimants and how much will go to plaintiffs' attorneys.
 
The Bartz settlement has been treated by the industry as a template — a number that other AI labs and rights holders are quietly using as a benchmark in their own negotiations.
 
If Martínez-Olguín ultimately approves the deal as written, expect a wave of similar class settlements in the OpenAI, Meta, and Microsoft cases; if she requires material changes — higher per-work payments, broader injunctive relief, or stronger deletion provisions — the leverage in every pending AI training case shifts toward plaintiffs.


The structural question underlying the pending order is the scope of the release. 
The settlement's scope has been widely misunderstood: Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training.
 
For Anthropic, the settlement releases them from liability for past conduct in exchange for a $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies; it does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme, and it does not bind any other AI developer or any future case.
 Anthropic's payment schedule runs in tranches: 
Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 Any delay in the final approval order does not necessarily alter the payment schedule, but it does keep Anthropic legally exposed on the underlying conduct pending appeal.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Largest-Dollar-Value Campaign on Record


Keller Postman, a Chicago-based litigation firm, announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing thousands of U.S. businesses in individual arbitration proceedings against Google, following two landmark federal antitrust rulings that found the company engaged in monopolistic behavior across key segments of the digital advertising ecosystem.


The liability predicate is established by two separate courts. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) have ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
Both the Department of Justice and Google filed competing remedies proposals in November 2025, with the government seeking forced divestiture of Google's AdX exchange and publisher ad server, while Google argued that behavioral changes — including opening its exchange to rival ad servers — would suffice.
 
A court ruling on remedies in the ad-tech case remains pending.


The damages theory relies on Clayton Act § 4 trebling. 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist retained by the firm, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly — the two separate rulings that established Google's liability.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.
 
Potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law.
 Per Keller Postman's own estimate: 
most qualifying businesses could recover up to 30% of their total Google Ads spend during the relevant period.


The arbitration route is dictated by contract, not strategic preference. 
Arbitration clauses in Google's advertiser contracts effectively prevent damages via class action litigation; instead, advertisers join together to make claims as part of a group arbitration, handled by a single law firm, which gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations.
 
The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most group proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.



Similar mass arbitrations have lasted 12 to 24 months between the filing of claims and resolution.
 That timeline implies a resolution window of roughly May 2027 to May 2028, assuming claims were filed in April/May 2026 as announced. The remedies exposure in the underlying antitrust cases remains open: 
on the search side, Google filed an appeal in January 2026 seeking to pause data-sharing mandates imposed by the September 2025 remedies ruling in the Mehta case, adding further legal uncertainty to the regulatory picture.
 A successful Google appeal that narrows or reverses the liability finding would not extinguish filed arbitration claims, but it would materially reduce the settlement leverage Keller Postman can bring to any global resolution.

A parallel structural development: 
Google updated its Ads Terms of Service for the first time since 2018, with changes effective July 1, 2026.
 The TOS revision, coming in direct proximity to the mass arbitration announcement, is notable for what it signals about Google's posture — the company is tightening indemnification language and AI-generated-content liability, but the arbitration provisions governing pre-July 2026 ad spend are not retroactively altered by the new terms.

### Broader Landscape: Settlement Fee Trajectories and the One-Third Ceiling

The simultaneous pendency of the Comcast one-third fee request (33.3% of $117.5 million) and the Bartz 25% request ($375 million of $1.5 billion) illustrates the fee-award trajectory this desk has flagged across the mega-settlement cycle. Courts applying FRCP 23(h) and Ninth and Third Circuit precedent are increasingly skeptical of percentage-of-fund awards unchecked by lodestar cross-check when the fund is large. In *In re Google Play Store Antitrust Litigation*, class counsel accepted a downward adjustment when the percentage implied an hourly rate well above market. The same dynamic applies here: $375 million in fees on a case that litigated for less than two years before settling raises a multiplier question that Martínez-Olguín flagged explicitly at the May 14 hearing. In *Hasson*, the Third Circuit's sliding-scale approach under *In re Rite Aid Corp. Securities Litigation* typically produces lower percentage awards as fund size increases — meaning the one-third ask is likely to face pressure even in a case where counsel produced a substantial settlement.


If the *Bartz* settlement is approved, it would rank among the largest copyright class action settlements on record and could become an early benchmark for how courts value mass claims against AI companies over the use of copyrighted books.
 That benchmarking effect extends to OpenAI, Meta, and Microsoft — 
there are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders with claims in the *Bartz v. Anthropic* settlement (N.D. Cal. No. 3:24-cv-05417) should monitor CourtListener Docket ID 69058235 for the final approval order, which could issue any week given that the May 14 hearing is concluded and supplemental briefing closed by May 21 — distributions will not flow until the order issues and any appeal resolves, and the second installment payment of $450 million from Anthropic is not triggered until September 25, 2026 regardless. Comcast class members with documented losses have until September 14, 2026 to file claims in *Hasson v. Comcast*, No. 2:23-cv-05039-JMY (E.D. Pa.); documented-loss claims (up to $10,000) are superior to the flat $50 alternative payment for any claimant who can produce bank statements, FTC identity-theft reports, or credit-freeze invoices traceable to the October 2023 breach — file documented rather than flat-payment claims now to avoid the claims deadline. Businesses that purchased Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016 should evaluate participation in the Keller Postman mass arbitration before state-law statute-of-limitations constraints tighten; the Clayton Act provides a four-year limitations period, but state-law tolling varies and Keller Postman has stated it is actively filing claims ahead of state filing deadlines — the firm operates on full contingency, so there is no upfront cost to evaluation. Across all three tracks, the practical instruction is the same: act on the earlier of the filing deadline or the date a global settlement is announced, because opt-in windows typically close faster once a resolution is in sight.
