### *Hasson v. Comcast* — Fee Petition Filed; Final Approval Set for Aug. 5


Comcast Cable Communications LLC and Comcast Corp. agreed to pay $117,500,000 to settle a class action lawsuit
 arising from the October 2023 Xfinity data breach. 
Court filings show the breach occurred between October 16 and October 19, 2023, exposing usernames, contact information, dates of birth, passwords, partial Social Security numbers, and security questions belonging to an estimated 35.8 million customers.


The settlement administrator is Kroll Settlement Administration LLC. 
The court has scheduled a final approval hearing for August 5, 2026, at 12:30 p.m. ET, at the James A. Byrne U.S. Courthouse, 601 Market Street, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19106.
 
The settlement requires Comcast to establish a fund of $117,500,000 to cover cash payments to class members, reimbursement for documented out-of-pocket losses and lost time, identity defense services, notice and administrative costs, and court-approved attorneys' fees and service awards.


The one-third fee request — roughly $39 million — sits at the high end of the typical percentage-of-fund range for data breach cases and will likely draw scrutiny under the *Gunter/Prudential* cross-check methodology in the Third Circuit. 
Class counsel characterized the request as merited by the work performed and the "extraordinary result achieved."
 The court can approve, reduce, or defer the fee at or before the Aug. 5 hearing.

The claims posture is consequential for recovery math: 
the claim deadline has been extended to September 14, 2026, and the final approval hearing rescheduled for August 5, 2026.
 
Class members may receive up to $10,000 for documented losses, or a pro rata payout of up to $50.
 The opt-out and objection deadline passed July 1. 
Plaintiff theories included failure to safeguard customer data, inadequate data security, unjust enrichment by using personal data, violation of the federal Cable Act, and certain state consumer statutes.
 
Comcast denied wrongdoing but agreed to provide free identity theft protection services to affected customers for three years in addition to the monetary fund.


The claims-rate picture is not yet public — the administrator has not disclosed filing volume — but the extension of the claims deadline from August 14 to September 14 suggests administration is still in active notice/filing mode. Lower ultimate participation rates benefit filers on the pro rata tier; higher rates compress the per-claimant recovery.

### *In re Social Media Adolescent Addiction MDL* — States' AG Trial Approaches; Penalty Math Contested


The central hub for social media addiction litigation is a federal MDL known as *In re Social Media Adolescent Addiction/Personal Injury Products Liability Litigation*, MDL No. 3047, pending in the Northern District of California before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers.
 
Case No. 4:22-md-03047-YGR; discovery matters are overseen by Judge Peter H. Kang.


Friday's development was specific to the state attorneys general track. 
The MDL judge denied Meta's final attempt to dismiss the nearly 30 state attorneys general claims before trial, finding too many factual disputes for dismissal — including questions about Meta's platform design, its knowledge of teen mental health risks, and whether it misrepresented those risks — and separately found that Meta failed to satisfy parental notice requirements under COPPA.
 
Trial is set to begin August 18, 2026, in Oakland and is expected to last more than a month.


At the pretrial hearing, 
the judge took issue with both sides' "extreme" penalty estimates: the states' $1.4 trillion proposal was called "unreasonable," while Meta's $4 million counter-estimate was dismissed as "not even a slap on the hand."
 The practical implication: the court is signaling it will set penalty figures somewhere in between but has not anchored to either party's framework. 
One day before the Los Angeles state-court bellwether verdict, a New Mexico court ordered Meta to pay $375 million in civil penalties in a lawsuit brought by the state's attorney general.
 That figure may function as a floor reference for what a major state enforcement penalty can look like, even if the legal theories differ.

**Prior bellwether posture (carry-forward):** 
The first federal bellwether case — the Breathitt County, Kentucky school district — settled before trial in May 2026 with Snap, TikTok, and YouTube first, then Meta on the eve of the scheduled June 2026 trial; Reuters reported combined value near $27 million.
 
Judge Gonzalez Rogers had selected six school districts — Maryland, Georgia, Kentucky, New Jersey, South Carolina, and Arizona — as the first wave of bellwether cases.
 
The next bellwether, the state attorneys-general track, is reported set for around August 2026.


On the California state-court parallel track (JCCP 5255, before Judge Carolyn B. Kuhl, L.A. Superior Court): 
a Los Angeles County Superior Court jury on March 25, 2026, returned a verdict finding Meta Platforms and Google liable for designing social media platforms that addicted a young user — the first time a jury held Big Tech accountable for the addictive design of Instagram and YouTube — in K.G.M. v. Meta Platforms and Google.
 
The verdict remains subject to post-trial motions and likely appeal.


The penalty sizing dispute going into the August 18 AG trial is the critical near-term variable for the entire MDL. A jury award or court-imposed penalty in the hundreds of millions against Meta on the AG track would substantially reshape global settlement mathematics for thousands of personal injury plaintiffs still in queue.

### *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* — Final Approval Still Pending; Docket Active Through July 13

The tracked-case ground truth shows docket entry 679 (filed July 13, 2026, *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) as Anthropic's opposition brief addressing objections under DE 676 — the last confirmed filing as of the July 19 data pull. No final approval order has issued.

To recap the post-hearing posture: 
at a 75-minute hearing on May 14 in San Francisco, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement in the *Bartz v. Anthropic* copyright infringement lawsuit, but the tone of the hearing led most observers to expect final approval relatively quickly.
 
Post-hearing, the court ordered Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, due no later than May 21, 2026.



In that supplemental filing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that two objectors seeking relief failed to show "excusable neglect," and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any record supporting relief.
 
Final approval remains pending.



The settlement is $1.5 billion — the largest publicly reported copyright recovery in U.S. history — covering approximately 482,000 works at an implied rate of $3,113 per book.
 
At the final fairness hearing, the opt-in rate was reported at 92.77% (447,576 of the eligible works claimed); attorney fees were reduced from $300 million to $187.5 million after judicial pushback.



If Judge Martínez-Olguín grants approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and pay $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.
 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved; payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest, with Anthropic paying into the fund over time so not all funds will be disbursed at once.


The July 13 opposition brief (DE 679) on the untimely opt-out question is the last discrete obstacle before the court can enter a final approval order. If the court adopts Anthropic's position on all five late requests, the class is closed and the order can issue. Any granted late opt-out narrows the fund's distribution pool marginally but does not materially affect the per-work figure.

**Downstream significance:** 
For many authors implicated in *Bartz*, litigation around AI training and book piracy is not ending — it is shifting to other cases, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others.
 
Several of those cases appear to be adopting the strategy that proved effective in *Bartz*: rather than challenging AI training as such, they focus on the underlying piracy and on infringing copies.

### Fee-Award Mechanics: One-Third in *Comcast*, $187.5M in *Bartz* — A Study in Contrast

The juxtaposition of this week's two fee events is instructive. In *Comcast*, class counsel seeks 33.3% of a $117.5 million fund — the standard one-third ask in consumer data breach cases in the Third Circuit. In *Bartz*, fee counsel opened at $375 million (25%), faced judicial resistance, and landed at $187.5 million — 12.5% of a $1.5 billion fund. The reduction reflects both the court's percentage-of-fund cross-check and the lodestar ceiling: in mega-fund cases, a straight percentage-of-fund calculation can produce a windfall that courts will not permit under *In re Rite Aid Corp. Securities Litigation* principles. The $187.5 million figure implies a lodestar multiplier that the court found defensible; the *Comcast* 33.3% figure will face a parallel analysis at the August 5 hearing. In both cases, the net distribution to claimants hinges directly on the fee outcome — particularly in *Comcast*, where the claims deadline remains open through September 14 and the per-claimant pro rata pool contracts with every dollar of fees approved.
