### *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Fee Controversy, Claims Architecture, and Installment Exposure

The fee dispute is the sharpest remaining litigation risk for this settlement's economics. 
Class counsel urged Judge Martínez-Olguín to grant final approval of the $1.5 billion settlement alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million — a reduction from the original 25% ask — while arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Anthropic separately contested the $75 million in fees that non-class counsel requested as part of the settlement, arguing there is "scant justification" for the request.
 The fee architecture is therefore bifurcated: lead class counsel at the reduced $187.5 million figure, and a disputed non-class counsel tranche that Anthropic is actively contesting. For claimants and rights-holders valuing their recovery, the net per-work figure turns on how much the court awards in total fees and costs before the fund is divided.

On the claims side, the participation figures are unusually strong. 
Class counsel filed papers showing 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate — with the final tally vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval, indicating a large volume of claims arrived at the end of the filing period.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed at the May 14 hearing that the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100.
 
The high participation rate means earlier projections of a larger payout per work — based on the 54% mid-period figure — will not materialize, and the actual payout tracks the original $3,000 per-work estimate.


The installment structure creates additional timing exposure. 
Anthropic is funding the $1.5 billion settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The September 2026 and September 2027 tranches are still outstanding. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 Any appellate challenge to a final approval order — even a weak one — extends the distribution timeline into 2027 at minimum given the installment schedule.

One structural note worth carrying forward: 
the scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood — Bartz is not, strictly speaking, a settlement about AI training.
 
Judge Alsup had held that Anthropic's use of lawfully acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and thus fair use, but simultaneously ruled that Anthropic's creation and retention of a "central library" comprised in part of pirated works was not transformative and constituted infringement.
 The settlement resolves the piracy liability track, not the training-as-fair-use question, which is already resolved in Anthropic's favor at the district level.

### *In re Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation* — Distribution in Progress; Tier-2 and Tier-3 Waves Ahead

MDL 2406, Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP, N.D. Ala., is now in active payment distribution — the most advanced large-fund antitrust settlement currently in the pipeline. 
The April 24, 2026 distribution order was the last procedural step; the case is captioned *In re: Blue Cross Blue Shield Antitrust Litigation*, MDL 2406, Master File No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama, Southern Division, with a $2.67 billion gross settlement representing approximately $1.9 billion Net Settlement Fund after attorneys' fees and administrative costs.
 
Class Counsel filed the motion for distribution on April 23, 2026, and Judge Manasco approved the distribution order on April 24, 2026, with a target start date of May 11, 2026.


The Tier-1 individual wave is now materially complete. 
The court order directed the Settlement Administrator to begin issuing payments during the week of May 11, 2026, with some claimants reporting virtual prepaid card payment emails, while others remain in queue depending on payment method, claim status, and processing timing.
 
Claimants with a calculated distribution of $200 or more receive 90% in the initial round with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution; claimants receive funds by check, PayPal, Venmo, or electronic debit card.
 Tier-2 (employee contribution) and Tier-3 (employer group) distributions are the remaining exposure. 
Tier-2 notification emails began in the first batch (covering approximately 15% of validated Tier-2 claims) around June 1, with payments arriving four to eight weeks after notification; Tier-3 first payments had not yet started as of the most recent reporting.


For practitioners tracking appellate tail risk: 
the Eleventh Circuit affirmed the settlement on October 25, 2024, clearing the path for claim allocation.
 No further appellate exposure is active on the subscriber track. The residual risk is administrative — uncashed, returned, or failed payments that will be reconciled before any second distribution is declared.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — The Largest Mass Arbitration Campaign by Dollar Value

The most structurally novel development tracked on this desk continues to build. 
In April 2026, Keller Postman's Ashley Keller began filing mass arbitration claims against Google/Alphabet on behalf of advertisers, seeking damages tied to two federal court rulings in 2024 that found Google illegally monopolized online search and advertising technology — with potential claims estimated at $218 billion or more, described as the largest mass arbitration campaign ever attempted by dollar value.



Following two federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman announced on May 11, 2026, that it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade; experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs, creating the potential for substantial recovery under federal antitrust law.


The damages theory is grounded in the two existing liability findings — the D.C. search monopoly ruling and the E.D. Va. ad tech ruling — rather than requiring new liability proof. 
The $218 billion figure is based on calculations from an economist Keller Postman has retained, covering damages from both the search monopoly and the ad tech monopoly.
 
Potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law (Clayton Act § 4).


The procedural mechanics are consequential. 
Advertiser contracts with Google require mandatory arbitration over legal disputes, which is why companies that displayed ads — including major publishers who have sued for damages since the 2024 rulings — are funneled into this arbitration track rather than class litigation.
 
The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs at scale, as most group arbitration proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.
 
Keller represents multiple major businesses with hundreds of millions of dollars in annual Google media spend; mass anonymous arbitration allows large brands to pursue settlements without creating a public record that could complicate their ongoing advertiser relationship with Google.


The systemic risk for Google is fee-driven, not merit-driven in the first instance. 
Only 1% of consumer mass arbitration cases closed in 2024 with a formal award; yet 59% settled — suggesting companies settle to avoid escalating filing fees, not because they lose on the merits.
 The Google campaign targets business claimants at a far larger per-claim spend level than any prior consumer campaign, which materially changes the economics of any fee-driven settlement pressure.

For governance and process context: 
the Ninth Circuit has been actively refining bellwether and consolidation standards — striking down Live Nation's New Era ADR clause in *Heckman* (2024) and a provision that bound non-participants in *Pandolfi v. AviaGames* (Aug. 2025), while upholding JAMS consolidation authority in *Jones v. Starz*.
 How AAA and JAMS handle batching of thousands of B2B Google advertiser claims — including fee schedules, consolidation authority, and bellwether selection — will define the settlement pressure timeline.

### Downstream AI Copyright — Adjacent Litigation That Affects the Bartz Precedent's Value

*Bartz* has not foreclosed the AI copyright landscape. 
For many authors implicated in *Bartz*, the litigation around AI training and book piracy is not ending but shifting to other cases — there are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft and active putative class actions against Meta.
 Practitioners valuing opt-out rights or advising on whether to participate in *Bartz* versus holding claims for future actions should note that the district court's fair use ruling on training is binding only as to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class — meaning the training-is-fair-use question remains open for any plaintiff who opted out of *Bartz* and brings a fresh training-use claim in a different case.
