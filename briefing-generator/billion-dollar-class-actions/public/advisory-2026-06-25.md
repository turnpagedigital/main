### I. Roundup — Bayer/Monsanto $7.25 Billion Settlement: Remand Holds, Eighth Circuit Appeal Filed, Final Hearing July 9


A Missouri judge has given preliminary approval to Bayer's proposed $7.25 billion Roundup settlement, a deal the company hopes will resolve thousands of cancer claims tied to the weedkiller.
 That preliminary approval was entered on 
March 4, 2026, setting the stage for a final approval hearing on July 9, 2026.


The settlement's path to final approval has been obstructed on two tracks. On the removal track: 
U.S. District Judge Henry Autrey, of the Eastern District of Missouri, ruled Wednesday that a group of objecting plaintiffs was not authorized to move the case to federal court, finding that the right to remove a case belongs only to the defendant.
 
The order returns the dispute to the 22nd Judicial Circuit Court in St. Louis, where a judge is set to hear arguments for final approval on July 9. The Missouri court has already preliminarily approved the agreement, and the remand moots any potential transfer of the case into the consolidated Roundup multidistrict litigation in federal court.
 
A federal judge in St. Louis denied a bid by settlement critics to have the case sent to U.S. District Judge Vince Chhabria in San Francisco, who is overseeing all Roundup cases filed in federal court and has blasted the settlement's terms as "mind boggling."
 Objectors immediately escalated: 
the objecting plaintiffs filed a notice of appeal asking the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to review Judge Autrey's decision.


On the constitutional track, 
Bayer's proposed $7.25 billion class action settlement has been characterized as a "sweetheart deal" that violates the U.S. Constitution by running "roughshod over basic due process rights," according to a court filing; those objections were filed May 21 in Missouri's Circuit Court of the City of St. Louis.
 The structural objections center on the futures subclass: 
the settlement creates a "futures" subclass that automatically covers anyone in the United States exposed to Roundup or other glyphosate-based weed killers before February 17, 2026, including children and people who have not yet been born; if any of these people are later diagnosed with NHL, they would be limited by the settlement's compensation caps unless they opted out by June 4, 2026.


Settlement economics: 
people currently diagnosed with NHL could receive between $6,000 and $165,000 or more, depending on exposure history, age at diagnosis, and type of cancer. The strongest cases — occupational exposure, diagnosis before age 60, aggressive NHL — carry an average payout of $165,000. Many other cases may receive as little as $20,000.
 
The class-action settlement, which Bayer proposed in February, is a key part of the company's plans to cap its obligations by resolving current and future damage claims, with funding running for up to 21 years.


Layered over the settlement is the Supreme Court's pending ruling in *Monsanto v. Durnell*. 
At the heart of the case is whether federal law — specifically the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA) — preempts state court juries from holding Monsanto liable for failing to warn about cancer risk when the EPA has not required such a warning.
 
If the Supreme Court sides with Monsanto, it could effectively shut down future failure-to-warn lawsuits — the legal theory that produced billion-dollar verdicts against Bayer in recent years. Supporters of the settlement have used *Durnell* as leverage, telling plaintiffs that opting out is too risky because of a possible negative ruling.
 
A final decision from the Supreme Court is expected within the next few weeks.


The advisory lens here is straightforward: the July 9 hearing is the dispositive near-term event, but it is not the final word. Even if the 22nd Judicial Circuit grants final approval, 
after final approval and resolution of any appeals, class members will have 180 days to register for benefits.
 The Eighth Circuit appeal of the remand adds appellate drag. Claims-rate math favors early filers in the event the settlement survives: with 
roughly 61,000 active Roundup lawsuits remaining pending
 against a $7.25 billion fund, the per-claim average at full participation is approximately $118,000 — but the tier structure compresses that range dramatically, and a low opt-out rate among occupational-exposure claimants would concentrate recoveries at the top tiers.

### II. Claritev Healthcare Antitrust MDL — Judge Kennelly Strikes Unclean-Hands Defense (N.D. Ill., June 24)


Healthcare data firm Claritev and a group of major insurers cannot assert an unclean hands defense in multidistrict litigation accusing payors of scheming to fix reimbursement rates through the data firm's pricing tools, an Illinois federal judge ruled Wednesday.
 The ruling was issued June 24, 2026 by 
Judge Matthew Kennelly of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois Eastern Division.


The substantive allegations: 
plaintiffs allege that Claritev — formerly MultiPlan — facilitated a horizontal price-fixing agreement among insurers that artificially suppressed out-of-network payment rates and cost providers billions of dollars; the litigation is consolidated in the Northern District of Illinois and alleges a coordinated price-fixing conspiracy to systematically underpay out-of-network physician practices and healthcare facilities.
 Named insurer defendants include 
UnitedHealth Group, Elevance Health, Aetna, and Cigna,
 among others.

The unclean-hands ruling matters for settlement posture: stripping a key affirmative defense before trial reduces the defendants' ability to deflect damages by pointing to plaintiffs' own conduct in the out-of-network billing ecosystem — a theory defendants had apparently advanced to complicate both liability and remedies under Sherman Act §§ 1–2 and Clayton Act § 4. With that defense gone, provider plaintiffs carry a cleaner damages case into what appears to be active fact discovery. 
At the February 27, 2026 case management conference, the court cleared the way for depositions of MultiPlan/Claritev representatives and required the company to produce key internal records.


A separate regulatory development adds context but does not directly bear on the civil MDL: 
on June 17, 2026, the DOJ Antitrust Division informed Claritev that the antitrust grand jury proceeding is being brought to a close and that the company is not under any criminal investigation.
 However, 
on May 19, 2026, the company received a civil investigative demand from the Antitrust Division and is cooperating fully with the DOJ in responding to the information requested.
 Criminal closure removes one pressure point but does not moot the civil MDL or the DOJ's civil review.

### III. Hut 8 Corp. — $2.35 Million USBTC Merger Securities Settlement (S.D.N.Y., Filed June 22)


Hut 8 Corp. has agreed to pay $2.35 million to settle a securities class action alleging it misled investors about operational risks at a Texas Bitcoin mining site linked to its 2023 merger with US Bitcoin Corp. (USBTC), according to a preliminary settlement approval motion filed June 22 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York.
 
The settlement details were laid out in a memorandum of law before Judge Victor Marrero; the original complaint was filed on February 7, 2024.


This matter is below the $1B threshold tracked here but warrants coverage for two reasons: recovery-rate benchmarking and the traceability-defense dynamic that compressed settlement leverage post-dismissal. On rate: 
plaintiff counsel estimated that the $2.35 million settlement represents about 19.6% of the maximum recoverable damages of roughly $12.08 million, a recovery described as above recent averages for Securities Act-only settlements.
 
Plaintiff counsel said that it exceeds the 12.9% median and 14.6% average recovery for comparable cases.


On the traceability dynamic: 
in September 2025, Judge Marrero dismissed investors' Exchange Act claims in their entirety and rejected their Securities Act claims arising from alleged misstatements about USBTC's financial condition before the merger; he allowed Securities Act claims to proceed only on alleged omissions regarding risks at King Mountain — specifically, whether Hut 8's merger materials adequately disclosed infrastructure vulnerabilities at a facility material to USBTC's mining operations.
 
Facing that narrower case, Hut 8 indicated it would seek judgment on the pleadings by challenging traceability, arguing that registered and unregistered shares had been commingled after the merger, making it difficult for aftermarket purchasers to trace their holdings back to the registration statement.
 That traceability threat — not unusual in all-stock merger securities cases — appears to have been the decisive factor driving the settlement below what the raw percentage might suggest.


The proposed all-cash settlement covers investors who acquired Hut 8 securities in the United States between February 13, 2023, and January 18, 2024; the agreement follows a mediator's proposal accepted by both parties on May 13, 2026.
 
The parties took part in a full-day virtual mediation session on May 7 before JAMS mediator Jed Melnick.
 
Lead Plaintiff Abhishek Maheshwari has asked the court to grant preliminary approval, certify the settlement class, and schedule a final fairness hearing.
