### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Final Approval Entered July 20; Distribution Clock Running


On July 20, 2026, Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California (N.D. Cal.) granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* (No. 3:24-cv-05417) and entered final judgment in the case.
 The order ends the merits phase; the distribution clock now starts.

The fee arithmetic is significant. 
The court denied class counsel's requested 12.5% of the $1.5 billion fund — which would have yielded $187.5 million — and instead granted a lodestar multiplier that produced a total fee award of $101,561,111, plus approximately $3 million in expense reimbursements and an $18.2 million cost reserve for claims administration.
 
The court determined "a multiplier of 3.75, for a total fee award of $101,561,111 is reasonable,"
 departing sharply from the 6.92 multiplier counsel had sought on a stated lodestar of $27,082,963. That reduction keeps an additional ~$86 million in the non-reversionary fund for authors.

Service award treatment was equally disciplined. 
The court trimmed the requested service awards for the three class representatives — Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson — from $50,000 to $15,000 each, while recognizing the substantial work they performed on behalf of fellow authors.


On class participation: 
class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate, with 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works, or 0.03% of the total number of works.
 
In aggregate, the settlement has a reported claims rate of 92.77%,
 meaning the per-claim denominator is nearly fully subscribed — a structure that compresses per-work recovery but reduces the windfall-to-non-filers problem that plagues lower-participation settlements. 
*Bartz v. Anthropic* is described by Bloomberg as "the largest copyright class action settlement in US history," paying "about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works."


On the objector front: 
Judge Martínez-Olguín overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds, finding the proposed settlement was "negotiated at arm's length," the relief adequate, and that "the proposed Settlement treats Class Members equitably relative to each other."
 No formal objector appeal has been confirmed as of this writing. 
There is a 30-day window from July 20
 during which appellate exposure exists — the standard period for a notice of appeal. If that window closes without a filed appeal, distribution can proceed on the administrator's timeline.

On injunctive terms: 
the agreement requires Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion to the authors and publishers of nearly half a million books it downloaded from notorious pirate sites, as well as destroy all the original files of works torrented and downloaded from those sites and any copies that originate from the torrented copies.
 
If money remains after all valid claims are paid, the parties anticipate redistributing it to class members; if a final balance remains that is economically infeasible to distribute, it will go — subject to court approval — to charitable purposes.


The contextual framing: 
prior to class certification, Judge William Alsup held that AI companies can use legally purchased books for training, not illegal ones
 — a ruling that established the liability perimeter Anthropic ultimately settled within. The settlement does not resolve claims involving legally purchased copies, keeping open parallel theories in other AI copyright proceedings.

**Practice note for rights-holders:** The 30-day appeal window expires approximately August 19. Authors and publishers who filed claims should confirm their claim status with the Anthropic Copyright Settlement Administrator before that date. Distribution timing has not been formally announced; the Authors Guild has indicated it expects payouts to begin promptly once the appellate window closes without incident.

### II. J&J Talc MDL (MDL 2738, D.N.J.) — Expert Withdrawal Opens Dismissal Front; Bellwether Docket at Risk

The J&J talc litigation entered a structurally dangerous phase for plaintiffs this month. 
Johnson & Johnson asked a federal judge to dismiss thousands of lawsuits in the talc MDL after attorneys for the victims withdrew two experts who were expected to testify that talcum powder use contributed to ovarian cancer — with J&J arguing the experts acknowledged during recent court hearings that there is no reliable scientific method to determine whether talc was a substantial contributing factor in a specific woman's ovarian cancer.
 
The plaintiffs formally withdrew the experts on June 8; in its filing, J&J is seeking dismissal not only of the six bellwether cases but of all remaining ovarian cancer claims in the MDL.


The stakes on the expert ruling are enormous. 
The federal talc MDL currently includes approximately 67,000 claims alleging that talcum powder products contributed to the development of ovarian cancer.
 Without general causation testimony — testimony that talc can cause ovarian cancer at all — individual specific causation opinions are moot, and the entire MDL ovarian-cancer docket becomes legally exposed. J&J's motion is styled as a Daubert-plus-dispositive motion rather than a summary judgment motion alone; if granted, it would function as a case-dispositive ruling at scale. 
J&J argues that without admissible causation testimony, the six scheduled bellwether cases, and potentially all pending talc MDL claims, cannot proceed.


The procedural backstory matters here. 
In January 2026, a federal judge ruled that plaintiffs' experts should be allowed to testify about the association between talcum powder use and ovarian cancer; in a 685-page report, special master Freda Wolfson noted that the experts "applied reliable methodologies" to find a link.
 The same experts were then challenged in May evidentiary hearings and withdrew in June — meaning the January clearance was effectively vacated by the plaintiffs' own strategic decision to pull the witnesses rather than defend them in full Daubert proceedings before the district court.


In the J&J talc MDL, new scheduling deadlines have been established by U.S. District Judge Michael A. Shipp of the District of New Jersey.
 Judge Shipp has not yet ruled on J&J's dismissal motion; the response deadline for plaintiffs has not been publicly confirmed in available sources. Plaintiffs' lead counsel have not announced substitute expert witnesses as of this writing. The Law360 headline ("J&J Talc Plaintiffs Must Back Up Cancer Link Or Risk MDL") signals Judge Shipp has set a hard deadline for plaintiffs to produce new general-causation evidence or face further adverse action — the precise contours of that order are not yet available in public docket records and will be tracked in the next cycle.

**Practice note for MDL claimants:** The expert-withdrawal development is material to case valuation. Any claimant or counsel relying on the withdrawn experts' opinions for specific causation should assess whether substitute testimony can satisfy the Daubert standard on general causation before J&J's dismissal motion is fully briefed. J&J's post-bankruptcy-failure posture — all three Texas Two-Step attempts rejected — means it is now litigating aggressively on the merits rather than seeking a global resolution, which changes the settlement calculus for individual claimants.

### III. Securities Class Actions — On Track for $4.4 Billion Aggregate in 2026, a Six-Year High


The value of securities class action settlements is on track to reach a six-year high of $4.4 billion, fueled in part by a handful of "mega settlements" and other high-dollar deals, according to a Cornerstone Research report released July 22, 2026.
 The timing matters: as *Bartz v. Anthropic* resolves and distribution begins, settlement pressure in the PSLRA docket is building simultaneously across multiple sectors.


Federal securities class action filings rose to 118 cases in the first half of 2026, with AI-related litigation reaching a five-year high and average settlement values climbing to $54 million — the highest in a decade.
 
The average settlement value was $54 million in H1 2026, a 32% increase relative to the 2025 inflation-adjusted average settlement value of $41 million.
 The $54 million average is being driven upward by a small number of outsized resolutions; the median of $18 million has held flat year-over-year, confirming the skew is concentrated in the tail.


During the first half of 2026, 105 federal securities class actions were resolved, of which 65 were dismissed and 40 were settled.
 A 38% settlement rate on resolved cases is broadly consistent with historical averages, suggesting the increased dollar figures reflect deal size, not deal frequency.

The broader class action market confirms the elevated environment. 
As of end of H1 2026, the aggregate settlement total across all areas of class action litigation and government enforcement lawsuits is $53.795 billion
 — more than double the $21.77 billion recorded at the same point in 2025. 
The top 5 settlements at mid-year 2026 include: $34.875 billion in antitrust class actions; $8.609 billion in products liability/mass tort; $4.25 billion in government enforcement actions; $1.979 billion in securities fraud; and $1.535 billion in consumer fraud.

### IV. Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — Claims Filed, Forum Mechanics in Play


Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges, announced May 11, 2026.
 This campaign is the most consequential active mass arbitration proceeding by claimed value.


Ashley Keller estimates potential claims for online search and display ads could reach $218 billion or more, based on calculations from an economist his firm retained.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade.
 The $218 billion figure represents a partial treble-damages estimate under Clayton Act § 4; the actual settlement value — if and when J&J-style pressure mounts — would be negotiated against that ceiling.

The structural mechanic: 
arbitration clauses in Google's advertiser contracts effectively prevent damages via class action litigation; instead, advertisers can join together to make claims as part of a group arbitration, which is usually handled by a single law firm and gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations.
 
Google's advertiser contracts specify a particular arbitration forum — likely AAA or JAMS — both of which have mass arbitration rules triggered at 25 or more simultaneous coordinated claims.
 Once mass-arbitration supplementary rules engage, per-claim filing fees paid by the respondent (Google) scale linearly, creating the fee-pressure dynamic that has historically accelerated settlement in prior Keller Postman campaigns against DoorDash, Postmates, and Intuit.


Federal judges in both the District of Columbia — Judge Amit P. Mehta — and the Eastern District of Virginia — Judge Leonie M. Brinkema — ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 Those liability findings are the predicate for the arbitration claims. 
Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling is expected in 2026; Keller Postman is actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines.
 The remedies ruling in the E.D. Va. ad-tech case — when it arrives — could substantially narrow or expand the damages theory underpinning the arbitration, making it the critical external trigger to watch.

**Practice note for advertisers:** Any business that purchased Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016 is a potential claimant. Claims are handled on a contingency basis. The window to file before state statute-of-limitations deadlines is actively narrowing; Keller Postman's own filings reference state-specific cutoffs as a driver of current intake urgency. Businesses evaluating participation should assess: (a) volume of Google ad spend during the monopoly period; (b) applicable state limitations period; and (c) whether their advertiser agreement specifies AAA or JAMS and what mass-arbitration supplementary rules apply.
