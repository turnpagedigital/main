### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Pending; Distribution Mechanics Now Operative


The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement.
 
After a 75-minute hearing in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for the moment, to approve the settlement from the bench — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expected her to grant final approval relatively quickly.



Per the order entered following the hearing, Anthropic had until May 21, 2026 to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and plaintiffs were required to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (docket entry 646-1).
 The court stated it would consider no further submissions from objectors. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín indicated that short orders might follow as soon as the day of the hearing — though she ultimately took the matter under full submission.


The claims data at the time of the hearing had materially shifted from the motion-for-final-approval projections. 
Class counsel filed papers advising the court that 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% claim rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval of Settlement filed March 19, 2026, eleven days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson stated at the hearing that the payout per work in the class would be approximately $3,100.
 
The high claim rate means that earlier forecasts of a larger per-work payout based on the March 19 report will not materialize; the actual payout is likely to land near the original $3,000-per-work figure.


On fee structure: 
plaintiff counsel sought 25% of the $1.5 billion fund — $375 million — with additional deductions for JND Legal Administration costs and service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives).
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín questioned class counsel, including Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman, about the service awards, cost reserve, attorneys' fees, and the fee multiplier.


On distribution sequencing: 
the settlement timeline calls for calculation of per-work and per-claimant distributions on or around June 11, 2026, with the earliest payments to class members beginning June 2026 if the settlement is not appealed, and summer 2026 as the earliest realistic window if objectors are present but no appeals are filed.
 
A second payment tranche of $450 million (Schedule Payment 1) is due September 25, 2026 — twelve months after the preliminary approval order — with a second wave of checks targeted for October 9, 2026, and a further $450 million payment (Schedule Payment 2) due September 25, 2027.


The structural lesson from Bartz's 91.3% participation rate is worth preserving analytically: the inverse relationship between claim rate and per-claim yield collapsed here. 
A claim rate nearly nine times the typical class action claim rate of approximately 10%
 is partly a function of the Authors Guild's aggressive outreach, partly a function of a well-identified class (ISBN/ASIN registration + LibGen provenance), and partly a function of a straightforward equal-per-work formula that eliminated the pro-rata uncertainty that depresses participation in damages-fund settlements. Rights-holders in subsequent AI-copyright settlements should treat 91% as the floor benchmark for a similarly-defined, well-noticed class — which recalibrates expectations about per-work yield across cases in the OpenAI and Meta tracks.


For many authors implicated in *Bartz*, the litigation around AI training and book piracy is not ending — it is shifting to other cases. There are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta.
 The piracy-first litigation strategy validated here — targeting shadow-library ingestion rather than the fair-use training debate — is already the template for subsequent filings.

### Keller Postman / Google Advertiser Mass Arbitration — July 1 TOS Deadline Creates Filing Urgency


In April 2026, Keller Postman's Ashley Keller began filing mass arbitration claims against Google/Alphabet on behalf of advertisers, seeking damages tied to two federal court rulings in 2024 that found Google illegally monopolized online search and advertising technology. Potential claims are estimated at $218 billion or more — the largest mass arbitration campaign ever attempted by dollar value.



Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges.
 
Keller Postman formally announced the campaign on May 11, 2026, targeting companies of all sizes — from local businesses to large enterprises — that spent money on Google search or display advertising on or after August 2016.


The underlying liability theories rest on two distinct judicial findings. 
Federal judges in both the District of Columbia (Judge Amit P. Mehta) and the Eastern District of Virginia (Judge Leonie M. Brinkema) ruled in favor of the U.S. Department of Justice, finding Google liable for anticompetitive conduct in its advertising business.
 
According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade; experts in the federal antitrust cases estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs.


On the damages arithmetic: 
potential recovery is estimated at up to 30% of total Google ad spend, with treble damages available under federal antitrust law.
 
The $218 billion figure is an estimate, not a filed demand amount; in mass arbitration, the leverage comes from the volume of individual filings and the per-claim fee obligations they trigger — not from any single damages number.


**The July 1 gate.** 
Google's current terms of service expire June 30, 2026; Google sent notification emails to account holders on June 1, 2026.
 
The updated terms take effect July 1, 2026, allowing for batch arbitration of identical or nearly identical claims — replacing the previous framework that handled each claim individually.
 
The 30-day window to opt out of the new arbitration clause begins July 1; if advertisers opt out, they retain the right to bring disputes in court. If they miss the window, they are bound by the arbitration clause.


The structural implication: Google's pivot from individual-claim arbitration to batch arbitration under the new TOS is a direct defensive response to the Keller Postman campaign. 
The Google mass arbitration may be the first to seek to represent corporate plaintiffs, as most group legal proceedings to date have sought to arbitrate consumer or labor-related claims.
 
In addition to representing private claimants in arbitration, Keller Postman represents seven state attorneys general in a parallel case.
 Google's ability to batch and sequence claims under the new TOS — rather than face fee exposure on thousands of individual proceedings simultaneously — meaningfully changes the cost-pressure calculus that drove earlier defendants (DoorDash, Postmates, Intuit) to settle.

### BCBS Subscriber Settlement, MDL No. 2:13-cv-20000-RDP (N.D. Ala.) — First Distribution Wave Complete; Tier 2 Underway


The $2.67 billion Blue Cross Blue Shield antitrust settlement began paying out on May 11, 2026.
 
Approximately $1.9 billion will be distributed to roughly six million claimants, with individual payouts varying based on coverage history and premiums paid.
 
On April 23, 2026, subscriber plaintiffs filed a motion in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama asking the court to approve a distribution plan for nearly 7.8 million claimants.



Claimants with a calculated distribution of $200 or more receive 90% in the initial round, with 10% held in reserve for a second distribution; claimants receive funds by check, PayPal, Venmo, or electronic debit card.



The administrator confirmed a second batch of prepaid debit cards was mailed during the week of May 19; paper checks for non-debit elections began shipping May 25.
 
Tier 2 (employee) notification emails started June 1 for the first batch, covering approximately 15% of validated Tier 2 claims.
 
Estimated Tier 1 wave completion is targeted for the end of July 2026.


The denominator math is worth holding: 
payment amounts depend on factors including length of coverage, premiums paid, and plan type, with estimates averaging around $300 but ranging significantly.
 The reserve structure — 10% held back per claimant — is standard mechanics for a fund this size, preserving administrator discretion for deficiency claims and disputes without halting primary distribution.

A parallel provider-side settlement is running on a separate docket. 
The BCBS Provider Settlement site handles providers who provide healthcare services — explicitly separate from the subscriber settlement at BCBSSettlement.com.
 
The settlement claims administrator began issuing claim notices for the provider track on February 16, 2026; claimants may receive email or mail notification and can log in to their portal to view or download their notice.

### In re Payment Card Interchange Fee, MDL No. 1720 (E.D.N.Y.) — Second Initial Distribution Approved June 15; Wave Targeted September 2026


On May 26, 2026, class counsel filed a motion requesting court approval of a second initial distribution of settlement funds from the $5.54 billion Visa/Mastercard interchange settlement; on June 15, 2026, the court approved the motion, finding that the proposed distribution is in the best interests of eligible claimants while protecting those whose claims are not yet ready for distribution. The second initial distribution is anticipated to occur in September 2026.


The parallel Rule 23(b)(2) injunctive track remains a live appellate exposure point. 
U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan of the Eastern District of New York granted preliminary approval of the revised settlement, finding it provides more extensive relief than the prior settlement the court rejected in June 2024 and that it covers approximately 12 million merchants.
 The National Association of Convenience Stores (NACS) had signaled it would appeal to the Second Circuit if Cogan grants final approval of the injunctive terms.

On Visa's escrow exposure: 
between October 1, 2023, and March 31, 2026, Visa made payments of approximately $4.2 billion from its U.S. litigation escrow account for settlements of U.S. covered litigation, reducing estimated interchange fees at issue in unresolved claims by more than 50%; the current U.S. litigation escrow account balance is approximately $650 million.
 That depleted escrow balance is structurally relevant: it caps Visa's remaining buffer for opt-out litigation and any NACS-led appellate outcome that unwinds the injunctive settlement. Merchants who filed timely claims in the Rule 23(b)(3) damages track and are waiting for the September wave have no appellate exposure from the NACS action — their claims are in the damages sub-fund, not the injunctive relief track.
