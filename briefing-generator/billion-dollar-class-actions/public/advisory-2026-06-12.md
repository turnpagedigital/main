### I. *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee* — MDL 1720 (E.D.N.Y.) · Revised $38B Settlement Clears Preliminary Approval

The most consequential class-action development this week is the June 9, 2026 preliminary approval ruling in the nation's longest-running commercial antitrust class action. U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan in Brooklyn granted preliminary approval on Tuesday to Visa's and Mastercard's revised $38 billion settlement, ruling nearly two years after a different judge rejected a proposed $30 billion settlement as too small. The case caption is *In re Payment Card Interchange Fee and Merchant Discount Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 1720, Docket No. 05-md-01720 (E.D.N.Y.), and Judge Cogan said the settlement covering more than 12 million merchants was "fair, reasonable, and adequate," and that he was likely to eventually grant final approval.

The settlement announced last November was intended to end litigation that began in 2005, when merchants accused Visa, Mastercard, and banks of conspiring to violate U.S. antitrust laws, including through the collection of "swipe fees." The two-decade history is pivotal context: in rejecting the earlier accord in June 2024, U.S. District Judge Margo Brodie said fees would have still been above where they were absent any antitrust violations, and merchants would remain stuck with the "Honor All Cards" rule. The revised deal significantly enlarged the value. Experts hired by plaintiffs, Nobel Prize-winning economist Joseph Stiglitz and University of Washington professor Keith Leffler, said the changes could save merchants $38 billion by 2031 and provide $224 billion of benefits overall, including to consumers.

Critically, preliminary approval is a procedural checkpoint, not a final ruling — the court will now notify class members, solicit objections, and eventually hold a fairness hearing before deciding whether to grant final approval. Opposition remains organized: trade groups including the National Retail Federation, the Merchants Payments Coalition, and the National Association of Convenience Stores objected to the revised settlement as well. The NRF, the world's largest retail trade group, also plans further challenges. The structural critique is unchanged from the prior rejection cycle — the NRF and allied groups contend the settlement treats symptoms rather than the underlying disease: a duopoly that controls the vast majority of card-based payment processing in the U.S. In 2025, the credit card networks charged merchants almost $119 billion in swipe fees, which frames the objectors' argument that fee-reform, not just damages, is the appropriate remedy.

For counsel representing opt-out merchants or advising merchant class members: the preliminary approval order initiates the formal notice period. The fairness hearing calendar has not yet been publicly confirmed in the sources reviewed this run, but practitioner practice in large MDL settlements in the E.D.N.Y. typically schedules fairness hearings 90–120 days after preliminary approval, placing a probable final-approval hearing in the September–October 2026 window. Opt-out and objection deadlines will be embedded in the notice order and should be calendared immediately upon receipt.

### II. Corteva Pesticide MDL — $85M Preliminary Approval Motion Filed, M.D.N.C.

A group of farmers has asked a North Carolina federal judge to preliminarily approve an $85 million settlement with Corteva Inc. to resolve antitrust claims that the company used loyalty rebate programs to artificially extend their patent monopolies over certain pesticides. The filing was reported June 11, 2026 by Law360. The case is lodged in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina. The multidistrict litigation accuses Corteva and Syngenta of using loyalty rebate programs to artificially extend their patent monopolies over certain pesticides by offering payments to distributors that agree to limit their sales of cheaper generic products.

Corteva participated in mediation conferences on March 10, 2026, and May 14, 2026; the mediator reported to the court on May 29, 2026 that a settlement had been reached only with Corteva. The deal does not impact the farmers' claims against Syngenta AG over a similar rebate program. The parallel FTC enforcement action — filed September 29, 2022, under the Sherman Act — remains active. Farmers have been filing claims following the Federal Trade Commission and attorneys general from California, Colorado, Illinois, Iowa, Indiana, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon, Texas, and Wisconsin's separate lawsuit alleging the companies violated the Sherman Act and its antimonopoly provisions. The Trump administration and the states had previously asked the court to set a trial date for some time in October 2026.

For agricultural-sector claimants and counsel: the Corteva settlement carves only the private class claims; the FTC enforcement track continues. Syngenta remains a defendant with no reported settlement, and the M.D.N.C. trial calendar pressure is undiminished for that defendant. Farmers who qualify as Corteva customers from 2016 forward should assess whether to participate in the class or opt out in light of individual damages calculations relative to the average per-claimant recovery on an $85 million fund.

### III. Bayer/Roundup — $7.25B Class Settlement · Fairness Hearing July 9

The Roundup litigation continues its dual-track structure: a proposed global class settlement running in parallel with ongoing individual trial verdicts. On February 17, 2026, Bayer announced a proposed $7.25 billion Roundup settlement to resolve thousands of lawsuits and establish a compensation program for current and future victims diagnosed with non-Hodgkin's lymphoma after glyphosate exposure. The deal is structured to be paid out over a 21-year period, representing a massive effort by the German conglomerate to finally exit a legal quagmire that has plagued the company since its 2018 acquisition of Monsanto. The proposal was filed in the Circuit Court of the City of St. Louis, Missouri, and seeks to manage both current claims and potential future lawsuits from individuals who develop non-Hodgkin's lymphoma after using the glyphosate-based herbicide.

A preliminary approval decision has already been issued, with that ruling staying all Roundup lawsuits in Missouri pending a final settlement order and setting a fairness hearing on the settlement proposal for July 9. The stakes of that hearing are enormous: Bayer has been fending off litigation brought by close to 200,000 people suffering from non-Hodgkin's lymphoma they blame on exposure to Roundup and other Monsanto glyphosate-based herbicide brands since buying Monsanto in 2018. So far, the litigation has cost the company more than $11 billion in settlements and jury verdicts. Bayer has further bolstered its litigation reserve: the company increased its total litigation provisions to 11.8 billion euros ($13.9 billion) and secured an $8 billion bank loan facility to manage the immediate cash flow impact.

Individual verdicts continue to shadow settlement negotiations. Under the proposed settlement structure, some class members could receive as little as $6,000 total payout, with the highest payouts averaging $165,000. This disparity fuels objector activity: the settlement still needs judicial approval and faces notable pushback, with some law firms questioning the compensation structure and whether impacted individuals should receive more from Bayer. The U.S. Supreme Court's preemption review in *Monsanto Co. v. Durnell* — testing whether EPA label approval bars state failure-to-warn claims — adds a structural wildcard; a decision in Bayer's favor could effectively shield pesticide manufacturers from future cancer-related lawsuits nationwide. A decision is expected before the end of the Court's June 2026 term, meaning it could land before or contemporaneous with the July 9 fairness hearing, directly affecting class member opt-out calculations.

### IV. Keller Postman Google Mass Arbitration — $218B Demand Campaign, Active Filings

The largest mass arbitration campaign by dollar value in U.S. history continues to scale up. Following two landmark federal antitrust rulings finding that Google engaged in monopolistic and anticompetitive conduct, Keller Postman formally announced on May 11, 2026 that it is representing U.S. businesses in a coordinated mass arbitration seeking recovery for alleged advertising overcharges. Ashley Keller estimates potential claims for online search and display ads could reach $218 billion or more, based on calculations from an economist his firm has hired. That figure represents the largest mass arbitration campaign ever attempted by dollar value.

The mechanism is structurally sound from a precedent standpoint: due to contractual arbitration provisions implemented by Google, claims are proceeding through individual arbitration rather than class action litigation, with Keller Postman coordinating thousands of individual claims on behalf of businesses nationwide. According to Alphabet's public filings, U.S. businesses spent roughly $728 billion on Google search and display ads over the past decade, and experts in the federal antitrust cases have estimated that anticompetitive conduct may have significantly inflated those costs, creating the potential for substantial recovery under federal antitrust law. Keller argued that arbitration clauses Google has in its advertisers' contracts effectively prevent damages via class action litigation — but claimants can join together in mass arbitration, which is handled by a coordinated law firm and gives them more leverage in settlement negotiations.

The antitrust liability predicate is established: two federal courts have ruled against Google. The Google mass arbitration campaign is directly downstream of two landmark federal antitrust rulings in 2024: a federal judge in Washington D.C. found that Google illegally monopolized online search — the first major antitrust ruling against a tech giant in decades. On the ad tech side, Judge Brinkema's remedies ruling in the ad tech case is expected in 2026, with Keller Postman actively filing advertiser compensation claims ahead of state filing deadlines. Similar mass arbitrations have lasted 12 to 24 months between filing of claims and resolution, suggesting a resolution window of mid-2027 to mid-2028 for the bulk of the Google advertiser demands. Keller Postman believes most eligible businesses could recover up to 30% of their ad spend on Google Ads.
