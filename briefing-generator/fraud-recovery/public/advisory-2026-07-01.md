### BLMIS / Picard — Seventeenth Distribution Closed; Fee Creep Watch Continues (Bankr. S.D.N.Y., No. 08-01789)


On February 27, 2026, SIPA Trustee Irving H. Picard announced that the seventeenth pro rata interim distribution from the BLMIS Customer Fund commenced, distributing more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to account holders with allowed claims.
 
Inclusive of that distribution, aggregate payments to eligible BLMIS customers reached nearly $15.38 billion — including approximately $850.9 million in SIPC advances — with the seventeenth distribution representing 1.302% of each allowed claim, paid on 768 accounts.
 
Approximately 72.848% of each customer's allowed claim has now been paid across the seventeen interim distributions, unless that claim has already been fully satisfied.


The recovery-to-date figure matters for claim pricing. 
As of January 31, 2026, the SIPA Trustee has amassed approximately $15.366 billion through recoveries and settlement agreements since appointment in December 2008.
 
Three claims remain "deemed determined pending litigation," requiring the Trustee to maintain reserves; the required reserve for deemed determined claims stands at approximately $566.2 million, with other reserves (deferred payments and unallocated funds) at approximately $90.621 million.
 That $656+ million in reserves is the delta between current aggregate distributions and the theoretical ceiling — a meaningful number for secondary buyers pricing the tail risk on unsatisfied claims.

On the fee side: 
on February 13, 2026, the SIPA Trustee and BakerHostetler filed the 50th Interim Fee Application with the Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, covering work from August 1 through November 30, 2025, and seeking approval of fees representing approximately 63,774.40 hours of professional and paraprofessional services billed at an average public-interest-discounted rate of approximately $588.88 per hour.
 
The Bankruptcy Court hearing on that 50th Fee Application was scheduled for April 29, 2026.
 The 10% public-interest discount is structural — it has been applied since inception — but the blended rate creeping toward $589/hour across 63,000+ hours is a signal worth tracking for anyone pricing the SIPC-funded cost base against remaining recoverable value. 
All of the SIPA Trustee's recoveries are allocated to the Customer Fund subject to court approval; no administration costs are paid from recoveries — all trustee, legal, and accounting fees are paid by SIPC.
 The practical implication: the fee structure does not dilute claimant recoveries, but SIPC's $578.5 million in outstanding unreimbursed advances do create a senior priority claim on any incremental distributions to fully-satisfied accounts.

No eighteenth distribution motion has been publicly filed as of the last confirmed docket activity. The next distribution cycle will depend on continued settlement activity in surviving adversary proceedings. Claims investors should not anticipate an eighteenth motion before Q4 2026 at the earliest.

For claims investors: the final distribution schedule totaling more than $339 million across 14,000+ groups is the practical floor on remaining payouts. ### OneCoin / DOJ Remission — Petition Window Closed Today; $40 Million Pool Now Frozen to Timely Filers

Today is the hard deadline. 
Between 2014 and 2019, Ruja Ignatova and Karl Sebastian Greenwood co-founded OneCoin Ltd. in Sofia, Bulgaria, and orchestrated an international cryptocurrency investment scheme through a global MLM network in which victims invested over $4 billion in the fraudulent cryptocurrency.
 
Victims of the scheme could apply for compensation from a $40 million fund of seized assets established by the U.S. Department of Justice.
 
The Criminal Division's Money Laundering, Narcotics, and Forfeiture Section managed the process, with Kroll Settlement Administration LLC serving as the designated administrator.



Victims who purchased OneCoin packages between Q4 2014 and Q4 2019 and suffered a net financial loss were eligible; the FBI urged submission of petitions through the official program before the June 30, 2026 deadline.
 As of today, the window is closed. The $40 million fund against $4 billion in admitted losses represents a sub-1% gross recovery rate — a structural feature of the case that distinguishes it sharply from BLMIS (72.8% recovery to date) and which has significant implications for the secondary claim market: there is effectively no viable claims-trading thesis on OneCoin remission petitions at this dollar level.

The cross-border enforcement picture remains active on a parallel track. 
On January 16, 2026, the Royal Court of Guernsey ordered the registration and enforcement of an overseas forfeiture order made against funds of GBP 8,590,200.92, which German proceedings had found to be proceeds of Ignatova's criminal conduct.
 
The money was held at a Guernsey financial institution for the benefit of two Guernsey-registered companies through which Ignatova acquired two freehold flats in Kensington, London.
 
Co-founder Greenwood pleaded guilty and was sentenced to 20 years in prison in September 2023 along with $300 million in restitution; Ignatova, often called the "Cryptoqueen," has been a fugitive since 2017 and remains on the FBI's wanted list.
 The Guernsey forfeiture — enforceable via Germany's non-conviction-based confiscation mechanism — demonstrates that the cross-border tracing architecture (§ 423 Insolvency Act analogues, Mareva injunctions, MLAT cooperation) continues to yield incremental recoveries even where the primary defendant remains at large. Those proceeds are not yet flowing to the DOJ remission fund.

### Structural Theme — Ponzi Presumption and Net-Winner Clawback Exposure

The three active estates above present a continuing illustration of the asymmetry between net winners and net losers in Ponzi-scheme insolvencies. Under the Ponzi presumption — applied across SDNY BLMIS proceedings and widely adopted in SEC receivership courts — any transfer made in furtherance of the scheme is presumed to have been made with actual fraudulent intent, satisfying the intent prong of 11 U.S.C. § 548(a)(1)(A) and its state-law UVTA/UFTA equivalents. Net winners — those who withdrew more than they invested — hold no good-faith defense to the extent of their fictitious profits, which remain vulnerable to clawback regardless of whether they knew of the fraud. The BLMIS estate has pursued and continues to pursue net-winner clawbacks through hundreds of adversary proceedings. In the Stanford estate, the equivalent risk is addressed through the Receiver's litigation program. For claims buyers in either estate: the identity of the counterparty seller and whether that seller has received a clawback demand (or sits in a net-winner position) requires diligence before acquisition. A seller facing an open § 548 adversary proceeding may be purporting to transfer a claim that will be offset or voided entirely.
