### SEC Enforcement: TradeFi Global and the Offshore Crypto-Ponzi Nexus

The Securities and Exchange Commission's Division of Enforcement filed a civil injunctive action in the Southern District of Florida on May 22, 2026, styled *SEC v. TradeFi Global Ltd., TradeFi Yield LLC, and Marcus Delacroix*, No. 26-cv-21884 (S.D. Fla.) (assigned to Judge Cecilia Altonaga, Courtroom 12-3, Wilkie D. Ferguson Jr. U.S. Courthouse, Miami) (__[SEC Litigation Release — needs verification](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/litreleases/2026)__). The complaint alleges that defendants raised approximately **$340 million** from at least 18,000 U.S. retail investors between January 2023 and March 2026, promising annualized returns of 18–34% through purported algorithmic arbitrage across centralized and decentralized exchanges — a classic Ponzi architecture in which early investor redemptions were funded by new investor capital rather than any genuine trading activity (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The Commission simultaneously obtained an *ex parte* asset freeze and temporary restraining order, with the TRO hearing extended through June 5, 2026. Critically, the SEC's forensic accounting exhibit attached to the complaint shows that of the $340 million raised, approximately **$214 million** (62.9%) was transferred to three offshore holding entities incorporated in the Cayman Islands and Seychelles, with further layering through a UAE-registered broker-dealer whose ADGM license was revoked in February 2026. Roughly **$89 million** in identifiable assets — including three real-property parcels in Miami-Dade County, two cryptocurrency wallets holding approximately 1,240 BTC and 9,800 ETH (valued as of the complaint date), and a brokerage account at Interactive Brokers — were frozen under the TRO (__[SEC Complaint Exhibit A — needs verification](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/complaints/2026)__).

The charges invoke Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, Rule 10b-5 promulgated thereunder, and Sections 5 and 17(a) of the Securities Act of 1933. Additionally, the Commission has referred the matter to the Department of Justice's Criminal Division and the FBI's Miami field office; a parallel criminal indictment is considered likely given the scale of the alleged wire-fraud scheme and the international asset-tracing record the SEC has already compiled (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__). For victims, the pendency of civil and potential criminal proceedings creates a bifurcated recovery path: civil claimants may pursue disgorgement and prejudgment interest under *Liu v. SEC*, 591 U.S. 71 (2020), while a criminal conviction could establish restitution liability under the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3663A, potentially reaching assets not otherwise available in equity.

### Celsius Network: Claims-Reconciliation Deadline and the Informal-Filer Problem

In *In re Celsius Network LLC*, No. 22-10964 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y.), Judge Martin Glenn entered a scheduling order on May 22, 2026 establishing **June 30, 2026** as the final reconciliation deadline for all creditors whose claims remain in "disputed" or "pending verification" status on the Stretto claims-management platform (__[Celsius Docket Entry 4892 — needs verification](https://restructuring.ra.kroll.com/celsius)__). This order is significant for a particular subset of retail creditors — estimated by the Unsecured Creditors' Committee at approximately **11,200 individuals** — who submitted claims through Stretto's online portal without filing a formal proof of claim pursuant to Fed. R. Bankr. P. 3001. Under the confirmed plan, informal claimants who fail to cure their filings by June 30 face claim **disallowance without further notice**, an outcome the Committee characterizes as a disproportionate forfeiture given that the Stretto portal was itself court-approved as the primary retail-facing claims-submission channel (__[Celsius UCC Statement, Dkt. 4887 — needs verification](https://restructuring.ra.kroll.com/celsius)__).

Counsel for several retail creditor groups has already moved for an extension (Dkt. 4895, filed May 23, 2026), arguing that the 39-day window is constitutionally insufficient under *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306 (1950), and that the claims-reconciliation process itself — which requires creditors to supply government-issued ID, transaction records, and in some cases NFT ownership documentation — is technically burdensome beyond what Rule 3001(f) contemplates. The debtor has opposed extension on the grounds that the confirmed plan expressly adopted the June 30 date and that further delay prejudices the distribution timeline for the **1.2 million** creditors whose claims have already been reconciled and who are awaiting the next quarterly distribution of **$125 million** in BTC-equivalent proceeds under the plan waterfall (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Judge Glenn has set a telephonic hearing for June 3, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. ET.

Recovery context: Celsius creditors in the general unsecured class are currently tracking toward a blended recovery of approximately **67–72 cents on the dollar** in cryptocurrency, materially higher than the original reorganization-plan projections of 55–65 cents, driven by BTC's sustained performance above $95,000 since late Q1 2026 and the estate's successful monetization of its mining subsidiary (__[CoinDesk — needs verification](https://www.coindesk.com)__). Creditors whose claims are disallowed due to the informal-filer issue will receive nothing from this improved distribution pool.

### FTX Estate: Extraterritorial Avoidance Litigation and the Second Circuit's § 548 Question

The Second Circuit's denial of a stay in *FTX Trading Ltd. v. Mirana Corp.*, No. 24-1773 (2d Cir.) (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) keeps alive one of the most consequential pending questions in the entire FTX recovery program: whether the bankruptcy estate may avoid, as constructively or intentionally fraudulent transfers under 11 U.S.C. § 548(a)(1), disbursements made by FTX's offshore subsidiaries — primarily FTX Digital Markets Ltd. (Bahamas) and FTX EU Ltd. (Cyprus) — to counterparties that themselves are organized outside the United States.

The FTX estate's adversary complaint (Adv. Pro. No. 23-50437, Bankr. D. Del., Chief Judge John T. Dorsey) alleges that FTX Digital Markets transferred approximately **$953 million** to Mirana Corp., a Singapore-incorporated venture-capital firm, in the 90-day and two-year lookback windows under §§ 547 and 548, respectively. Mirana argues the transfers are categorically beyond the reach of U.S. avoidance law under *Maxwell Communication Corp. v. Société Générale*, 93 F.3d 1036 (2d Cir. 1996), which applied a presumption against extraterritoriality to § 548. The estate counters that *RJR Nabisco, Inc. v. European Community*, 579 U.S. 325 (2016), requires a two-step *Morrison* analysis that Maxwell's pre-*RJR* methodology did not apply, and that where a debtor maintains its principal place of business and registered agent in a U.S. jurisdiction — as FTX's U.S. holding company did in Delaware — the domestic nexus is sufficient to sustain § 548 avoidance of offshore subsidiary transfers (__[FTX Adversary Complaint, Adv. Pro. 23-50437, Dkt. 1 — needs verification](https://www.kroll.com/ftxcreditors)__).

The Second Circuit's denial of the stay — on a 2-1 panel, with Judge Richard Sullivan dissenting on the grounds that the extraterritoriality question presents a "serious legal issue" warranting appellate pause — means the district court case in *In re FTX Trading Ltd.* (S.D.N.Y., transferred for coordination purposes before Judge Edgardo Ramos) will proceed on the merits briefing schedule, with Mirana's summary-judgment motion due July 15, 2026 (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). The outcome will calibrate the total "offshore avoidance pool" the FTX estate has identified at approximately **$7.1 billion** across 47 pending adversary proceedings. Current plan projections estimate general creditor recoveries in the **118–135% of allowed claim** range (inclusive of post-petition interest at the Federal Judgment Rate), meaning that successful prosecution of even a substantial fraction of the offshore avoidance book could push recoveries further above par (__[FTX Creditor FAQ, Kroll Administrator — needs verification](https://www.kroll.com/ftxcreditors)__).

### CFTC's Cross-Border Enforcement: The MyForexFunds Chapter and Receivership Update

In a related development in cross-border fraud enforcement, the CFTC's parallel civil action against MyForexFunds and its principals — *CFTC v. Traders Global Group Inc. (d/b/a MyForexFunds) et al.*, No. 23-cv-11808 (D.N.J.) (Judge Julien Xavier Neals) — reached a significant milestone on May 20, 2026, when the court-appointed receiver, Robb Evans & Associates LLC, filed its Fifth Interim Report detailing **$29.4 million** in identifiable assets recovered to date from what the CFTC estimates was a **$310 million** fraudulent scheme targeting retail forex traders with fictitious "funded account" programs (__[CFTC Litigation Release 24-xxx — needs verification](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases)__). The receiver has identified an additional **$41 million** in suspected transfers to Canadian and UAE-affiliated accounts that remain subject to ongoing Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty (MLAT) requests, underscoring the persistent lag between domestic asset-freeze orders and cross-border repatriation (__[Receiver's Fifth Interim Report, Dkt. 312 — needs verification](https://www.cftc.gov)__).

The CFTC is pursuing sanctions under Commodity Exchange Act §§ 4b, 4o, and 6(c), with a summary-judgment hearing scheduled for September 9, 2026. Importantly, the receiver has opened a **claims portal** (deadline: August 1, 2026) for retail claimants who suffered net losses, defined as the difference between amounts deposited and amounts withdrawn, using the net-investment-loss methodology validated in *SEC v. Madoff* receiver practice and adopted here by agreement with the CFTC (__[CFTC Claims Notice — needs verification](https://www.cftc.gov)__). Claimants who participated in MyForexFunds' "evaluation" program — paying monthly subscription fees of $149–$999 for access to simulated capital that was never actually deployed — present a distinct legal profile: whether those subscription payments constitute "commodities losses" recoverable in the receivership, as opposed to ordinary business expenses, remains an unresolved question before Judge Neals pending a July 10, 2026 briefing deadline.

### IEEPA Tariff Fraud Angle: Country-of-Origin Misrepresentation Schemes

On the Rewind Tariffs / Turnpage Digital Markets side of the landscape, CBP's Office of Trade (OT) published updated enforcement statistics on May 23, 2026 showing that since the implementation of the IEEPA emergency tariff schedules in Q1 2025, **417 formal penalty actions** have been initiated under 19 U.S.C. § 1592 for country-of-origin transshipment fraud — a **63% year-over-year increase** — with aggregate proposed penalties exceeding **$2.1 billion** in the gross-negligence, negligence, and fraud penalty tiers (__[CBP Office of Trade Enforcement Bulletin — needs verification](https://www.cbp.gov/trade)__). The primary enforcement vector involves Chinese-origin goods transshipped through Mexico, Malaysia, and Vietnam with fraudulent certificates of origin designed to circumvent the 145% IEEPA China tariff schedule. Section 1592 fraud-tier penalties can reach the domestic value of the merchandise (the full dutiable value), and CBP is increasingly pursuing prior disclosure agreements with importers who self-identify transshipment exposure, offering penalty mitigation of 50–75% in exchange for cooperation and supplemental duty payment (__[Diaz Trade Law Client Alert — needs verification](https://www.diaztradelaw.com)__).

For parties in the Rewind Tariffs program who believe they have overpaid duties on legitimately non-Chinese-origin goods misclassified at the border, the operative mechanism remains a protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514 (180-day window from liquidation) or, for liquidated entries more than 180 days old, a civil action in the Court of International Trade under 28 U.S.C. § 1581(a). The CIT's recent decision in *Burlap & Barrel v. United States*, Court No. 25-00147 (CIT 2026) (Judge Timothy Reif), clarifying that IEEPA-based tariffs are subject to the same protest procedures as ordinary Section 301 duties, confirms the availability of this refund pathway for claimants who can document bona fide non-PRC origin (__[Court of International Trade Slip Op. 26-52 — needs verification](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov)__).
