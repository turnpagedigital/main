### BLMIS / Picard (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. No. 08-01789): Seventeenth Distribution Closed; Fifty-First Fee Cycle Pending


On February 27, 2026, SIPA Trustee Irving H. Picard announced that the seventeenth pro rata interim distribution from the Customer Fund had commenced, distributing more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims.
 
The seventeenth distribution represents 1.302% of each allowed claim and is paid on claims relating to 768 accounts; combined with the prior sixteen distributions, eligible customers have now received approximately 72.848% of their allowed claims.
 The headline recovery figure has continued to inch upward post-distribution: 
as of May 26, 2026, the SIPA Trustee has recovered or reached agreements to recover approximately $15.411 billion.


For claims pricing, the operative constraint is what remains outstanding above the 72.848% watermark. 
As of February 27, 2026, SIPC advances total approximately $850.9 million, and that commitment will continue to increase as claims currently in litigation are allowed as a result of settlements or the conclusion of litigation.
 
SIPC has received $272.4 million in reimbursement from the Customer Fund for advances on fully satisfied claims, leaving $578.5 million in SIPC advances outstanding.
 The Trustee's website flagged a fiftieth fee application statement in February 2026 — BakerHostetler's fees are SIPC-funded under the SIPA structure, so fee-order activity does not directly reduce the Customer Fund, but the pace of fee applications is a signal of ongoing recovery litigation. 
No funds recovered in the Madoff Recovery Initiative are used to pay costs associated with the recovery; all trustee, legal, and accounting fees, as well as administrative expenses, are paid by SIPC.
 The next distribution motion will require a further settlement or litigation recovery to fund it. Watch the Trustee's docket for any eighteenth motion filing; none is publicly visible as of this run.

Clawback posture: the Ponzi-presumption doctrine under § 548(a)(1)(A) and the two-year lookback (extended to six years under § 548(a)(1)(B) for constructive fraud, with state UFTA reaching further) remain operative for net winners who have not yet settled. Picard's clawback litigation portfolio has been the primary engine funding each successive distribution; the dip in per-distribution amounts over recent cycles — from $387 million (eleventh) to $253 million (seventeenth) — reflects the shrinking pool of unresolved defendants, not a change in legal theory.

### Stanford Receivership (N.D. Tex., SEC v. Stanford Int'l Bank, Ltd., No. 3:09-CV-00298-N, Judge David C. Godbey): Final Distribution Underway; Swiss Tranche Unresolved

This is the most operationally live event in the tracked portfolio this week. 
On March 9, 2026, Receiver Ralph S. Janvey filed with the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, Dallas Division, the Schedule of Payments to Be Made to Eligible Investor CD Claimants Pursuant to the Final Distribution Plan, in accordance with the October 8, 2025 Order Approving Receiver's Final Distribution Plan and the November 25, 2025 Order Approving Joint Motion to Amend.
 
According to the Receiver's update posted April 6, 2026, payments under the Final Distribution Plan began on April 3, 2026.


Two friction points are now live. First, the payment-mechanics dispute: 
claimant coalition COViSAL and numerous other claimants have raised concerns about the lack of clear information regarding how payments are being issued — specifically whether disbursements are being sent via physical check or wire transfer — a distinction that creates serious practical problems for international claimants.
 Second, and more consequential for claims-pricing, the Swiss tranche: 
COViSAL sent a formal written request to the Receiver on March 11, 2026 regarding the status of $8.1 million in Swiss assets released to the Receiver on December 17, 2025; the Receiver and his legal team had not responded as of April 2026, leaving open the question of whether those funds will be included in a future distribution separate from the current Final Distribution Plan.


A further procedural development landed June 1: 
in May 2026, Jaime Escalona submitted materials to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas regarding the Receiver's distribution process, and on June 1, 2026, the Receiver filed a response to that submission, which includes an update concerning the status of the Receiver's ongoing distribution efforts.
 Additionally, 
on May 12, 2026, the Court granted the Receiver's motion for authorization to dispose of certain Stanford records and equipment
 — a signal that the estate is entering administrative wind-down, though the Swiss funds issue and any residual claims disputes mean the docket is not closed.

For claims investors, the Final Distribution Plan filing and the April 3 commencement of payments are the last major liquidity event in a receivership that generated approximately $2.7 billion in total recoveries against roughly $5 billion in CD-holder hard-dollar losses. 
The five bank settlements that drove the bulk of recoveries — including $1.205 billion from Toronto Dominion Bank, $157 million from Société Générale Private Banking (Suisse) S.A., $100 million each from Trustmark National Bank and Independent Bank/Bank of Houston, and $40 million from HSBC Bank — totaled more than $1.6 billion.
 Secondary-market purchasers of Stanford CD claims approaching the end of this receivership should note that the Swiss tranche question ($8.1 million, modest in absolute terms but material for tail-end claimants) is unresolved and that the Receiver's June 1 response is the most current public indicator of distribution status.

### OneCoin / Ignatova (S.D.N.Y. Criminal Docket; DOJ Remission): June 30 Victim-Filing Deadline Is Four Days Away


OneCoin, co-founded by Ruja Ignatova and Karl Sebastian Greenwood, marketed and sold a fraudulent cryptocurrency through a global multi-level-marketing network beginning in 2014; the DOJ has launched a remission process for OneCoin fraud victims, with over $40 million in forfeited assets available for distribution.
 
The filing deadline is June 30, 2026.
 
The Criminal Division's Money Laundering, Narcotics, and Forfeiture Section is managing the process; Kroll Settlement Administration LLC serves as the designated administrator.
 Victims can file at www.onecoinremission.com per DOJ guidance attributed to U.S. Attorney Jay Clayton.

The $40 million remission pool is a fraction of the estimated losses. 
Throughout the scheme, OneCoin is believed to have defrauded victims out of more than $4 billion; prosecutors in the Southern District of New York have described this as one of the largest fraud schemes in history.
 The recovery rate on these forfeited assets — relative to the claimed loss universe — is under 1% of scheme-wide losses at current recovery levels, which is the central pricing constraint for any claims-buying strategy in this pool.

Cross-border asset tracing has made incremental progress on Ignatova's concealed holdings. 
On January 16, 2026, the Royal Court of Guernsey ordered the registration and enforcement of an overseas forfeiture order made against funds totaling GBP 8,590,200.92, which had been found, in German legal proceedings, to be the proceeds of Dr. Ruja Ignatova's criminal conduct.
 
The money was held with a Guernsey financial institution for the benefit of two Guernsey-registered companies; Ignatova had commissioned the purchase of shares in both companies to acquire two freehold flats in Kensington, London.
 
The German proceedings found that OneCoin was released with fraudulent intent; criminal proceedings against Ignatova could not conclude as her whereabouts remain unknown, and the German authorities therefore initiated "Independent Confiscation Proceedings," which led to the overseas forfeiture order.



Court proceedings across multiple jurisdictions have steadily eroded Ignatova's visible asset base; a UK court imposed a global asset freeze on Ignatova and her OneCoin associates in August 2024, and a Guernsey court ordered the seizure of properties worth millions held in her name through shell companies.
 The FBI continues to carry a $5 million reward for information leading to her capture; she remains at large. For claims investors, the June 30 deadline is a hard-stop for participation in the current $40 million DOJ remission round. Any second-wave distribution depends on further international asset recovery, which is proceeding via § 1782 requests, Mareva injunctions, and MLAT channels — tools that move on multi-year timescales.

### SEC Enforcement Pipeline: Atkins-Era Posture and the Receiver-Appointment Calculus

The institutional context for all forward-looking Ponzi recovery work has shifted. 
During fiscal year 2025, the Commission filed 456 enforcement actions and obtained orders for monetary relief totaling $17.9 billion.
 But the philosophical reorientation under Chairman Paul Atkins is directional: 
the current Commission intends to prioritize the policing of alleged fraud, market manipulation, insider trading, and breaches of fiduciary duty while moving away from cases it views as regulation by enforcement or as lacking a direct nexus to investor harm.
 
Current leadership is reorienting the program away from what it characterizes as headline-driven, high-volume enforcement activity and toward a so-called "back to basics" approach to fraud, investor protection, and individual accountability.


Specifically relevant to the receiver-appointment pipeline: 
notable 2025 actions included charges in connection with several large-scale Ponzi schemes, including one involving approximately 2,700 investors and an alleged $400 million in losses and another involving approximately 300 investors and more than $140 million in alleged losses.
 
One of the most notable trends from FY 2025 is the Commission's increased emphasis on charging individuals; approximately two-thirds of stand-alone actions involved charges against one or more individuals, representing a 27% year-over-year increase.
 For claims-market participants, a fraud-and-individual-accountability focus suggests the SEC will continue generating new receiverships — but the pace of crypto-adjacent enforcement has slowed, with several Biden-era crypto enforcement actions voluntarily dismissed in Q1 2026. The dismissal of the Tron/Justin Sun action (filed March 5, 2026, with Rainberry Inc. consenting to a $10 million civil penalty on a Section 17(a)(3)-only violation — no disgorgement) illustrates how crypto enforcement resolutions are now yielding smaller penalty pools and cleaner clawback targets. (__[Morrison Foerster, Top 5 SEC Enforcement Developments for March 2026](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/260421-top-5-sec-enforcement-developments-for-march-2026)__)

One forward-looking structural issue: 
the U.S. Supreme Court is deciding SEC v. Sripetch on whether the SEC has the ability to seek disgorgement as a penalty without showing investors suffered financial harm; the case was slated for oral argument on April 20, 2026.
 A narrow ruling limiting disgorgement availability would directly compress the penalty pools feeding future Fair Funds and receiver distributions — a first-order pricing variable for claims buyers evaluating nascent enforcement cases.
