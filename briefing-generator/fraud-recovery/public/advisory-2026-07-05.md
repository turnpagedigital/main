### BLMIS / Picard — Seventeenth Distribution Closed; Litigation Pipeline Determines Eighteenth Timing

The BLMIS liquidation, *SIPC v. Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities LLC*, Bankr. S.D.N.Y., No. 08-01789, is now in its seventeenth-distribution run. 
On February 27, 2026, SIPA Trustee Irving H. Picard announced that the seventeenth pro rata interim distribution commenced, distributing more than $253 million on a pro rata basis to BLMIS account holders with allowed claims.
 
Including this distribution, aggregate payments to eligible BLMIS customers total nearly $15.38 billion, including approximately $850.9 million in SIPC advances; the seventeenth distribution itself represents 1.302% of each allowed claim, paid on claims relating to 768 accounts.
 Cumulative recovery through seventeen distributions now stands at approximately 
72.848% of each customer's allowed claim, unless that claim has been fully satisfied.



As of June 26, 2026, the SIPA Trustee has recovered or reached agreements to recover approximately $15.456 billion.
 
SIPC has committed approximately $850.9 million to the BLMIS liquidation, with advances expected to increase as claims currently in litigation are allowed through settlements or the conclusion of litigation.
 The delta between the $15.456 billion recovered and the $15.38 billion distributed signals that additional funds remain in the pipeline — but the next distribution depends entirely on the pace of pending adversary proceedings and any new settlements. 
The Trustee continues to move forward aggressively to resolve litigation and appeals that are delaying further distributions to BLMIS customers.


For claims-pricing purposes, the marginal rate of recovery from the eighteenth distribution onward will be determined by what remains in contested adversary proceedings. The case has now run 17 years; the fee structure is materially favorable to claimants because 
no funds recovered in the Madoff Recovery Initiative are used to pay costs associated with the recovery — all trustee, legal, and accounting fees, as well as administrative expenses, are paid by SIPC.
 That funding model is unusual and differentiates BLMIS claim pricing from virtually every other large Ponzi receivership, where professional fees erode distributable assets. 
As of March 2026, the Trustee had recovered $15.3 billion through legal action against Madoff associates, feeder funds, and scheme beneficiaries, and the DOJ separately recovered an additional $4.3 billion.

### Stanford Financial — Final Distribution Plan Executing; Swiss Funds Allocation Unresolved

The Stanford Financial receivership (*SEC v. Stanford International Bank, Ltd.*, N.D. Tex., Dallas Div., Receiver Ralph Janvey, J. David C. Godbey) has entered terminal distribution mode. 
On March 9, 2026, the Receiver filed with the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, Dallas Division, the Schedule of Payments to Be Made to Eligible Investor CD Claimants Pursuant to the Final Distribution Plan.
 
Payments on the Final Distribution Schedule total more than $339 million and include payments to over 14,000 claim groups, to be issued on a rolling basis beginning 14 days after the filing, with the process expected to take several weeks.


The rolling disbursement is now well underway. 
On April 3, 2026, the Receiver issued payments to over 2,000 claimant groups under the Final Distribution Plan totaling approximately $47.5 million, with further updates promised as additional payments are issued.
 By early May, the pace had accelerated: 
as of May 4, 2026, the Receiver had issued payments to over 5,600 claimant groups.


Two unresolved items are material for claims investors. First, the Swiss asset tranche: 
on December 17, 2025, the Antiguan Joint Liquidators informed the Receiver that the Swiss Ancillary Bankruptcy proceeding concerning Stanford International Bank had concluded, and transferred $8.1 million to the Receiver pursuant to the Cross-Border Protocol, with no further distributions from Switzerland expected.
 As of April 2026, claimant advocacy group COViSAL had submitted a second written request to the Receiver regarding the disposition of these $8.1 million in Swiss funds. 
COViSAL sent a formal written request on March 11, 2026 titled "SECOND REQUEST: Status of $8.1 Million Swiss Funds" — and as of early April, the Receiver and his legal team had not responded.
 Whether those funds roll into the Final Distribution Plan or require a separate allocation order is unresolved.

Second, Venezuelan claimants faced a delivery interruption. 
Beginning December 11, 2025, the Receivership was unable to arrange delivery of distribution payments via physical check to Venezuelan addresses; as of March 11, 2026, courier delivery had resumed.
 For claims-buyers holding Venezuelan-situs positions, the delivery interruption may have affected the 90-day window for cashing checks on prior distributions — an administrative choke point worth confirming before acquiring any such positions.

The receivership's total recoveries — including more than $1.3 billion from bank litigation settlements — significantly exceeded expectations for a case originally written off as nearly total-loss. 
On January 29, 2025, Judge Godbey ordered the closure of the 16-year lawsuit and the creation of a Fair Fund for the Receiver to distribute restitution amounts.
 The Final Distribution Plan executes that order. Claim trading at this stage is essentially duration arbitrage on a payment-schedule that has been court-approved but is subject to reversion risk (uncashed checks) and residual allocation decisions on the Swiss tranche.

### OneCoin / Ignatova — Guernsey Forfeiture Closed; Schneider Extradition Ruling in France

The OneCoin asset-recovery thread — anchored to *United States v. Ignatova* (S.D.N.Y.) and parallel German proceedings in Bielefeld — produced two meaningful developments in early 2026.

On the asset side: 
on January 16, 2026, the Royal Court of Guernsey ordered the registration and enforcement of an overseas forfeiture order against funds totaling GBP 8,590,200.92, which had been found in German legal proceedings to be the proceeds of Ruja Ignatova's criminal conduct.
 
German prosecutors in Bielefeld, working with the Royal Court of Guernsey, traced two of Ignatova's London properties — a Kensington penthouse sold for £10 million and a smaller flat for £1.4 million — through shell companies registered in the Channel Islands; after Ignatova ignored a 28-day ultimatum, the Court released GBP 8,590,200.92 plus interest into the Seized Asset Fund, to be claimed by Bielefeld for OneCoin victims.
 The mechanism used — German independent confiscation proceedings in absentia, then cross-border recognition in Guernsey — is precisely the model that asset-recovery practitioners should track for other fugitive-controlled offshore structures. 
Criminal proceedings against Ignatova could not conclude because her whereabouts are unknown; accordingly, German authorities initiated independent confiscation proceedings to reach the proceeds.


On the network side: 
Frank Schneider, a former deputy chief of Luxembourg's foreign-intelligence service who advised Ignatova through his firm Sandstone, fled French house arrest in May 2023, was returned, and in April 2026 the Nancy Court of Appeal sided with Washington's extradition request — though Schneider has indicated he will appeal.
 If Schneider is ultimately extradited to the S.D.N.Y., his cooperation potential is substantial: he sits at the intersection of the OneCoin financial network, European banking relationships, and offshore structuring.

Ignatova herself remains a fugitive. 
She is publicly listed on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list, and U.S. authorities have announced a reward of up to $5 million for information leading to her arrest and/or conviction.
 The global strategy, confirmed by the German prosecutorial posture, is to systematically neutralize her asset base regardless of whether she is physically apprehended. 
In April 2026, authorities issued a court ultimatum giving Ignatova a 28-day window to object to the confiscation of additional assets — legal maneuvers described as part of a broader effort to dismantle the financial infrastructure left behind after the OneCoin collapse.


For claims investors, the operative question is whether victim-restitution proceeds from the Guernsey forfeiture — and any future asset-stripping actions — are being routed through a structured claims process. At this stage, the Bielefeld proceedings hold the restitution mandate; no U.S. victim-compensation fund or receiver has been appointed in the U.S. OneCoin matter. That structural gap limits claims-market liquidity.

### Power Block Coin, LLC — Fee Wind-Down and Liquidating Trust Trajectory

*In re Power Block Coin, LLC*, Bankr. D. Utah, No. 24-23041, J. Cathleen D. Parker, represents the intersection of the crypto-insolvency and fraud-recovery tracks: a Utah cryptocurrency financial-services company that filed June 20, 2024 under Chapter 11, and which the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors has been driving toward a liquidating-trust structure.

The June 9, 2026 hearing (Docket Entry 519, per the tracked-case ground truth) followed a May 14, 2026 notice of hearing on the Amplēo Fee Application. 
Judge Cathleen D. Parker set a hearing for June 9, 2026 at 9:30 a.m. (Utah time) on the Third and Final Application of CFO Solutions, LLC d/b/a Amplēo for Allowance of Compensation; Amplēo, as financial advisor and accountant to the Debtor, sought final allowance of $10,436.50 in fees for the period April 1, 2025 through April 22, 2026, plus previously allowed interim fees.
 A final fee application from the debtor's financial advisor at this level of granularity signals the estate is in clean-up mode.

The structural backdrop: 
the Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors filed a comprehensive liquidating plan in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah (Case No. 24-bk-23041), with Greenberg Traurig, LLP on January 30, 2025, proposing to wind down the company through a Liquidating Trust overseen by an independent trustee.
 
The plan addresses claims totaling over $192 million, including significant claims from Celsius Network LLC exceeding $133 million, with a disinterested Liquidating Trustee appointed to manage assets and pursue potential legal claims.



The committee's filing raised concerns about pre-bankruptcy management, citing unexplained losses of cryptocurrency assets, insider transactions including loans to affiliated entities at allegedly below-market rates, and specifically a $17.4 million loan to Solara Communities LLC — an affiliate developing real property in Toquerville, Utah — the unsecured status of which and recent encumbrances on the underlying property drew committee scrutiny.
 The Solara loan is the primary clawback vector: if a liquidating trustee pursues it as a fraudulent transfer under 11 U.S.C. § 548 or Utah UVTA, recovery depends on the property's current value and any intervening liens.

The Celsius Network claim ($133+ million) effectively sets a ceiling on creditor-committee leverage in plan negotiations. Celsius itself is working through its own reorganized structure; how the Celsius estate handles this Power Block Coin claim — whether as a non-core asset to be monetized or held — affects the claims landscape for other Power Block Coin creditors.

### SEC Enforcement Posture — Fraud Cases Remain Priority; Retail Investor Protection Anchors Pipeline

The enforcement backdrop for new-receivership creation remains active. 
On April 3, 2026, the DOJ and SEC announced parallel enforcement actions against Vincent J. Camarda, Long Island-based investment adviser and CEO of A.G. Morgan Financial Advisors LLC, in connection with an alleged fraud that took at least $138 million from more than 430 investors — many of them elderly — with Camarda pleading guilty the same day to securities fraud and investment adviser fraud, facing up to 20 years in prison and restitution exceeding $160 million.
 That combination — parallel DOJ/SEC action, same-day guilty plea, and restitution order — is the enforcement template that precedes most new SEC-driven receivership appointments.


At the 2026 SEC Speaks Conference, Acting Enforcement Director Sam Waldon declared that the Commission's Enforcement Division is moving "full steam ahead" by focusing on quality over quantity and bringing actions against those who "lie, cheat, and steal."
 
SEC Chairman Atkins has promised that the enforcement program would return to "first principles" by focusing on fraud and investor harm, including offering frauds, insider trading, accounting and financial frauds, and investment advisers' breaches of fiduciary duty.
 That posture is consistent with a pipeline of retail-fraud cases that, at the charging stage, are precursors to receiver appointments — the event that creates claims-market entry points.

Claims investors should take three near-term steps. First, for Stanford Financial positions: confirm with the Receiver's claims agent whether your specific check has been issued under the Final Distribution Schedule and whether the 90-day cashing deadline has run; Venezuelan-situs positions warrant particular attention given the December 2025–March 2026 delivery interruption. Second, for OneCoin exposure: the Guernsey forfeiture and the Schneider extradition ruling are positive indicators of asset-recovery momentum, but no U.S. victim-restitution vehicle exists yet — buyers of OneCoin victim claims are pricing German Bielefeld-driven recovery with inherent jurisdiction and timing uncertainty, and should model recovery timelines against Germany's independent confiscation framework, not a U.S. § 548 or receivership process. Third, for Power Block Coin (24-23041): the estate is approaching plan confirmation with the Liquidating Trust structure in view; the $17.4 million Solara Communities loan and the status of the Celsius $133 million claim are the two assets to track — monitor the docket at CourtListener ID 68873409 for any adversary-proceeding filings, plan-confirmation scheduling orders, or trustee-appointment motions that would set the distribution timeline and clawback-litigation scope.
