### I. Sterling Capital / Myers: Parallel Criminal Indictment + SEC Civil Complaint (N.D. Ill.)

The week's leading new enforcement action is a textbook parallel-track prosecution. On June 5, 2026, the SEC charged John Sterling Myers and his two companies, Sterling Capital, LLC and Sterling Capital Management, LLC, with allegedly engaging in a multi-year fraud, alleging that from January 2022 through at least July 2025, the defendants misappropriated investor money, falsified investor account statements, and engaged in other misconduct while acting as investment advisers to a pooled investment vehicle, Sterling Capital Investments, LLC.

The criminal track followed immediately. A Chicago resident operating as an unregistered investment advisor was indicted on four counts of federal wire fraud after allegedly running a Ponzi scheme that raised approximately $4 million from at least 28 investors across five states — and left more than $3.6 million of their money gone. Myers, 41, operated Sterling Capital and Sterling Capital Management as unregistered investment advisory firms out of a home office in Chicago. From 2022 to 2025, Myers fraudulently solicited and obtained money from three victims, including a husband and wife from Michigan, based on false representations about the performance and value of the investments, according to an indictment returned Thursday in U.S. District Court in Chicago.

The mechanics of the scheme were granular and well-documented in both the civil complaint and the indictment. According to the SEC complaint, Myers marketed Sterling Capital Investments beginning in January 2022, describing it on a website as a "premier capital pool" and "leader in asset management," touting his prior investment banking experience on Wall Street — he had worked as an analyst and associate at a New York investment bank from 2007 to 2013, before being laid off — and falsely listed two siblings in executive roles. Neither sibling was involved with the firm. Myers raised approximately $4 million from at least 28 investors across five states, many of whom were family members, friends, or personal acquaintances.

The fabricated performance data was brazen. Myers distributed fabricated quarterly account statements depicting annual returns ranging from 16% to 54%, depending on the investor. When the scheme's structural weakness became apparent, Myers compounded the harm: Sterling Capital's only brokerage account was closed by the brokerage firm in or around March 2024 due to a pattern of third-party deposits, trading losses, and withdrawals; Myers subsequently routed investor funds through his personal accounts. His own tax returns reported more than $890,000 in deductible trading losses in 2023 and more than $1 million in 2024. When investors requested redemptions in spring 2025 totaling approximately $560,000, Myers told them the fund's assets had been frozen due to the SEC's investigation — a claim the complaint characterizes as false — and the fund did not have the assets to meet the withdrawals.

On the civil charging posture, the SEC's complaint, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, charges the defendants with violating Section 17(a) of the Securities Act of 1933; Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and Rule 10b-5 thereunder; and Sections 206(1), 206(2), and 206(4) of the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and Rule 206(4)-8 thereunder, along with control person liability pursuant to Section 20(a) of the Exchange Act, and seeks permanent injunctive relief, disgorgement with prejudgment interest, and civil penalties against all defendants.

Critically, none of the three entities — Myers, Sterling Capital LLC, or Sterling Capital Management LLC — has ever been registered with the SEC as an investment adviser. That registration gap matters enormously in the recovery context: it forecloses SIPC protection, leaves FINRA arbitration unavailable as a claims vehicle, and means that investor recoveries will depend entirely on the SEC disgorgement pool plus any criminal restitution ordered in the parallel case.

Tomorrow's hearing is the critical near-term date: Myers, charged with four counts of wire fraud, is scheduled for arraignment in federal court in Chicago at 10:00 a.m. on June 12, 2026, before U.S. District Judge Edmond E. Chang. Each count carries a maximum penalty of 20 years in federal prison. Claim-holders and victim-class counsel should monitor the docket in the Northern District of Illinois for restitution proceedings; the standard practice in parallel SEC/DOJ cases is to coordinate disgorgement and restitution to avoid duplicative recoveries, which means the SEC's civil timeline will shadow the criminal case closely.

### III. A.G. Morgan / Camarda (E.D.N.Y., No. 26-CR-51) — Sentencing Horizon

The Camarda matter remains on the radar for elderly-investor recovery practitioners as sentencing approaches. Vincent Camarda pleaded guilty on April 3, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York to one count of securities fraud and one count of investment adviser fraud, facing up to 20 years in prison, $160 million in restitution, and $6.6 million in forfeiture, stemming from a scheme spanning January 2017 through December 2024 in which he defrauded roughly 400 clients. Sentencing before U.S. District Judge Nusrat J. Choudhury is expected in August 2026.

The fraud's mechanics are instructive for recovery practitioners assessing collectibility. From about mid-2020 through the end of 2023, Camarda and McArthur steered advisory clients into promissory notes issued by five private funds they controlled, telling investors the products were conservative and diversified; instead, four of the funds invested entirely in a single high-risk mining venture, while the fifth funneled money into a startup coffee shop owned by Camarda's son. According to federal prosecutors, Camarda diverted client funds into a mining business and a food service company, and also helped himself directly: wire transfers from client accounts funded plastic surgery, expensive jewelry, luxury travel, and credit card bills.

Asset recovery prognosis is bleak. The asset picture today is stark: both Camarda's office building in Massapequa and his personal residence in Amityville are in foreclosure proceedings. The court has ordered restitution of at least $160,022,836 and forfeiture of $6,639,498 — neither figure guarantees that defrauded investors will recover meaningful amounts, as schemes of this scale rarely yield full recovery for victims.

The civil track against co-defendant James McArthur, who served as the firm's president and chief compliance officer, remains live: McArthur remains a defendant in the SEC's civil case, has not entered a criminal guilty plea, and his litigation continues in the Eastern District of New York. The SEC's civil complaint, filed simultaneously with the criminal plea, names Camarda, McArthur, and A.G. Morgan Financial Advisors LLC and seeks permanent injunctions, disgorgement of ill-gotten gains with interest, and civil penalties against all three.

### IV. Macro Enforcement Context: DOJ National Fraud Division + Parallel-Track Surge

The Myers/Sterling Capital and Camarda actions are not isolated; they reflect a documented surge in parallel-track enforcement activity. The White House's fiscal year 2027 budget request seeks $30 million for the DOJ's newly established National Fraud Division, which would support 140 positions including 100 attorneys, with the budget document stating that these resources would "equip the department to investigate and prosecute fraudsters and provide much needed relief to those harmed by their schemes." The SEC is seeking a similar budget enhancement to its Enforcement Division compared to what was allocated in 2026, and FinCEN is seeking $25 million more in 2027 to bolster its Bank Secrecy Act enforcement program.

The institutional count is notable: the DOJ's Criminal Division announced in early 2026 that its Fraud Section had brought white collar charges against more than 200 individuals and secured 140 criminal convictions, and investment adviser fraud cases have remained a staple of the SEC's enforcement docket.

For recovery practitioners, the practical implication of expanded National Fraud Division capacity is a faster civil referral pipeline — the SEC increasingly files its civil complaint on the same day as or within 48 hours of the criminal indictment, as it did in both the Myers matter (civil complaint June 5; indictment returned the same week) and the Camarda matter (civil complaint filed the same day as the guilty plea on April 3, 2026). This simultaneity compresses the window in which sophisticated claimants and recovery counsel can file protective civil actions before a receiver or disgorgement pool is established — counsel should be monitoring PACER and SEC EDGAR for parallel civil complaint filings the moment a criminal indictment is announced in any advisory-fraud matter.
