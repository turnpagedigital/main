🎵 Jamendo just drew a new fault line in AI copyright litigation — and it's not a book case.

Filed this week in N.D. Cal., Jamendo v. Nvidia targets Fugatto and Audio Flamingo, claiming Nvidia trained its AI music models on Jamendo's curated database without authorization. Demand: injunction plus damages of no less than €17.8M (~$20.3M USD).

What makes this structurally distinct from the shadow-library suits isn't the dollar figure. It's the § 107(4) market-harm theory. Jamendo already sells machine-learning licenses for its catalog. If Nvidia used the MTG-Jamendo Dataset — 55,000+ tagged audio tracks built with Universitat Pompeu Fabra — for exactly the kind of commercial AI use Jamendo monetizes, the market-substitution argument is concrete in a way that general web-scraping cases aren't. The Thomson Reuters v. ROSS analogy is the right frame here.

One complication worth watching: the U.S. Copyright Office issued the dataset registration on June 17, 2026 — eight days before filing — while alleged infringement was discovered around March 2024. Under 17 U.S.C. § 412, statutory damages under § 504(c) are generally unavailable for infringement that began before registration. That limits remedies exposure unless Jamendo can bring the claim within the three-month publication window.

Meanwhile, Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal. 3:24-cv-05417) still has no final approval order on the public docket as of the June 22 CourtListener sync. Distribution cannot start until the order enters and the objector appeal window closes.

Practical takeaway: if you license curated datasets for ML use, document those transactions now. That commercial licensing record is your § 107(4) foundation if unauthorized training surfaces later.
