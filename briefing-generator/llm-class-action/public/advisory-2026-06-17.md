### Bartz v. Anthropic — Final Approval in Submission; Distribution Calendar Now Operative


After a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench and indicated that short orders may follow.


The procedural posture emerging from the May 14 hearing is precise. 
The Court's post-hearing order directed Anthropic, by no later than May 21, 2026, to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; plaintiffs were simultaneously required to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1); the Court stated it would not require anything more from objectors and would consider no further submissions from them.
 That supplemental briefing closed over three weeks ago. The matter is fully submitted.

The claims posture is unusually strong. 
Class counsel filed papers advising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed March 19, 2026, eleven days before the claims deadline.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson reported at the May 14 hearing that the payout per work will be approximately $3,100, with a 92.77% participation rate across the works list.


On attorneys' fees, the headline number shifted materially during briefing. 
Class counsel urged final approval of the $1.5 billion settlement alongside a fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Separately, Anthropic objected to the $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel, arguing there is "scant justification" for that request.
 Judge Martínez-Olguín questioned co-leads Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman at the hearing about the fee multiplier and cost reserve — those issues remain under submission with the broader approval motion.

For recovery-side counsel, the distribution timeline under the Plan of Allocation is now the operative framework. 
Per the settlement administrator's schedule: June 11, 2026 was the date for distribution calculations; payment disbursement is expected to begin June 2026 or later, subject to change.
 
Under the payment schedule, Anthropic's next tranche of $450 million (Scheduled Payment 1) falls due September 25, 2026; a second $450 million payment is scheduled for September 25, 2027.
 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved; payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest, and Anthropic is paying into the fund over time so not all funds will be disbursed at once.


One point that warrants close watch for claims investors: 
Anthropic has denied any wrongdoing, and the infringement question has not been adjudicated; the settlement does not create binding precedent that future courts cannot find such use non-infringing.
 Any objector appeal to the Ninth Circuit — permissible after entry of the final approval order — would toll the distribution timeline and could push initial payments into late 2026 or beyond.

**What changed and why it matters:** The approval order is the final remaining gating item. Once entered, the clock starts on Anthropic's destruction of the LibGen/PiLiMi dataset copies, the initial payment tranche, and any appellate risk window. Rights-holders and claims investors should be positioned now rather than waiting for the docket entry.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Two-Front Music-Publisher War; Concord I MSJ Approaching Decision

The Concord litigation has bifurcated into two separately docketed actions before the same judge in the Northern District of California, creating a layered risk profile that matters for anyone evaluating Anthropic's exposure posture in the run-up to any future licensing discussions.

**Concord I** — 
the Northern District of California action in *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL
 — is the lead case. 
Fact and expert discovery in Concord I have been completed, and the case is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — which will govern the viability of claims in both actions.



Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO have asked the federal judge to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrights and to reject the AI company's fair use defense.
 The publishers' cross-MSJ papers are direct on the four-factor framework. 
On fair use, the publishers argue Anthropic's use was purely commercial — noting the company is "a for-profit technology company valued at $380 billion or more" with a revenue run rate approaching $14 billion — that it copied lyrics in full, and that its output directly competes with licensed lyrics services.
 
On the infringement side, the filing states Anthropic "does not deny that the lyrics to Publishers' Works are included in Claude's training data" and "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."


The market-harm argument under § 107(4) has factual teeth here. 
The publishers' brief cites a Deezer study estimating over 60,000 AI-generated tracks were being submitted to that platform daily by January 2026, accounting for 3% of total streams
 — direct evidence that generative AI outputs are substituting for licensed works in a measurable market segment.

**Concord II** — 
Anthropic moved to stay *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK ("Concord II"), pending final resolution of the substantially similar Concord I litigation or, in the alternative, pending resolution of the parties' cross-motions for summary judgment in Concord I and any subsequent appeal.
 
Concord II is the publishers' second, larger lawsuit against Anthropic filed in January 2026, covering more than 20,000 songs and seeking over $3 billion in statutory damages.
 The stay motion was noticed for hearing May 20, 2026 at Courtroom 7 of the San Jose Division, 280 South 1st Street. No ruling has been publicly confirmed in available sources.

The strategic dynamic is clear: a publishers' victory on the Concord I MSJ — particularly a finding that lyrics reproduction is not fair use — would carry preclusive effect into Concord II and potentially force Anthropic to negotiate licensing terms at scale. Conversely, an Anthropic victory would effectively resolve both dockets short of trial. Judge Eumi K. Lee's MSJ ruling is the most consequential unresolved fair-use question in the AI-copyright system at the moment.

### In re OpenAI Copyright MDL — Discovery Leverage Consolidating in SDNY


*In re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143, is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein with referral to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang in the Southern District of New York.
 
The MDL consolidates 16 suits from news organizations, authors, and others alleging unauthorized use of works to train large language models.


The most consequential 2026 development in this docket remains the January 5 production order. 
Judge Sidney H. Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs to plaintiffs.
 
Judge Wang's relevance finding held that even output logs without direct reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's fair use defense — logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal patterns relevant to whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works.
 OpenAI had sought to limit production to conversations referencing specific works; the court rejected that framing. That ruling is now the evidentiary fulcrum for the entire MDL.


On March 26, 2025, the court ruled that all copyright infringement claims survive, the unfair competition claim should be dismissed with prejudice, and most DMCA claims should be dismissed without prejudice.
 
OpenAI and Microsoft filed answers to the first amended complaint on April 29, 2025; a second amended complaint followed on May 28, 2025.
 
Active discovery disputes as of late 2025 and early 2026 included issues concerning deleted datasets, privilege assertions, and the scope of discovery into how certain book-related datasets were handled.
 That dataset-deletion issue carries significant risk: internal communications about the decision to delete training data could undercut fair use arguments and support § 504 statutory damages claims at the higher willfulness threshold.


Authors including John Grisham, George R.R. Martin, Jonathan Franzen, and Jodi Picoult are plaintiffs in the consolidated author suits.
 
Legal analysts suggest the 20 million conversation logs — once analyzed by plaintiffs' experts — will likely determine settlement terms across the consolidated litigation, as they may reveal patterns of systematic output that mirrors copyrighted training content.

### System-Level Observations — What the Dockets Are Telling You

Three doctrinal threads are running simultaneously across these matters, and they interact:

**1. Training-data piracy versus licensed-acquisition distinction.** The Alsup ruling in Bartz drew a bright line: 
Judge William Alsup split the baby in June 2025 — he ruled that Anthropic's use of legally acquired books was "quintessentially transformative" and protected as fair use, but simultaneously held that downloading and keeping pirated copies was not fair use.
 That line is not transferable to the Concord MSJ, where the training corpus did not come from any authorized licensed acquisition — the publishers allege Anthropic simply ingested lyrics without a license.

**2. Output substitution as § 107(4) market harm.** The Concord brief's Deezer data point, the OpenAI ChatGPT log production order, and the Bartz settlement's piracy hook all converge on the same statutory factor: does the AI system's output substitute for the original market? The January 2026 log-production order in the MDL is structured precisely to answer that question. Whoever wins the discovery fight over those logs controls the narrative on factor four.

**3. Settlement as precedent-setter without stare decisis.** 
Anthropic has denied any wrongdoing, and the settlement does not create binding precedent — future courts may find such use non-infringing.
 That matters for claims investors: the $1.5 billion in *Bartz* establishes a market comparator for negotiations, not a legal floor. Defendants in the OpenAI MDL and Concord proceedings will resist any argument that Bartz prices the infringement.
