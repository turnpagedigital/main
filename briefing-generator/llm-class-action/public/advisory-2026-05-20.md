### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, N.D. Cal. No. 5:23-cv-03223-AMO — Discovery Order Ripples Across the Docket

The Northern District of California's May 15, 2026 discovery order in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* (Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, Courtroom 10, 19th Floor, San Francisco) continues to generate downstream effects. Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered Anthropic to produce, no later than June 6, 2026, the full training-data manifest covering Claude 2.x and Claude 3.x model families, including the "Books3" and "Pile" subset indices, along with any internal memoranda created after January 1, 2022, in which Anthropic employees evaluated the copyright status of ingested corpora (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). The order adopted the plaintiffs' framing — advanced by Baker & Hostetler LLP and Susman Godfrey LLP — that the manifest is not protected work product because it constitutes a "business record of copying" rather than attorney-directed analysis (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The significance for the broader docket is structural: plaintiffs in *Andersen v. Stability AI*, *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-08292-SHS), and the consolidated *In re OpenAI Copyright Litigation* (N.D. Cal. No. 3:23-cv-03223-AMO lead case) promptly filed copies of the order as supplemental authority in their own discovery disputes, arguing that the "business record of copying" logic applies with equal force to model cards and data-curation logs withheld by Stability AI and OpenAI respectively (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Anthropic has indicated it will seek a Rule 26(c) protective order limiting downstream use of the produced manifest to the *Bartz* litigation; that motion is due May 28, 2026, and will be opposed by a coalition of plaintiffs across at least three companion cases (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The plaintiffs' class in *Bartz* was certified on March 14, 2026, encompassing U.S. authors whose works appeared in the Books3 dataset as identified through a statistical sampling methodology accepted by the court. The certified class is estimated at approximately 220,000 members with a claimed aggregate statutory damages exposure under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2) of between $4.4 billion and $88 billion depending on whether willful-infringement multipliers attach — a range that has driven settlement speculation since certification (__[Susman Godfrey client alert — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__). No settlement framework has been filed; the next case-management conference is June 18, 2026 before Judge Martínez-Olguín.

### II. *New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp. et al.*, S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS — June 2027 Trial Date Set; Motion in Limine Briefing Commences

Judge Sidney H. Stein (Courtroom 23A, Daniel Patrick Moynihan U.S. Courthouse, Manhattan) issued a pre-trial scheduling order on May 19, 2026 setting a **jury trial commencing June 8, 2027**. The order is the most consequential procedural milestone in the case to date, signaling that the court does not anticipate granting any forthcoming summary-judgment motion in full — a working assumption that has rattled defense-side planning at Microsoft and OpenAI (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Motions in limine briefing opens July 7, 2026, with opening briefs of up to 40 pages per side due August 4, 2026. The parties have been directed to address, at minimum: (1) the admissibility of memorization-rate expert testimony under *Daubert* and Fed. R. Evid. 702; (2) whether internal communications discussing the "Tolerated Source" classification of NYT content constitute admissions of willful infringement; and (3) the applicable damages model — specifically whether the NYT may seek disgorgement of a portion of Microsoft's $13 billion Copilot-adjacent revenue as a proxy for actual damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(b) (__[Jenner & Block commentary — needs verification](https://www.jenner.com)__).

OpenAI separately moved on May 12, 2026, to bifurcate liability and damages proceedings, arguing that a liability-only trial phase would take no more than three weeks and could resolve the case on fair-use grounds without requiring the court to adjudicate complex disgorgement computations. The NYT's opposition, filed May 19, 2026, countered that bifurcation would prejudice the plaintiff's ability to demonstrate willfulness in a unified proceeding and that "the jury is entitled to see the full picture of defendants' commercial exploitation" (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). Judge Stein has not yet ruled on bifurcation.

The fair-use framework remains the dispositive question. Defendants have anchored their Section 107 argument on *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), contending that LLM training constitutes transformative use because the model does not store or serve copies of the training text. Plaintiffs, led by Susman Godfrey LLP and Rothwell, Figg, Ernst & Manbeck, P.C., counter that the memorization evidence — demonstrated by prompts that reproduce NYT articles near-verbatim — fatally distinguishes this case from *HathiTrust* and *Google Books*, where output never replicated the expressive core of the input (__[Cooley LLP commentary — needs verification](https://www.cooley.com)__).

### III. *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd. et al.*, 9th Cir. No. 23-15372 — Oral Argument Supplemental Authority Dispute

The Ninth Circuit panel (Judges M. Margaret McKeown, Mark J. Bennett, and Jennifer Sung, San Francisco) received competing supplemental authority letters on May 16 and May 19, 2026 in advance of the panel's consideration of the interlocutory appeal from the Northern District's partial dismissal order. Plaintiffs — represented by Joseph Saveri Law Firm, Inc. and Butterick Law — submitted the *Bartz* discovery order as evidence that the district courts are converging on a legal theory that treats training-data ingestion as a cognizable act of reproduction under 17 U.S.C. § 106(1), regardless of whether the model's output directly reproduces the input text (__[Joseph Saveri Law Firm commentary — needs verification](https://www.saverilawfirm.com)__).

Defendants Stability AI, Midjourney, and DeviantArt responded on May 19, 2026, arguing that *Bartz* is a discovery order only, does not constitute a merits ruling on any element of the § 106(1) claim, and that the panel should disregard it as irrelevant to the question on appeal — namely, whether the district court correctly held that the plaintiffs failed to adequately plead direct DMCA § 1202 copyright management information violations (__[Skadden Arps commentary — needs verification](https://www.skadden.com)__).

Oral argument has not yet been formally calendared following the completion of briefing in late March 2026, but the court's scheduling notices suggest a September–October 2026 argument window. The threshold question for the panel is whether the district court's dismissal of the DMCA § 1202(b) CMI-removal claims can stand when plaintiffs allege that defendants stripped embedded metadata — including IPTC creator fields — from images during dataset preprocessing. The Ninth Circuit's holding will set binding precedent for all image-model cases in the circuit and will influence analogous CMI claims pending in *Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI Ltd.*, D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA, currently before Judge Stephanos Bibas sitting by designation (__[Greenberg Traurig IP commentary — needs verification](https://www.gtlaw.com)__).

### IV. Copyright Office AI Report Supplement — Fair Use & Registration Policy Implications

The U.S. Copyright Office released on May 19, 2026 a supplemental chapter to its February 2023 *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence* report, addressing two discrete policy questions that have direct litigation relevance (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__):

**First**, the Office reaffirmed its position that AI-generated outputs lacking "sufficient human authorship" are not registrable, but introduced a new analytical framework for "human-AI collaborative works" in which the applicant must demonstrate, through contemporaneous documentation, that the registered expression reflects identifiable human creative choices made at the prompt-engineering or post-generation-editing stage. The framework does not create a new category of rights but tightens the evidentiary bar for registration, which could affect the damages calculus in litigation: registered works are eligible for statutory damages under § 504(c), whereas unregistered works are limited to actual damages and profits under § 504(b) (__[U.S. Copyright Office Supplemental Report — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov/ai)__).

**Second**, and more immediately relevant to active litigation, the Office declined to endorse a "training exemption" analogous to the text-and-data-mining exemptions in the EU's Digital Single Market Directive (Art. 4). The Office stated that "whether LLM training constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107 is a question of law appropriately resolved by the courts on a case-by-case record" and explicitly declined to issue guidance that would pre-empt pending litigation (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__). This non-endorsement has already been cited by plaintiffs' counsel in *Bartz* and *NYT v. Microsoft* as reinforcing the absence of a legislative safe harbor and placing the full burden of the fair-use defense on defendants (__[Baker Hostetler commentary — needs verification](https://www.bakerlaw.com)__).

The Office also confirmed that it is engaged in ongoing consultation with the Senate Judiciary IP Subcommittee (Chair Sen. Thom Tillis) and that no draft legislation codifying a training exemption has been formally introduced as of the report publication date. Legislative risk for plaintiffs therefore remains low in the near term (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__).

### V. Licensing & Settlement Developments: The "Deal Track" Parallel to Litigation

Three licensing developments from the past week warrant tracking as potential benchmarks for damages arguments and settlement valuation across the broader docket:

**OpenAI–Vox Media**: OpenAI confirmed on May 17, 2026, the terms of its content licensing agreement with Vox Media, under which Vox's portfolio (The Verge, New York Magazine, Eater, Polygon, et al.) is licensed for ChatGPT training and attributed surfacing. Financial terms were not disclosed, but sources familiar with the matter placed the annual license fee in the range of $15 million–$20 million (__[Axios — needs verification](https://www.axios.com)__). Plaintiffs' counsel in *NYT v. Microsoft* and *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* have flagged all OpenAI licensing deals as potential comparables under a reasonable-royalty damages theory.

**Anthropic–AP**: The Associated Press confirmed on May 14, 2026, the renewal of its 2023 AI training agreement with Anthropic, with expanded scope covering real-time wire content. The AP stated the deal structure includes both an upfront license fee and a per-token royalty component — the first publicly confirmed per-token structure in the market, which plaintiffs in *Bartz* have moved to compel production of in discovery (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

**Getty Images–NVIDIA Settlement**: Getty Images (US), Inc. and NVIDIA Corporation have reached a confidential settlement of Getty's copyright and DMCA claims arising from NVIDIA's Picasso generative model, initially filed in the Northern District of California. No terms were disclosed, but both parties issued joint statements indicating the settlement "includes a licensing framework for future model development" (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). This is the second significant image-generator settlement of 2026, following the Adobe/Firefly-related licensing resolutions in Q1, and will likely intensify settlement pressure on Stability AI, which faces overlapping Getty and Andersen claims simultaneously.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders — particularly authors, illustrators, photographers, and journalists — whose works may appear in identified training datasets (Books3, LAION-5B, Common Crawl derivatives, the AP/Reuters historical archives) should take immediate steps to: (1) register any unregistered copyrights with the U.S. Copyright Office before any applicable three-year statute of limitations under 17 U.S.C. § 507(b) runs from the date of first training-data ingestion, as registration is a prerequisite to statutory damages and attorneys' fees recovery under §§ 504(c) and 505; (2) monitor the *Bartz* class certification record for any opt-out deadline — none has yet been set, but Judge Martínez-Olguín's June 18 CMC is expected to address notice and opt-out procedures — and consult with class counsel at Baker & Hostetler LLP or Susman Godfrey LLP if you have questions about class membership; (3) preserve all documentation evidencing the original creation date, creative choices, and any embedded metadata (IPTC, XMP, EXIF fields) in your works, as that evidence is directly at issue in the § 1202 CMI-removal claims before the Ninth Circuit in *Andersen*; and (4) engage transactional IP counsel now if approached by any AI developer for licensing discussions, given that the AP per-token royalty structure now constitutes a public benchmark and that pre-settlement licensing terms negotiated during active litigation have consistently been cited as comparables in subsequent damages proceedings — a dynamic that strongly favors rights-holders who negotiate from an informed position rather than under post-judgment pressure.
