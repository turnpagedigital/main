### 1. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* — Scheduling Order & "Expressive Selection" Hearing (N.D. Cal. No. 5:23-cv-03223-AMO)

Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín issued a scheduling order on June 6, 2026, in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:23-cv-03223-AMO (N.D. Cal.), extending the fact-discovery cutoff from July 18 to **September 19, 2026** and resetting the class-certification motion deadline to **November 7, 2026** (__[Bartz Docket — PACER/CourtListener — needs verification](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67648326/)__). The order also schedules a **two-day evidentiary hearing for October 22–23, 2026** on plaintiffs' "expressive selection" theory — the argument, grounded in *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), that the curated assembly of text corpora used to train Claude constitutes independently copyrightable selection and arrangement that Anthropic reproduced without license.

Plaintiffs' lead counsel at **Baker & Hostetler** (partners Kevin Attridge and Roy Zwierlein) and **Susman Godfrey** (partner Davida Brook) have taken the position that Anthropic's ingestion of the "Books3" dataset — estimated at approximately 196,000 titles and roughly 37 GB of text — constitutes verbatim reproduction under 17 U.S.C. § 106(1), with the weight-embedding step providing no transformation cognizable under the *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), *purpose-and-character* prong (__[Baker & Hostetler AI Copyright Practice](https://www.bakerlaw.com/services/intellectual-property/copyright/ai-copyright/)__). Anthropic's motion for summary judgment on fair use — filed April 3, 2026 — argues that (i) the training process is "intermediate copying" under *Sega Enterprises Ltd. v. Accolade, Inc.*, 977 F.2d 1510 (9th Cir. 1992) and its progeny; (ii) no market substitution occurs because Claude does not reproduce training text in outputs; and (iii) the "transformative use" analysis under *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023) — which cabined transformation to *purpose* rather than *character* — actually supports Anthropic because model training serves a completely distinct communicative purpose from the original works (__[Susman Godfrey Bartz Case Page — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com/cases/bartz-v-anthropic/)__).

The October hearing will also address whether plaintiffs' experts can opine on "memorization rates" — specifically a disputed methodology that purports to show Claude reproduces verbatim passages from training texts at a rate of 3.1% of sampled prompts — a figure Anthropic's Daubert motion (filed May 22, 2026, Dkt. 189) characterizes as methodologically unsound because the prompts were adversarially engineered to elicit reproduction (__[Anthropic Daubert Motion — needs verification](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67648326/)__).

**Practical weight:** The scheduling extension signals Judge Martínez-Olguín's view that the fair-use and copyrightability questions are not ripe for summary disposition without a more complete expert record. Rights-holders with works in the Books3 dataset should treat the October hearing as the critical inflection point for the merits trajectory of this case.

### 2. *The New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp. & OpenAI* — Discovery Protective Order Dispute (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS)

At the June 4, 2026 status conference before Judge Sidney H. Stein in the Southern District of New York, Microsoft's motion for a protective order (Dkt. 312) over a category of internal documents labeled "training-data provenance reports" drew sustained skepticism (__[NYT v. Microsoft Docket — CourtListener — needs verification](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/30244685/)__). NYT counsel at **Latham & Watkins** argued that these reports — compiled by Microsoft's AI engineering teams between 2020 and 2023 — directly address whether GPT-4's training corpus incorporated licensed NYT content and, if so, in what volumes and proportions. Microsoft, represented by **Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan**, invoked the work-product doctrine and attorney-client privilege, contending that the reports were prepared at counsel's direction in anticipation of litigation following OpenAI's December 2022 ChatGPT launch.

Judge Stein ordered Microsoft to submit the documents for **in camera review by June 20, 2026**, and noted from the bench that "provenance documentation prepared by engineers, even if later reviewed by counsel, does not acquire work-product protection retroactively" — language that signals the court is inclined to compel production of at least a subset (__[NYT v. Microsoft Status Conference Transcript — needs verification](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/30244685/)__). If produced, these documents could provide a factual foundation for the NYT's damages theory: that OpenAI and Microsoft commercially benefited from the unauthorized use of approximately **1,800 articles** allegedly reproduced nearly verbatim by GPT-4 in response to user prompts, and that statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) — up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement — could yield a liability exposure in the range of **$270 million** at the per-work ceiling, before trebling arguments (__[NYT Complaint ¶¶ 94–118 — CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/30244685/)__).

OpenAI's parallel motion to bifurcate liability and damages discovery (Dkt. 318) remains sub judice; Judge Stein indicated he will rule on both pending motions by **July 11, 2026**.

### 3. *Andersen v. Stability AI* — Ninth Circuit En Banc Petition (9th Cir. No. 23-15372)

The Ninth Circuit's three-judge panel affirmed in part and reversed in part the N.D. Cal. dismissal order in *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.*, Case No. 23-15372, in a January 2026 opinion that reinstated plaintiffs' direct infringement and DMCA § 1202 claims while affirming dismissal of the vicarious liability count against DeviantArt (__[Andersen v. Stability AI — 9th Cir. Opinion — needs verification](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov/opinions/)__). Stability AI filed a petition for en banc rehearing on March 14, 2026, arguing that the panel's interpretation of 17 U.S.C. § 1202(b)(3) — which prohibits distributing works knowing that copyright management information has been "removed or altered" — creates a circuit split with the Eleventh Circuit's narrower reading in *Mango v. BuzzFeed*, and that the panel failed to apply the *Warhol* commercial-purpose framework to image-diffusion model outputs (__[Stability AI En Banc Petition — needs verification](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov/)__).

The court called for a response from plaintiffs (represented by **Joseph Saveri Law Firm** and **Loevy & Loevy**) due **June 13, 2026** — a response that has now been fully briefed as of the date of this advisory. The en banc call-for-response is a significant procedural signal: the Ninth Circuit only requests responses in roughly 15% of en banc petitions it ultimately denies, making the June 13 response a meaningful data point for whether the full court views the § 1202(b) holding as circuit-split-worthy (__[9th Cir. En Banc Statistics — Federal Judicial Center — needs verification](https://www.fjc.gov/)__). If en banc is granted, the case returns to an eleven-judge panel, likely delaying a merits remand to Judge William Orrick in the N.D. Cal. by 12–18 months.

**Strategic note for rights-holders:** The § 1202(b)(3) claim is the stealthiest cause of action in the AI-training copyright docket. It does not require proof of copying a specific work — only that the defendant distributed AI outputs knowing that copyright management information (author name, title, copyright notice embedded in training-file metadata) had been stripped. If the panel opinion stands, it creates a statutory-damages pipeline that is structurally independent of the fair-use defense.

### 4. Copyright Office Supplemental NOI on AI Authorship & Implications for Pending Litigation

On June 5, 2026, the U.S. Copyright Office published a **Supplemental Notice of Inquiry** ("SNOI") in the Federal Register, 91 Fed. Reg. [TK] (June 5, 2026), narrowing the scope of its March 2025 "AI and Copyright" report's proposed framework for human-authorship thresholds in AI-assisted works (__[Copyright Office SNOI — Federal Register — needs verification](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/search#query=AI+copyright+authorship)__). The SNOI's key move: it draws a sharper line between (a) works where a human author uses AI as a "tool" (registrable, provided human creative expression is discernible), and (b) works where the AI system "determines the expressive elements" without sufficient human creative control (not registrable). The Office explicitly states it is **not** taking a position on whether AI training on copyrighted works constitutes infringement — that question it defers to the courts — but the definitional scaffolding it is erecting around "human authorship" has downstream significance for the damages calculus in pending cases.

Specifically, plaintiffs in *Authors Guild v. OpenAI*, S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-08292 (J. Colleen McMahon), have argued that outputs generated by large language models that closely paraphrase plaintiffs' registered works create derivative works under 17 U.S.C. § 101, entitling plaintiffs to profits attributable to the infringing component under § 504(b) (__[Authors Guild v. OpenAI Complaint — CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67630757/)__). The SNOI implicitly supports OpenAI's counter-position that AI outputs are not "authored" and therefore cannot be "derivative works" in the traditional copyright sense — a line of argument that, if accepted by the court, would significantly limit the § 504(b) profits-disgorgement theory while leaving § 504(c) statutory damages intact only for the *training-stage* copying. Judge McMahon has a scheduling conference set for **June 18, 2026**, at which the parties are expected to address how the SNOI affects the case management order.

In the Middle District of Tennessee, *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic*, No. 3:23-cv-01092-TRM-JEM (J. Thomas A. Varlan), the SNOI is likely to be cited in Anthropic's forthcoming reply brief on its fair-use summary judgment motion (due June 24, 2026) as support for the proposition that Claude's outputs do not constitute derivative works of the musical compositions in the training data, thereby eliminating a separate infringement theory plaintiffs have preserved as a fallback (__[Concord v. Anthropic Docket — CourtListener — needs verification](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67862745/)__).

### 5. Licensing Developments: OpenAI-News Corp Deal as Market Harm Benchmark

The OpenAI–News Corp licensing agreement, reported to cover a **$250 million, five-year term** for access to the Wall Street Journal, New York Post, Dow Jones Newswires, and Barron's archives, continues to function as the primary real-world market-rate benchmark in the ongoing fair-use litigation (__[OpenAI–News Corp Deal — Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/)__). Plaintiffs in *The New York Times v. Microsoft* have cited the News Corp deal in their opposition to Microsoft's motion to dismiss the market-harm prong of the fair-use analysis, arguing that it demonstrates a "sufficiently established licensing market" under *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539, 566 (1985) — the fourth *Campbell* factor. Microsoft and OpenAI counter that the News Corp deal reflects prospective commercial arrangements, not compensation for past training, and that no licensing market existed at the time of the alleged infringement.

The OpenAI–Associated Press deal (reportedly $7.5 million for a three-year term), the OpenAI–Axel Springer deal (reported at approximately **$100 million** over multiple years), and the Anthropic–Google licensing relationship (terms undisclosed) are all in the record across various cases as comparables (__[OpenAI–AP Deal — AP — needs verification](https://apnews.com/)__) (__[OpenAI–Axel Springer — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com/)__). Taken together, plaintiffs argue these deals establish a per-publication licensing rate floor that would, if applied to the full Books3 dataset, generate an aggregate damages exposure that dwarfs any statutory damages calculation.

Rights-holders whose works appear in the Books3, Common Crawl, or Pile datasets should coordinate with plaintiffs' counsel on the "memorization rate" expert methodology before the October 22–23 evidentiary hearing in *Bartz* — the outcome of the Daubert skirmish will determine whether percentage-of-reproduction testimony survives to trial and whether class certification is viable on that theory. Counsel monitoring the § 1202(b)(3) pipeline in *Andersen* should preserve documentary evidence of copyright management information embedded in registered works, as that is the evidentiary predicate for the claim that is entirely independent of the fair-use defense posture. Finally, licensing counsel should document any market-rate benchmarks against the News Corp and Axel Springer figures now — courts across multiple districts are treating these deals as the closest available "willing licensor/willing licensee" data points under the Harper & Row fourth-factor framework, and establishing contemporaneous market-rate evidence strengthens both the damages case and the market-harm prong of fair-use opposition.
