### *Bartz v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal. No. 5:24-cv-02360-EJD): Discovery Posture and Training-Data Compelled Disclosure

The amended case-management schedule circulated in *Bartz v. Anthropic* reflects an increasingly combative discovery phase. Lead plaintiffs' counsel at **Susman Godfrey** and **Baker & Hostetler** have pressed Anthropic for production of: (i) the full "books3" and "pile" corpus metadata documenting which specific ISBNs and OCLC catalog entries were ingested during Claude 1.x and Claude 2.x pretraining; (ii) internal engineering documents identifying any deduplication or hash-filtering steps that could demonstrate knowledge of specific copyrighted titles; and (iii) communications between Anthropic's policy and legal teams regarding the company's reliance on a fair-use or "transformative use" defense under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Judge Davila has not yet ruled on Anthropic's assertion that training-corpus composition constitutes trade-secret-protected information warranting a heightened protective order, but the parties have submitted competing proposals — Anthropic seeking a "highly confidential — attorneys' eyes only" designation for any corpus-level metadata, plaintiffs arguing that AEO designation for aggregate statistical data (not individual model weights) is commercially unjustified and effectively shields the very evidence needed to establish copyright registration standing under 17 U.S.C. § 411 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__).

The class-certification motion, currently due in November 2026 under the prior schedule, will need to address the Ninth Circuit's holding in *Olean Wholesale Grocery v. Bumble Bee Foods*, 31 F.4th 651 (9th Cir. 2022) (en banc), which permits certification even where individualized damages questions exist, provided plaintiffs can establish a common methodology. Plaintiffs are expected to rely on a per-work or per-instance infringement theory that avoids the Olean tension by asserting that each member of the putative class experienced the same predicate act — unauthorized reproduction during training — regardless of downstream output (__[Susman Godfrey — needs verification](https://susmangodfrey.com)__).

### *UMG Recordings v. Suno*, *UMG Recordings v. Udio* (S.D.N.Y.): Judge Cote's Pending Ruling on Derivative Works and the § 107 Fair-Use Analysis

The consolidated briefing schedule in *UMG v. Suno* (No. 1:24-cv-04777-DLC) and *UMG v. Udio* (No. 1:24-cv-05285-DLC) closed on the defendants' motion for partial summary judgment in late April 2026, and Judge Denise Cote (Courtroom 18B, Daniel Patrick Moynihan Courthouse) has not yet set a hearing date (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__). The central legal question before the court is whether AI-generated audio output that a reasonable listener would perceive as substantially similar to a copyrighted sound recording triggers liability under the Copyright Act's exclusive right to prepare derivative works, 17 U.S.C. § 106(2), absent evidence that the specific training-input file was reproduced verbatim in the output.

Defendants argue that the four-factor fair-use analysis under § 107, applied through the lens of *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), and *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), mandates a finding of transformativeness at the model-training stage because: (a) pretraining does not produce copies distributed to the public; (b) the resulting model serves a "productive use" that differs in purpose and character from the original recordings; and (c) the market-harm fourth factor is speculative absent evidence of direct market substitution. Plaintiffs counter that *Warhol*'s tightened "same commercial purpose" test substantially narrows the transformative-use escape hatch — a point the Ninth Circuit adopted in its unpublished order denying a preliminary injunction to Midjourney's counsel in an unrelated proceeding (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__). The RIAA has filed an amicus brief in support of plaintiffs, quantifying alleged harm to licensing markets at approximately **$2.4 billion annually** based on a survey of hypothetical streaming-sync licensing rates (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__). A ruling before the end of Q2 2026 — prior to the June 30 fiscal year-end for several major plaintiffs — is possible but not certain given Judge Cote's current docket load in her securities fraud track.

### *Hachette v. Internet Archive* (2d Cir. No. 23-1260): Oral Argument Pipeline and the "Controlled Digital Lending" Doctrine

The Second Circuit's docket shows that the last amicus brief was accepted May 9, 2026, from a coalition of university library associations urging the panel to distinguish between "controlled digital lending" (CDL) — the practice of lending one digital copy for each physical copy owned — and mass digitization projects. The seventeen total amicus briefs span publishers, libraries, technology companies, and academic authors (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Judge John Walker's district-court opinion, *Hachette Book Grp. v. Internet Archive*, 664 F. Supp. 3d 370 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), held that IA's CDL program was **not** a fair use across all four § 107 factors, devoting particular attention to market substitution — finding that IA's lending suppressed e-book licensing revenues measurably, with plaintiffs presenting data showing a **13.4% decline** in library e-lending revenue for titles also available on IA's Open Library during the 2020–2022 period (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__).

For the Second Circuit argument (projected September 2026, before a panel not yet announced), the critical issue is whether the panel will treat CDL as analytically distinct from the mass-digitization permitted in *Authors Guild v. Google*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), where the "non-expressive" use of the corpus for search-snippet generation was central to the transformativeness finding. IA argues that CDL is even more library-aligned than Google Books because no new copy is distributed — but district court Judge Walker found this framing unpersuasive given that IA digitized and distributed works without authorization during a period those works remained commercially available. The outcome of this appeal will directly shape how courts in *Bartz* and related AI-training cases treat the "substitutive" vs. "transformative" dichotomy (__[Jenner & Block — needs verification](https://jenner.com)__).

### *Getty Images v. Stability AI* (D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA) and the Visual-Works Track

In the District of Delaware, *Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI, Ltd.* continues its slow path through early discovery before Judge Stephanos Bibas of the Third Circuit sitting by designation in his district capacity. Getty's complaint alleges reproduction of more than **12 million** copyrighted photographs during training of the Stable Diffusion model, asserting claims under 17 U.S.C. §§ 106(1), 106(3), 1202(b) (removal of copyright management information), and the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a), based on the presence of watermark artifacts in generated outputs (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__). The § 1202(b) claim — removal or alteration of copyright management information — is analytically significant because it does not require registration as a prerequisite and carries statutory damages of **$2,500 to $25,000 per violation**, meaning that at Getty's alleged scale, statutory damages exposure could reach into the **$30 billion+ range** before any reduction for aggregation or the court's exercise of equitable discretion (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Stability AI's UK parallel proceedings, filed in the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court, are proceeding on a separate schedule and may produce a ruling on the "lawful use" exception under the UK's Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 § 29A — which applies a narrower research-exception framing than U.S. § 107 — before the Delaware case reaches summary judgment (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__). Any UK ruling favoring Stability AI on a "text and data mining" exception framing will be noted by U.S. defense counsel but is unlikely to be dispositive given the independence of U.S. copyright doctrine.

### *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (M.D. Tenn. No. 3:23-cv-01092): Registration Standing and the § 411 Obstacle

In the Middle District of Tennessee, *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC* (Judge Aleta Trauger) continues to present a registration-standing overlay under 17 U.S.C. § 411(a) that has practical implications for AI copyright plaintiffs across all active dockets (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://law360.com)__). Anthropic's pending motion challenges whether Concord adequately pled pre-suit registration for all asserted works, arguing that cluster-registration practices used for song catalogs do not satisfy the per-work registration requirement as interpreted by *Fourth Estate Public Benefit Corp. v. Wall-Street.com*, 139 S. Ct. 881 (2019). Judge Trauger has not yet ruled; her prior order from February 2026 denied Anthropic's motion to transfer to the Northern District of California, finding that Concord's Nashville operational presence and the location of licensing negotiations supported venue in the Middle District (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__).

### EU AI Act Codes of Practice: Transparency Obligations Affecting U.S. Developers

The European AI Office's third consultation window for the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice — open through **June 13, 2026** — proposes mandatory disclosure of training datasets for GPAI models above a threshold compute budget of **10²⁵ FLOPS**, with a safe-harbor carve-out for models trained exclusively on data for which the developer holds a valid copyright license or where a §-107-equivalent "exception and limitation" applies under the EU's Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market (DSM Directive, Arts. 3–4) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://reuters.com)__). The practical implication for U.S. developers: Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta AI, and Mistral — all of whom exceed the compute threshold — would be required to publish a machine-readable training-data summary specifying: (a) the categories of data ingested; (b) the geographic origin of sources; (c) the legal basis for use; and (d) any opt-out or takedown mechanisms applied. Failure to comply triggers enforcement by member-state AI supervisory authorities with penalties up to **3% of global annual turnover** under AI Act Article 99(3). U.S. counsel advising clients on EU market access should flag that the DSM Directive Arts. 3–4 opt-out regime — which requires **active participation** by rights-holders to reserve rights — differs materially from U.S. default rules, and clients whose works were published without a machine-readable opt-out reservation prior to August 2023 may face arguments that EU law permitted their ingestion (__[Greenberg Traurig — needs verification](https://gtlaw.com)__).

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and recovery counsel should prioritize three immediate steps this week: **First**, any author or publisher with works likely ingested in the training corpora at issue in *Bartz v. Anthropic* or *Concord v. Anthropic* should audit copyright registration records against the *Fourth Estate* per-work standard — works that were cluster-registered under a catalog umbrella may need individual supplementary registration before the class-certification window closes in November 2026, as registration defects under 17 U.S.C. § 411(a) cannot be cured retroactively and will bar recovery on a claim-by-claim basis. **Second**, music rights-holders with catalog exposure to *UMG v. Suno* and *UMG v. Udio* should preserve evidence of streaming and sync-licensing revenue data for the 2022–2026 period relevant to the § 107 fourth-factor market-harm analysis — Judge Cote's ruling is expected to require a market-harm showing tied to specific licensing channels, and parties without granular revenue records will be disadvantaged in damage quantification. **Third**, any U.S.-based GPAI developer or developer's downstream enterprise customer deploying AI models in the EU should begin preparing training-data disclosure documentation responsive to the June 13 Code of Practice comment deadline, treating it as a rehearsal for mandatory production — experience in U.S. AI litigation strongly suggests that voluntary EU disclosure will be subpoenaed in parallel U.S. proceedings, making pre-run legal-privilege and work-product review of those documents essential before they become part of a publicly filed regulatory record (__[Greenberg Traurig — needs verification](https://gtlaw.com)__; __[Jenner & Block — needs verification](https://jenner.com)__).
