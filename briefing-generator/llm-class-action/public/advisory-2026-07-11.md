### I. *NYT v. OpenAI* — Sanctions Motion Targets Project Giraffe, 78-Million-Log Database, and Alleged Spoliation


The New York Times and other news organizations suing OpenAI for copyright infringement asked a New York federal judge on July 9 to sanction the company, accusing it of deleting ChatGPT conversation logs and concealing for two years that it possessed tools to search for plaintiffs' content in training data and ChatGPT outputs.


The factual record underlying the motion is specific and worth unpacking precisely. 
In an April court-ordered deposition, OpenAI data privacy engineer Vinnie Monaco allegedly revealed that OpenAI had already conducted internal searches and evaluations of its training corpus to search for copyrighted journalism works — and that, beginning before the NYT filed its lawsuit, OpenAI had already amassed a database of approximately 78 million de-identified ChatGPT conversations it was using internally to determine how much it was infringing on others' works.
 
On top of that dataset, OpenAI also allegedly implemented a "Bloom" filter as part of a set of tools called "Project Giraffe," which detected and kept a record of regurgitation in outputs, shortly after the lawsuit was filed.


The discovery posture that makes this significant: 
the plaintiffs had originally asked OpenAI to provide a sample of 120 million chat logs, but OpenAI had negotiated to bring the sample down to just 20 million.
 
OpenAI negotiated that down to 20 million, then submitted a version in December so heavily redacted that the court called it unusable.
 
The newspapers also allege that OpenAI deleted billions of ChatGPT conversations after a court preservation order took effect.


The remedies sought are surgical and adversarially calibrated. 
Plaintiffs are asking the court to prevent OpenAI from using the 20-million-log sample as evidence, claiming it is unreliable; to accept as fact that ChatGPT logs would have shown major regurgitation and grounding of the plaintiffs' content; to prevent OpenAI from arguing that its provided chat logs don't demonstrate substantial regurgitation; and to make OpenAI pay legal fees for having to chase down this evidence.
 
The Times and other publishers asked for legal sanctions against OpenAI, alleging it violated rules set down by the court and acted in "bad faith" during the discovery phase of litigation.


The structural significance is this: if Judge Stein or Magistrate Judge Wang enters an adverse-inference instruction under Rule 37(e) — deeming the destroyed or withheld logs to have shown systematic reproduction of plaintiffs' journalism — OpenAI would be defending at summary judgment or trial on a record that presumptively establishes the § 507 infringement element. That shifts the entire litigation to whether fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107 operates as a complete defense, and it makes the § 504(c) statutory-damages exposure — potentially up to $150,000 per registered work per infringement for willful copying — a live front rather than a theoretical ceiling. 
Ian Crosby, the Times' lead attorney at Susman Godfrey, said OpenAI concealed what it knew for more than two years, and that resolving discovery this way would settle the case's most contested technical question without a trial.


OpenAI's denial is on the record. 
OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri denied the allegations, accusing the Times of trying to access private user conversations as its case weakens.
 That defense tracks OpenAI's prior position before Judge Stein. 
Throughout the case, OpenAI had argued that it lacked the ability to search its own training corpus, and also argued that searching or producing its massive collection of ChatGPT conversations would be technically burdensome and would raise user-privacy concerns because the logs would need to be retrieved, processed, and de-identified.
 Judge Stein rejected that framing on January 5, 2026. 
Judge Stein affirmed a magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20-million-log sample, not just the cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' works that OpenAI wanted to hand over.
 The Monaco deposition now suggests the earlier "inability" representations may themselves have been false — which is the spoliation hook.

The MDL consolidation matters procedurally. 
The discovery dispute arose in *In re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation* (MDL, S.D.N.Y.), a consolidated action that combines 16 copyright lawsuits.
 
The MDL docket (CourtListener) was last updated July 10, 2026, and is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein with referral to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, docket 1:25-md-03143.
 The sanctions motion was filed into the underlying *NYT* case, No. 1:23-cv-11195, 
assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein, referred to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, last docket update July 9, 2026.
 Response deadline and hearing date are not yet publicly confirmed in the sources reviewed this run.

One additional data point on litigation economics: 
the Times has already spent more than $28 million on fighting AI companies in court, according to filings with financial regulators.
 
That includes $4.2 million in this year's first quarter alone.
 That burn rate — and the fact that OpenAI is preparing for a public listing at valuations approaching $1 trillion — frames the settlement incentives on both sides.

### II. *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Final Approval Still Under Submission; Distribution Clock Paused


The final fairness hearing was held May 14, 2026 before Judge Martínez-Olguín, who heard class counsel and objectors and took the matter under submission; as of May 18, 2026 the final approval order had not yet been entered.
 The tracked-case ground truth confirms the last CourtListener filing date as July 1, 2026, with zero new entries in the prior 72 hours as of the July 10 sync. No final approval order has been confirmed this run.

The quantitative record from the fairness hearing is precise. 
Lead attorney Justin Nelson reported at the hearing that the opt-in rate had reached 92.77%, and there was no indication that if the settlement is approved, the per-work payout would change from approximately $3,000 to $3,100.
 
Class counsel filed papers confirming that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% rate.
 For context: the typical class action claim rate runs around 10%; the Bartz claim rate is roughly nine times higher, which directly compresses per-work recovery toward the $3,100 floor rather than any higher-end projection.


Authors urged the California federal judge to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 The fee reduction — from the originally requested $375 million (25% of fund) — followed judicial pressure at the May 14 hearing focused specifically on the fee multiplier and cost-reserve structure.


Following the fairness hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered that by no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic may file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 
Plaintiffs were also ordered to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.


The installment funding schedule governs when claimants actually receive payment. 
Anthropic is funding the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The first $300 million tranche has been deposited in interest-bearing escrow. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved; payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest.
 Any Ninth Circuit objector appeal would extend that timeline by 12 to 18 months.

On the scope limitation that continues to be misread in secondary coverage: 
in June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled on summary judgment that using books without permission to train AI was fair use if they were acquired legally, but denied Anthropic's request for summary judgment related to piracy — finding that the piracy was not fair use.
 The $1.5 billion settlement resolves only the piracy-track class — books Anthropic downloaded from LibGen and PiLiMi. 
For Anthropic, the settlement releases them from liability for past conduct in exchange for the $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies; it does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme and does not bind any other AI developer or any future case.

### III. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* — Cross-MSJ Pending; Docket Sync Awaiting

The tracked-case ground truth records *Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC* as status "Cross-MSJ pending · awaiting first docket sync" — the matter was transferred from the Middle District of Tennessee to the N.D. Cal. on June 26, 2024. 
UMG, Concord Music, and several other major music companies sued Anthropic on October 18, 2023 in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee, asserting that Anthropic is infringing their music lyric copyrights on a massive scale by scraping the web to train its AI without licensing, consent, or payment.
 
The case was transferred to the Northern District of California on June 26, 2024; the judge assigned is Judge Eumi K. [Lee — confirmed in tracked-case ground truth as the assigned N.D. Cal. judge].


The last known public filing per the ground truth is Anthropic's April 22, 2026 cross-motion for summary judgment on fair use. The music-publisher theory is analytically distinct from the book-piracy theory in Bartz: Concord alleges lyric reproduction at inference time — verbatim output of registered song lyrics in response to user queries — rather than shadow-library ingestion. Under § 107(4), the market-harm factor therefore turns on whether Claude's lyric reproduction substitutes for licensed lyric-display services (Genius, Musixmatch, ASCAP/BMI licensing windows). If the court denies both cross-motions, the case proceeds to a trial squarely testing whether output-stage reproduction triggers § 504(c) statutory damages independent of training-stage fair use. No docket sync has yet been completed for this matter; the analysis above is based on the tracked-case ground truth and publicly confirmed transfer order.

### IV. Parallel Docket Watch — *In re OpenAI MDL*, *Authors Guild v. OpenAI*, and *Thomson Reuters v. Ross*

**In re OpenAI MDL (1:25-md-03143-SHS, S.D.N.Y.):** 
The MDL docket was last updated July 10, 2026, assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein with referral to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang.
 
Expert reports were due November 14, 2025; summary judgment briefing closes April 2, 2026 per the scheduling order.
 Summary judgment papers are therefore fully submitted. The sanctions motion filed July 9 into the underlying NYT case flows through the same judicial pair managing the MDL, which means Judge Wang's resolution of the spoliation dispute will have direct discovery consequences for the consolidated authors' actions as well.

On the March 26, 2025 motion-to-dismiss ruling in the MDL: 
Judge Stein held that direct and contributory copyright infringement claims survive; DMCA § 1202 claims were dismissed with leave to amend; the common-law unfair competition claim was dismissed; and OpenAI's statute-of-limitations defense for 2019–2020 training was rejected.
 That ruling — particularly the survival of direct infringement and the limitations-period holding — positions the case for a summary judgment ruling that will test whether the § 107 fair-use defense operates as a matter of law across a journalism-specific training corpus.

**Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (D. Del., Feb. 2025):** The negative fair-use precedent continues to matter for framing. 
A Delaware federal court rejected fair use where the defendant used copyrighted legal headnotes to build a direct competitor to Westlaw, finding the use substitutive rather than transformative.
 That holding — distinguishing Google Books on the ground that the use directly competed with the plaintiff's product — is the template plaintiffs' counsel in NYT will deploy at summary judgment to argue ChatGPT's journalism outputs substitute for, rather than transform, the Times's content.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and claims investors tracking the Bartz distribution should register the critical path: final approval order from Judge Martínez-Olguín has not yet issued as of this run, and no payment — from any of Anthropic's four installment tranches — flows to claimants until that order is entered and any Ninth Circuit appeal from the seven identified objectors is resolved or the appeal period expires without a filing; at the $3,100-per-work rate across 440,490 claimed works, net distributable after the revised $187.5 million fee award and administration costs will be well under $1.3 billion in aggregate, with the September 25, 2026 and September 25, 2027 installments not yet deposited. Counsel monitoring the NYT/OpenAI sanctions motion in No. 1:23-cv-11195 should track the response deadline and any referral order from Judge Stein to Magistrate Judge Wang — a Rule 37(e) adverse-inference order, if granted, would materially alter the settlement dynamics in that case and in the parallel MDL (1:25-md-03143) by effectively establishing output-stage reproduction as a stipulated fact; that development would also sharpen the § 107(4) market-harm analysis in *Concord Music v. Anthropic* by providing a cross-defendant evidentiary baseline on regurgitation rates. Parties considering opt-out strategies from the Bartz class should note Judge Martínez-Olguín's terse response to the Canadian publisher that attempted a side negotiation — "seek a sweeter deal than other class members" was expressly foreclosed — and factor in that ClaimsHero's opt-out solicitation campaign was publicly characterized by the court as an apparent "extortion" gambit; individual opt-out value is therefore unlikely to exceed the $3,100 per-work rate without a separate, well-documented infringement theory of greater magnitude than the shadow-library piracy claim already settled.
