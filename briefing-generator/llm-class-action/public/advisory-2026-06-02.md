### I. *Andersen v. Stability AI* — Partial Summary Judgment Clock Starts

Judge Orrick's May 30, 2026 scheduling order in *Andersen v. Stability AI*, No. 3:23-cv-00201-WHO, sets the following milestones: opening cross-motions due **September 9, 2026**; opposition briefs due **October 14, 2026**; reply briefs due **November 4, 2026**; hearing tentatively scheduled for **December 2, 2026** at 2:00 p.m. in Courtroom 2, 17th Floor, San Francisco. The order is limited to four discrete issues: (1) whether plaintiffs' works are sufficiently registered under 17 U.S.C. § 411(a) to anchor each sub-class; (2) whether Stability AI's LAION-5B ingestion qualifies as "reproduction" under § 106(1); (3) the threshold element of the § 1202(b) CMI-removal claim (scienter and the nexus to future infringement); and (4) whether the DMCA § 512(c) safe harbor is facially available to a model-training pipeline (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The CMI and § 512 questions are particularly consequential: if Orrick rules at summary judgment that § 1202(b) is inapplicable because LAION scraping does not constitute "distribution" of copies bearing removed CMI, plaintiffs lose their highest-multiple statutory-damages theory (up to $25,000 per work per violation under § 1203(c)(3)(B)), which class counsel had estimated could produce aggregate exposure north of $10 billion across the named defendants. The case is calendared before the same judge who denied Stability AI's initial motion to dismiss the direct-infringement claim in October 2023, a ruling that distinguished *Authors Guild v. Google*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), on the ground that Stable Diffusion's outputs are not facially transformative in the way a search-index snippet is (__[U.S. District Court — N.D. Cal. PACER](https://www.pacer.gov)__).

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* — Lyric-Reproduction Theory Survives Initial Pleading; Dismissal Motion Fully Briefed

With briefing now closed in *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic*, No. 3:23-cv-01092-BN (M.D. Tenn., Judge Waverly Crenshaw, Jr.), the case presents the cleanest "output infringement" theory in the current litigation wave: plaintiffs assert that Claude, when prompted to reproduce song lyrics, does so verbatim or near-verbatim, and that this reproduction is not shielded by fair use under the four-factor *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* analysis, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), because (a) the purpose is commercial, (b) song lyrics are among the most creative and expression-dense works protected by § 102(a), (c) reproduction is often complete, and (d) market harm to licensing revenue is direct and quantifiable (__[Concord Music Group v. Anthropic, M.D. Tenn. PACER — needs verification](https://www.pacer.gov)__). Anthropic's motion argues that any reproduction is de minimis and that Claude's primary purpose — language understanding and generation — is transformative at the training stage under *Authors Guild v. Google* and, for outputs, under an emergent "functional transformation" theory that several academics have advanced in *amicus* filings. Plaintiffs' 68-page opposition, filed May 30, 2026, leans heavily on the Second Circuit's recent *ReDigi* progeny and on *Hachette v. Internet Archive*, No. 20-cv-4160-JGK (S.D.N.Y. 2023), for the proposition that convenience and scale do not manufacture transformativeness. The music publisher coalition, which includes Universal Music Group's publishing arm and Sony Music Publishing in addition to Concord, has quantified claimed licensing revenue foregone at approximately $250 million annually across the industry based on lyric-sync and mechanicals benchmarks (__[RIAA amicus brief — needs verification](https://www.riaa.com)__). Judge Crenshaw has not yet set a hearing date; given his court's current civil docket density, a ruling before Q3 2026 would be aggressive.

### III. *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Discovery Extension; Class Certification Pushed to Q4 2026

The joint stipulation filed May 29, 2026 in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:23-cv-04780-RS (N.D. Cal., Judge Richard Seeborg, Courtroom 3, 17th Floor), extends fact discovery from the prior June 30, 2026 deadline to **August 15, 2026**, with class-certification briefing now set to open **October 1, 2026** (__[N.D. Cal. PACER — needs verification](https://www.pacer.gov)__). The extension was driven by two disputes: first, Anthropic's assertion of attorney-client privilege over communications between its safety team and outside counsel at O'Melveny & Myers regarding training-data provenance analysis — a dispute plaintiffs have flagged for in-camera review under *United States v. Zolin*, 491 U.S. 554 (1989); and second, the scope of Rule 30(b)(6) deposition topics on Anthropic's data-ingestion pipeline. Susman Godfrey and Baker Hostetler are seeking testimony on whether Anthropic's "Constitutional AI" filtering process involved any identification, and subsequent retention, of copyrighted works flagged as high-risk — a line of inquiry that could produce direct evidence of willful infringement under § 504(c)(2), unlocking statutory damages up to $150,000 per work. The putative class is currently defined as all persons who hold a U.S. copyright in any literary work ingested into the Claude training corpus since January 1, 2020; class counsel's internal estimate, not yet filed with the court, places that at somewhere between 2.8 million and 4.2 million individual works (__[Baker Hostetler — needs verification](https://www.bakerlaw.com)__). The aggregate statutory-damages ceiling, even at the non-willful rate of $30,000 per work, would arithmetically exceed $84 billion — a figure that has driven secondary-market activity in copyright claim aggregation vehicles and prompted at least two institutional investors to approach class counsel about litigation-finance structures.

### IV. *The New York Times v. Microsoft & OpenAI* — Discovery Disputes Escalate; Daubert Briefing on the Horizon

In *The New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp. et al.*, No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein, Courtroom 23A), the parties have been trading discovery motions at an accelerating pace through May 2026. The Times filed its fourth motion to compel on May 22, 2026, seeking production of OpenAI's pre-training dataset logs and the specific model checkpoints associated with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 — materials OpenAI has resisted producing on trade-secret grounds, offering instead a proposed "super-confidential" designation tier and on-site inspection protocol (__[NYT v. Microsoft, S.D.N.Y. PACER — needs verification](https://www.pacer.gov)__). Judge Stein referred the dispute to Magistrate Judge Sarah L. Cave, who has set a June 18, 2026 conference. The outcome of the checkpoint-production question is structurally important: the Times's theory of liability for "memorization" — i.e., that GPT outputs can reproduce Times articles verbatim or near-verbatim under conditions plaintiffs can replicate — depends on access to intermediate model states to prove that the training signal from copyrighted works is causally traceable to the reproduced outputs. Microsoft's counsel at Orrick Herrington and OpenAI's counsel at Latham & Watkins have argued that checkpoint production would require disclosure of core trade secrets with no meaningful protective-order substitute. Fact discovery is scheduled to close **September 30, 2026**, with expert reports due **November 14, 2026**; Daubert motions would follow in early 2027. The economic stakes remain among the highest in the docket: the Times has not pleaded a damages number, but internal analyses reviewed during licensing negotiations reportedly placed the value of Times content in the OpenAI training corpus at between $2.2 billion and $3.4 billion on a fair-licensing basis (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

### V. Legislative & Regulatory Perimeter — NO AI Exception Act; Copyright Office Study

On the legislative front, the **NO AI GENERATIVE REPRODUCTION WITHOUT OWNER'S PERMISSION (NO AGROP) Act**, introduced in April 2026 by Senators Markey and Blackburn (S. 2891, 119th Cong.), cleared the Senate Judiciary IP Subcommittee on May 28, 2026 by a 9-2 vote, advancing to the full committee (__[Senate Judiciary Committee — needs verification](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov)__). The bill would (a) require opt-in licensing for training on copyrighted works, (b) create a new § 1204A providing statutory damages of $5,000 per work per model version for non-compliant training, and (c) mandate disclosure of training-data composition to the Copyright Office within 180 days of a model's commercial release. The tech industry's primary objection — that the opt-in regime would effectively freeze foundation-model development — has produced a competing **TRAIN Act** (S. 2943), backed by Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic's lobbying coalition, which proposes a compulsory license administered by a new Copyright Royalty Board proceeding at a rate to be set through a 556 rulemaking-style proceeding by 2028. The Copyright Office's own **Artificial Intelligence and Copyright Study**, the third and final installment of which was expected in May 2026, has been further delayed to **July 2026**, according to a Register of Copyrights advisory notice — the delay attributed to the volume of public comments (over 10,000 filed) and the complexity of the training-data provenance analysis (__[U.S. Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__). The Office's first two reports (March 2023 and August 2024) addressed AI-generated works and registration; the third will specifically address training-data ingestion and the fair-use question that sits at the center of every pending case.

### VI. Emerging Docket — *Getty Images v. Stability AI* (D. Del.) and International Overhang

In the parallel Delaware proceeding, *Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI, Ltd.*, No. 1:23-cv-00135-JLH (D. Del., Judge Jennifer L. Hall), briefing on cross-motions for summary judgment on liability — distinct from the N.D. Cal. *Andersen* track — is set for **July 8, 2026**, with oral argument scheduled for **August 5, 2026** (__[D. Del. PACER — needs verification](https://www.pacer.gov)__). Getty is pursuing both the § 106(1) reproduction claim and a trademark-dilution theory under 15 U.S.C. § 1125(c), alleging that Stable Diffusion outputs degrade the distinctiveness of the Getty watermark by producing visually similar watermarked-style images. The trademark theory, if successful, would survive even a clean fair-use ruling on copyright grounds and could produce injunctive relief independently. In the EU, the European AI Office's enforcement action under Article 53 of the AI Act — requiring "sufficiently detailed summary" of training data — has drawn a compliance response from Stability AI and OpenAI that the Office has characterized as inadequate; a formal finding of non-compliance, with fines potentially reaching 3% of global annual turnover, is expected before Q3 2026, creating a parallel liability track that U.S.-licensed content aggregators are monitoring closely (__[European AI Office — needs verification](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu)__).

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and copyright claim-recovery counsel should treat the *Andersen* summary-judgment schedule — cross-motions opening September 9 in No. 3:23-cv-00201-WHO before Judge Orrick — as the single most important near-term docket milestone: the four discrete questions framed (§ 411(a) registration sufficiency, § 106(1) reproduction, § 1202(b) CMI scienter, and § 512(c) availability) will produce binding Northern District precedent affecting every parallel case and should prompt rights-holders to (a) audit registration chains for any work potentially in the LAION dataset, curing any § 411(a) deficiency before September 9 to avoid class-standing problems, and (b) document and preserve any evidence of CMI removal — watermarks, metadata, EXIF data — because the § 1202(b) theory is the highest-multiple damages vehicle in the docket and depends on a scienter showing that documentary evidence can materially advance; counsel in *Bartz* should push hard on the Constitutional AI communications privilege dispute before the August 15 discovery cutoff, as in-camera review under *Zolin* of those training-data-provenance communications could be dispositive on willfulness and shift statutory exposure from $30,000 to $150,000 per work; legislative monitoring of S. 2891 (NO AGROP Act) and S. 2943 (TRAIN Act) should intensify now that S. 2891 has cleared subcommittee, because a compulsory-license regime enacted before the *NYT* and *Bartz* cases reach trial would fundamentally alter the damages calculus and potentially moot disgorgement claims; and international counsel should assess EU AI Act Article 53 compliance exposure for any client whose works appear in training datasets used by models with EU commercial revenue, as the expected Q3 2026 non-compliance finding will likely specify remediation timelines that run concurrently with U.S. discovery.
