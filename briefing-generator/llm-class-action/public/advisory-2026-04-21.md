# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 21, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The Bartz v. Anthropic settlement's extraordinary claim rate — 91.3% of eligible works, representing 440,490 titles claimed by the March 30 deadline — has emerged as a central data point ahead of the May 14 fairness hearing before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, now just twenty-three days away, with Anthropic's second installment of $300 million due in nine days on April 30, bringing total funded commitments to $600 million of the $1.5 billion fund. The Authors Alliance's newly published analysis of the unsealed objections, including Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's challenge to the $3,000 per-title payout as a fraction of the $150,000 statutory ceiling under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c), has sharpened the analytical framework Judge Martínez-Olguín will apply under Rule 23(e)(2), where the court must weigh the adequacy of the relief in light of both the costs, risks, and delay of trial and the effectiveness of the proposed method of distributing relief. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/newsbrief/index.html?record=5826)) The 91.3% figure is itself a powerful rebuttal to adequacy objections: in a field where typical class action claim rates hover around 10%, the participation rate demonstrates that the class notice program — which the Authors Guild describes as the most extensive in copyright class action history — reached and persuaded an overwhelming majority of eligible claimants, a factor courts have consistently treated as evidence of settlement adequacy under the third Rule 23(e)(2)(C) consideration. The practical implication for distribution mechanics is equally significant: at 440,490 claimed titles and a projected net fund of approximately $1.35 billion after attorneys' fees and administration costs, the per-title distribution could modestly exceed the initially estimated $3,000, depending on the court's resolution of the publisher-versus-author allocation formula and any cy pres distribution of unclaimed residuals.

The Concord Music Group et al. v. Anthropic docket has advanced materially with the filing of a partial summary judgment motion on March 23, 2026, supported by 218 undisputed facts drawn from deposition testimony, in which the music publishers argue that Anthropic's reproduction of 499 copyrighted compositions was purely commercial and that Claude's lyrics outputs directly compete with licensed services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch — an argument that, if credited, collapses the first and fourth fair-use factors simultaneously. ([Music Ally](https://musically.com/2026/03/25/music-publishers-file-for-partial-summary-judgement-against-anthropic/)) The cross-industry amicus record is now the broadest in any AI copyright case: the RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, and allied music organizations filed on March 30, followed by the AAP, News/Media Alliance, STM, and Authors Guild filing a second brief supporting the publishers' motion — together spanning music, book, news, and academic publishing and arguing that a functioning licensing market already exists and that Anthropic's unlicensed copying undermines it. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) ([AAP](https://publishers.org/news/book-news-and-journal-publishers-join-with-authors-in-amicus-brief-in-support-of-music-publishers-in-concord-v-anthropic/)) The parallel $3.1 billion Concord/UMG v. Anthropic II action (filed January 28, 2026) covering 20,000+ songs, combined with the individual Carreyrou v. Anthropic/OpenAI/Google/Meta/xAI/Perplexity opt-out action seeking $150,000 per title per defendant, together demonstrate the multi-front litigation pressure that has made unauthorized AI training on copyrighted works the most consequential intellectual property issue in federal courts.

The In re OpenAI MDL (No. 3143) continues its discovery phase following the March 2026 order expanding production to include the 78-million and 10-million ChatGPT log reservoirs beyond the original 20-million sample — an order that will generate granular output-level evidence needed to test the "transformative use" framework at scale when fair-use motions are briefed in mid-2026. ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)) Baker Botts's April 2026 legal watch confirms the intensive trajectory, with BakerHostetler's updated case tracker cataloging over 75 active AI copyright lawsuits in federal courts. ([BakerHostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/services/artificial-intelligence-ai/case-tracker-artificial-intelligence-copyrights-and-class-actions/)) Internationally, the Getty Images v. Stability AI appeal was filed with the UK Court of Appeal by the February 3 deadline, raising the pivotal question of whether AI model weights constitute "infringing copies" under CDPA § 27 even absent storage of actual reproductions — a question whose resolution will determine whether the importation of foreign-trained AI models into the UK triggers secondary copyright liability, a theory with profound implications for cross-border AI deployment. ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/next-steps-for-getty-v-stability-why-has-permission-to-appeal-been-granted)) ([IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2026/01/permission-to-appeal-granted-in-getty.html)) The CJEU's Like Company v. Google referral, which held its first hearing on March 10, 2026, remains the first occasion the Court of Justice has directly considered generative AI and copyright under EU law, and its advisory opinion could reshape the Article 4 text and data mining exception under the DSM Directive across all 27 member states.

On the legislative front, the bipartisan CLEAR Act (S. 3813) introduced by Senators Schiff and Curtis continues to gather industry support, with its retroactive disclosure mandate requiring detailed filings with the Register of Copyrights enumerating every copyrighted work in training datasets, backed by $5,000 per-work civil penalties — a framework that, combined with the White House's March 20 National AI Policy Framework encouraging collective licensing mechanisms, signals convergence between legislative and executive branches toward mandatory transparency and compensation regimes for AI training. ([IP Watchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/02/11/clear-act-establish-notice-requirements-copyrighted-works-ai-training-data/)) ([Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/legislation-watch-for-ai-developers-and-registered-copyright-owners-the-federal-clear-act/)) The Supreme Court's March 2 denial of certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter, confirming that AI-generated works require human authorship for copyright protection, and the March 25 unanimous reversal in Cox Communications v. Sony Music, narrowing contributory-infringement liability to active inducement or services incapable of substantial noninfringing uses, together define the doctrinal guardrails within which the next wave of fair-use decisions will operate — guardrails that simultaneously protect AI companies from secondary liability for end-user infringement while leaving their direct training-data practices fully exposed to per se infringement claims where pirated sources were used. ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/us-supreme-court-declines-to-consider-whether-ai-alone-can-create-copyrighted-works))

## Recommended Actions

Class members who submitted Bartz claims by the March 30 deadline should verify their submission confirmations through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and preserve all correspondence from the settlement administrator, as the 91.3% claim rate will be a centerpiece of class counsel's adequacy presentation at the May 14 hearing; rights-holders who filed objections should review the Authors Alliance's newly published analysis of unsealed objections to assess whether their arguments align with or diverge from the Bishop objection's focus on per-title valuation, given that Judge Martínez-Olguín may request supplemental briefing or group related objections for oral argument. AI developers with music-generation or text-generation capabilities should study the Concord v. Anthropic partial summary judgment motion's 218 undisputed facts as a preview of the evidentiary standard that plaintiffs will deploy across all AI copyright dockets, and should conduct internal audits of whether their outputs compete with licensed content services, given that the motion's core theory — that AI outputs are direct market substitutes — collapses the first and fourth fair-use factors simultaneously. Companies subject to the proposed CLEAR Act should accelerate training-data provenance audits and develop disclosure frameworks now, as the bill's retroactive requirement to enumerate every copyrighted work in existing datasets creates exposure that grows with each day of delay, and early compliance preparation positions companies favorably for both regulatory engagement and litigation risk mitigation. Practitioners monitoring the Getty v. Stability AI appeal should prepare for the possibility that a Court of Appeal finding that model weights constitute "infringing copies" would create immediate secondary liability for any organization importing foreign-trained AI models into the UK, a ruling that would require urgent reassessment of cross-border AI deployment strategies.
