# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | May 7, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

With the Bartz v. Anthropic fairness hearing exactly one week away, the single most consequential procedural development for the broader AI copyright docket is the Third Circuit's scheduling of oral argument in Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence, Inc. for June 11, 2026 — placing the first appellate fair-use determination on AI training just twenty-eight days after Judge Martínez-Olguín rules on the $1.5 billion settlement's adequacy under Rule 23(e). ([Chat GPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/04/14/third-circuit-sets-oral-argument-on-june-11-for-1st-appeal-of-decision-on-fair-use-in-ai-training-case-follows-another-recent-third-circuit-decision-on-fair-use-in-american-society-for-testing-v-upc/)) The timing is not incidental: ROSS Intelligence filed a Rule 28(j) letter alerting the panel to the Third Circuit's own recent decision in American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc., which addressed fair use in the context of a for-profit AI startup's republication of building-code materials to expand public access, arguing the decision supports a transformative-use finding for AI training on copyrighted works. ([Chat GPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/04/09/ross-intelligence/)) CCIA, Chamber of Progress, and NetChoice have already filed an amicus brief urging reversal of the district court's rejection of fair use, contending that intermediate copying for the purpose of building a functional tool — where user-facing outputs are not the copied editorial prose — falls within the transformative-use framework of Google v. Oracle, 593 U.S. 1 (2021). ([CCIA](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/04/ccia-urges-court-to-affirm-fair-use-protections-for-generative-ai-training/)) The convergence of these developments means that by mid-June 2026, the AI copyright landscape will have received its first appellate fair-use signal and its largest-ever settlement final-approval ruling within the span of a single month — a doctrinal inflection point that will ripple through every active docket, from the OpenAI MDL's anticipated mid-2026 fair-use briefing to the Concord v. Anthropic summary judgment ruling expected this summer.

On the settlement administration front, the Bartz claims data remains striking: 440,490 of 482,474 eligible works have been claimed, reflecting a 91.3% claim rate that class counsel will cite as evidence of class satisfaction under Rule 23(e)(2)(C)(ii). ([All About Lawyer](https://allaboutlawyer.com/bartz-v-anthropic-1-5-billion-copyright-settlement-authors-claim/)) The distribution computation is scheduled for June 11, with the settlement administrator to apply the weighted methodology that has drawn Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's objection challenging the allocation formula's treatment of individual authors versus publishers — an objection that remains live and will be addressed at the May 14 hearing. The Authors Alliance has published an updated FAQ detailing the hearing logistics, including the Zoom participation option, and has catalogued the unsealed objections, which cluster around five themes: exclusion of foreign and non-U.S.-registered works, systematic publisher favoritism in the distribution plan, alleged notice deficiencies, inadequacy of per-work compensation relative to 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) statutory damages potential ($150,000 per willful infringement), and class-counsel conflict concerns arising from simultaneous representation in the music publisher dockets. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/))

The legislative picture has sharpened considerably since the Blackburn-Welch roundtable on April 22, at which more than twenty artists — including Grace Potter — joined Senators Blackburn and Welch on Capitol Hill to advocate for the NO FAKES Act and the TRAIN Act during the Recording Academy's GRAMMYs on the Hill Advocacy Day. ([Blackburn Senate](https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/4/ai/blackburn-welch-hold-roundtable-with-over-20-artists-who-support-no-fakes-act-and-train-act-to-protect-creators-from-ai-harms)) The TRAIN Act (H.R. 7209 / S. 2455) would establish an administrative subpoena mechanism enabling copyright holders to compel disclosure of whether their works were used in AI training, with a rebuttable presumption of copying upon noncompliance — a procedural tool that would have dramatically simplified the discovery battles consuming the OpenAI MDL, where Judge Stein's January 5 and March 9 orders compelling production of 20 million (and then 78 million additional) ChatGPT logs came only after protracted litigation. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7209/text)) Meanwhile, the Blackburn TRUMP AMERICA AI Act's Title XIII — incorporating a declaration that AI training on copyrighted works is not fair use — continues to create tension with both the White House's National AI Policy Framework (which recommends judicial resolution) and the bipartisan CLEAR Act (S. 3813), which mandates training-data disclosure without prejudging fair use. For settlement class members, the legislative trajectory creates a double bind: statutory elimination of the fair-use defense would retroactively increase the value of released claims, but the release would already have locked in the $1.5 billion figure.

On the parallel music docket, the fully briefed cross-motions for partial summary judgment in Concord Music Group et al. v. Anthropic PBC represent the most imminent fair-use ruling on AI music training, with the court expected to rule this summer on whether Anthropic's acknowledged copying of more than 100 copyrighted compositions (and the broader catalog of 20,000+ songs identified in the RIAA/NMPA amicus) constitutes infringement "on a massive scale" as publishers contend, or falls within the transformative-use framework Anthropic will invoke. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/infringement-on-a-massive-scale-umg-concord-and-abkco-ask-court-to-rule-against-ai-company-anthropic-ahead-of-trial/)) The outcome directly affects the separate $3.1 billion UMG v. Anthropic damages action (filed January 28, 2026) and the broader licensing-market calculus, particularly given Disney's $1 billion investment in OpenAI and the Sora character-licensing framework that has established the first major studio-AI pricing benchmark. Norton Rose Fulbright's 2026 litigation update confirms that the total AI copyright case count has reached approximately 75 federal lawsuits, with 2026 expected to deliver the first wave of dispositive fair-use rulings across multiple dockets simultaneously — Concord, the OpenAI MDL, Andersen v. Stability AI (trial set September 8, 2026), and UMG v. Suno (Judge Saylor). ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026))

## Recommended Actions

With the fairness hearing seven days out, class members should verify their claim status and payment information through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and confirm that individually registered works are properly enumerated in the distribution plan, particularly authors whose works were filed under group Copyright Office registrations — the Bishop objection's challenge to the weighted allocation methodology makes individual-work verification critical before the June 11 distribution computation. Practitioners should calendar the Thomson Reuters v. Ross oral argument on June 11 as the most significant near-term doctrinal event: the Third Circuit's ruling will be the first appellate fair-use determination on AI training, and its treatment of the transformative-use and market-substitution factors under 17 U.S.C. § 107 will directly inform valuation arguments in every pending settlement and damages action. Rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz class — including the Carreyrou group pursuing § 504(c) statutory damages in their non-class action — should assess whether the Thomson Reuters outcome affects the expected value of their individual claims, as an appellate holding that AI training is fair use would significantly narrow their damages theory. AI developers facing training-data litigation should evaluate the TRAIN Act's administrative subpoena mechanism against their current data-provenance documentation: if enacted, the rebuttable presumption of copying upon noncompliance will shift the evidentiary burden in a way that makes proactive training-data audits a cost-effective risk-mitigation strategy now rather than after the statutory framework is in place. Music-industry practitioners should monitor the Concord v. Anthropic summary judgment ruling timeline closely and prepare for the cascading implications across the BMG, UMG, and Suno/Udio dockets — a ruling against Anthropic on partial summary judgment would dramatically increase settlement leverage across all pending music-publisher actions.
