### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, N.D. Cal. No. 5:23-cv-03223-AMO — Motion to Dismiss Fully Briefed; Ruling Imminent

*Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, filed June 2023 in the Northern District of California before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín (Courtroom 10, 19th Floor, San Francisco), remains the leading bellwether for training-data ingestion liability. The case was brought by a putative class of authors — lead plaintiff Ta-Nehisi Coates among them in an amended complaint posture — represented by Susman Godfrey LLP and Clarkson Law Firm, with Baker & Hostetler serving as co-counsel on the DMCA §1202 track (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Anthropic's motion to dismiss the First Amended Complaint raised four principal arguments: (1) that ingestion of copyrighted text for model training constitutes transformative fair use under the four-factor *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* framework, 510 U.S. 569 (1994); (2) that plaintiffs lack Article III standing to assert DMCA §1202(b) copyright management information (CMI) removal claims because model outputs do not reproduce the CMI-stripped text in actionable proximity to the original; (3) that the direct-infringement claim fails because temporary copies created during training are non-volitional and thus exempt under *Cartoon Network LP v. CSC Holdings*, 536 F.3d 121 (2d Cir. 2008) — an argument Anthropic acknowledges is circuit-law transplant from the Second Circuit; and (4) that vicarious-infringement allegations against Anthropic's corporate parent structure are insufficiently pled (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Plaintiffs' opposition, filed in April 2026, countered that (a) the Ninth Circuit has never adopted the Second Circuit's transient-copy carve-out in the training context; (b) the four-factor fair-use analysis is inherently fact-intensive and cannot be resolved at 12(b)(6); (c) CMI removal is actionable even where the output does not reproduce the original verbatim, citing *Fischer v. Forrest*, 286 F. Supp. 3d 590 (S.D.N.Y. 2018), for the proposition that §1202 protects the integrity of attribution metadata regardless of reproduction quantum; and (d) Anthropic's commercialization of Claude — generating revenues estimated at $1.3 billion annualized as of Q1 2026 — weighs against fair use under the fourth factor (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The reply was filed approximately May 9, 2026. Judge Martínez-Olguín has not yet scheduled oral argument, but local rules permit ruling on submission. **Practitioner note:** If the court denies dismissal in whole or part on the §1202 claim, it would be the first district court ruling in the Ninth Circuit squarely holding that large-language model training pipelines can generate §1202(b)(1) liability — a precedent that would immediately be cited in *Andersen*, *Silverman*, and the *Times* litigation. A grant of dismissal with leave to amend on fair-use grounds would likely survive appeal only if Judge Martínez-Olguín issues a detailed factual finding, which she would be reluctant to do at pleading stage.

### II. *New York Times Co. v. OpenAI Corp.*, S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS — Discovery Disputes, ESI Protocol, and the "Memorization" Evidence Track

Before Judge Sidney H. Stein (Courtroom 23A, Daniel Patrick Moynihan U.S. Courthouse), the *New York Times* litigation — the highest-profile direct-copyright-infringement case against a generative AI company — is now deep in the discovery phase following the court's February 2026 ruling largely denying OpenAI and Microsoft's motion to dismiss (__[New York Times / Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The Times seeks both statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. §504(c)(1)-(2) and actual damages, and has publicly estimated potential exposure in the billions of dollars based on willful-infringement multipliers. OpenAI disputes the framing, arguing that fair use under §107 renders statutory damages unavailable and that the Times' "memorization" evidence — screenshots of GPT-4 reproducing Times articles near-verbatim — was engineered through adversarial prompting that is not representative of ordinary use.

The current discovery contest centers on three fronts: (i) OpenAI's assertion of work-product protection over training-dataset curation logs and internal "red-team" memorization analyses; (ii) the Times' demand for production of the complete Common Crawl ingestion record and associated deduplication decisions; and (iii) a dispute over whether Microsoft's Azure infrastructure hosting logs are within the scope of the Times' subpoena to Microsoft Corporation, a co-defendant (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Magistrate Judge Sarah Netburn, to whom discovery is referred, held a conference on approximately May 22, 2026, and ordered supplemental letter briefs on the work-product question. A ruling is expected by mid-June 2026.

The "memorization" evidentiary track is strategically significant beyond this case. If the court accepts the Times' methodology — using systematic API querying to surface verbatim reproduction of >25-word sequences — it would establish a reproducible evidentiary framework applicable in every pending training-data infringement case. OpenAI's position, echoed in its April 2026 letter brief, is that such testing manipulates the model's output distribution in ways that are not probative of what the model ordinarily "does" with training data, and that the appropriate fair-use inquiry focuses on the nature of the training process, not the possibility of extraction under adversarial conditions (__[OpenAI public filing — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

### III. *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.*, 9th Cir. No. 23-15262 — Amicus Phase and the Visual-Art Copyright Theory

The appeal of Judge William Orrick's (N.D. Cal.) dismissal order in *Andersen v. Stability AI* — the class action brought by visual artists Sarah Andersen, Kelly McKernan, and Karla Ortiz, represented by Matthew Butterick and Joseph Saveri Law Firm — reached the Ninth Circuit briefing cycle in early 2026. Appellants challenge dismissal of direct-infringement, DMCA §1202, and right-of-publicity claims against Stability AI, Midjourney, and DeviantArt (__[Courthouse News Service — needs verification](https://www.courthousenews.com)__).

The appeal raises a circuit-level question with no binding precedent: whether the storage of compressed latent-space representations derived from copyrighted images constitutes "copies" within the meaning of 17 U.S.C. §101 — the definitional provision requiring that a work be "fixed in any tangible medium of expression." Appellants argue that diffusion-model weight matrices are sufficiently expressive of the training images to constitute derivative works under *Gaste v. Kaiserman*, 863 F.2d 1061 (2d Cir. 1988)'s "more than de minimis" standard, adapted to the Ninth Circuit's *Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin* (2020) extrinsic-intrinsic test for substantial similarity (__[Volokh Conspiracy / Reason — needs verification](https://reason.com)__).

The Copyright Alliance and Authors Guild filed amicus briefs in support of appellants in April 2026, arguing that a ruling affirming dismissal would effectively immunize the entire generative-AI training process from copyright scrutiny in the Ninth Circuit. The Computer & Communications Industry Association (CCIA) filed in support of appellees, contending that model weights are functional artifacts, not expressive copies, and that extending copyright protection to latent representations would chill socially beneficial AI development (__[Copyright Alliance — needs verification](https://copyrightalliance.org)__). Oral argument has not yet been calendared, but the fully briefed panel is likely to hear argument in September or October 2026.

### IV. Copyright Office AI Part 2 Report — Training Data, Licensing, and the §108 Question

The U.S. Copyright Office's *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence Part 2: Generative AI Training* report — the statutory-and-policy companion to the January 2025 Part 1 report on AI-generated output copyrightability — has been in internal review since the agency circulated a draft to select stakeholders in March 2026. The Office received over 10,000 comments in the 2023 notice-of-inquiry phase, and the report is expected to address: (a) whether existing §107 fair-use doctrine adequately covers LLM training, or whether legislative intervention is warranted; (b) the feasibility of a mandatory licensing or compulsory-licensing regime modeled on §115 mechanical licensing; (c) whether the §108 library-and-archives exemption can be read to cover AI training datasets curated by AI labs; and (d) international TRIPS compatibility of any proposed licensing framework (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__).

The Office's Register of Copyrights, Shira Perlmutter, has publicly signaled — in remarks at a March 2026 Georgetown Law symposium — that the Office is unlikely to recommend a blanket fair-use safe harbor for commercial training and is instead examining "middle-path" licensing mechanisms. If the Part 2 report endorses a licensing regime, it would provide ammunition for plaintiffs in *Times*, *Bartz*, and *Andersen* to argue that industry's failure to seek licenses is evidence of bad faith under the fourth fair-use factor (__[Georgetown Law / Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__). Publication is now projected for late June to early July 2026; a pre-July release would land during the *Bartz* post-ruling window and could influence any amended pleading strategy.

### V. Parallel Litigation Horizon — *Silverman v. OpenAI*, *Tremblay v. OpenAI*, and the §117 Parallel-Copying Defense

Consolidation proceedings in the Northern District of California before Judge Vince Chhabria — covering *Silverman v. OpenAI Corp.*, No. 3:23-cv-03416-VC, and *Tremblay v. OpenAI Inc.*, No. 3:23-cv-03223-VC — are proceeding on a modified class-certification schedule after Judge Chhabria's March 2026 case-management order (__[Courthouse News / Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Plaintiffs — a class of book authors represented by Joseph Saveri Law Firm and Matthew Butterick — allege that GPT-series model training on Books3 and similar datasets constitutes wholesale copyright infringement of their registered works.

OpenAI has pressed a novel §117(a)(1) argument: that AI labs qualify as "owners of a copy" of the training corpus under the statutory text, and that copying necessary for AI training is analogous to the archival-copy privilege. No court has accepted this argument; the §117 exemption was designed for software-backup contexts under *Vault Corp. v. Quaid Software*, 847 F.2d 255 (5th Cir. 1988), and its extension to training-data ingestion of third-party expressive works would require a statutory construction no circuit has endorsed. Judge Chhabria has signaled skepticism of the §117 theory in oral remarks, though no written order has issued (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). Class certification briefing is expected to commence in Q3 2026, with plaintiffs' motion due approximately August 15, 2026 per the March scheduling order.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders, literary estates, publishers, and visual artists with unregistered works should **immediately prioritize registration** under 17 U.S.C. §411 — registration is a jurisdictional prerequisite to statutory damages under §504(c), and the *Times* litigation has confirmed that willful-infringement multipliers (up to $150,000 per work) are the strategic leverage point in settlement negotiations with AI labs. Counsel monitoring *Bartz v. Anthropic* (No. 5:23-cv-03223-AMO) should prepare class-membership documentation in anticipation of a ruling denying dismissal, which would trigger a class-certification scheduling order within 90 days under N.D. Cal. Civil L.R. 23-2; the class definition as currently pled covers all authors whose registered works appeared in training corpora used to develop Claude 1, 2, and 3 series models. Practitioners advising AI-developer clients should conduct an urgent audit of CMI-stripping practices in their training pipelines — the §1202(b) theory is now fully viable at the pleading stage in multiple district courts, and remediation (retaining author/title metadata in training records) is significantly cheaper than post-hoc damages exposure. Finally, monitor the Copyright Office Part 2 report release window closely: if the Office endorses a licensing framework, AI labs that have publicly resisted licensing negotiations (OpenAI, Anthropic, Stability AI) face a materially weakened fair-use posture under the fourth-factor commercial-harm analysis, and settlement dynamics in all pending cases will shift accordingly.
