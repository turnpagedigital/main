## Analysis & Developments

**The two-front Anthropic exposure is now fully joined.** *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.), carries a $1.5 billion settlement that remains unsigned — 
Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement after the May 14 fairness hearing, and as of the June 2026 reporting cycle, final approval remains pending.
 Simultaneously, *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.) ("Concord I"), has cross-motions for summary judgment fully briefed before Judge Eumi K. Lee, with a ruling on the threshold fair-use question that could reframe the entire AI-copyright docket. These two proceedings are running in parallel and are analytically entangled: the fair-use outcome in *Concord I* will set the market-harm comparator framework that courts across the docket — including any *Bartz* objector appeal — will cite first.

### Bartz v. Anthropic — Settlement in Post-Hearing Limbo; Distribution Clock Paused

The May 14, 2026 fairness hearing before Judge Martínez-Olguín in Courtroom 10 (N.D. Cal., San Francisco) ran 75 minutes. 
After the hearing, the judge declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but observers broadly expect her to grant final approval relatively quickly given the tone of the proceedings.
 The immediate post-hearing order (Dkt. entry, signed May 14, 2026) established a narrow supplemental briefing track: 
Anthropic was permitted to file a brief of no more than 2 pages by May 21 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and plaintiffs were required to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the April 30, 2026 proposed order; the court closed the record to objectors with no further submissions permitted.


The late-opt-out question is not trivial administratively. 
In its supplemental filing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any record supporting relief and because other rightsholders have not consented.


On the fee side, the revised fee request is materially smaller than the original ask. 
Class counsel urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request that had been revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 That downward revision — from 25% to 12.5% of the $1.5 billion fund — is significant for claims investors and rights-holders modeling net distributions: less fee drag means more of each installment reaches claimants. 
Administrative costs for JND Legal Administration and a $50,000 per-named-plaintiff service award (totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives) are also charged to the fund.


Participation figures finalized at the claims deadline: 
class counsel reported that 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% participation rate (compared to a typical class action rate of around 10%), vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 Motion for Final Approval filed 11 days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 
Class counsel Justin Nelson indicated the per-work payout will be approximately $3,100, with the class achieving a 92.77% works-participation rate.
 The high participation rate compressed per-work recovery from earlier estimates in the $5,000–$6,000 range (which assumed only 54% uptake) to the original design figure.

**Distribution mechanics.** 
Anthropic funds the $1.5 billion in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The first $300 million tranche is already held in an interest-bearing escrow account. 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 That appeal window is the most consequential remaining risk. Seven objectors appeared at the May 14 hearing; any one of them can appeal a final approval order to the Ninth Circuit, triggering delay of potentially 12–24 months on distribution. Rights-holder counsel should monitor the docket for a notice of appeal promptly after the final approval order issues.

**Class scope and § 411 anchor.** 
The class covers all rightsholders of books Anthropic acquired from LibGen and PiLiMi that were registered with the U.S. Copyright Office in a timely manner and bear an ISBN or ASIN number; notably, Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy — not the act of AI training — so the fair-use ruling on training applies only to the three named plaintiffs, not class members.
 That certification boundary has lasting doctrinal significance: the settlement does not adjudicate whether AI training on licensed or legally acquired books is fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107, leaving that question open for *Concord I*, *Kadrey*, *In re OpenAI MDL*, and every parallel proceeding.

### Concord I — Cross-MSJ Fully Briefed; Fair-Use Ruling Under Submission with Judge Lee

*Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee; Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen for discovery), is the docket where the fair-use question will actually be decided. The case covers 499 copyrighted musical works. 
The publishers' motion for partial summary judgment was filed March 23, 2026 in the Northern District of California, arguing that Anthropic's own admissions and evidence leave no material facts in dispute; it was accompanied by a 47-page statement of 218 "undisputed facts" supported by deposition testimony, internal Anthropic documents, and company admissions.


The publishers' § 107(4) market-harm theory is particularly concrete. 
On market harm, the filing states that when Claude outputs lyrics in response to user requests, "Anthropic provides the same service to its users as Publishers' licensees, without Publishers' authorization or any restrictions on that use," and cites data showing a Deezer study estimating over 60,000 AI-generated tracks were being submitted to that platform daily by January 2026, accounting for 3% of total streams.
 On the copying side, 
the filing states that Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works" and "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."
 Anthropic's cross-motion characterizes Claude's learning as "transformative" — 
Anthropic asked Judge Lee to find that its use of copyrighted materials to train Claude is "transformative" fair use, comparing Claude's learning to how humans learn from reading and internalizing the themes of various works.


The amicus record is unusually dense. 
On March 30, 2026, a coalition including the RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, the Music Artists Coalition, and the Artist Rights Alliance filed one amicus brief urging rejection of Anthropic's fair-use defense, while the Association of American Publishers, News/Media Alliance, International Association of Scientific, Technical & Medical Publishers, and the Authors Guild filed a second brief making parallel arguments from the book and academic publishing industries — both in support of the publishers' motion for partial summary judgment before Judge Lee.
 
The AAP brief presented an extensive chart of AI licensing deals involving OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and Perplexity — characterizing Anthropic as a "holdout" that has opted to scrape copyrighted content rather than license it like its peers.
 That licensing-market evidence goes directly to § 107(4) and to the § 504(c) statutory damages exposure should the court find willful infringement.


Dispositive motions in Concord I were fully briefed by June 8, 2026 (Concord I Dkt. 587 at 8).
 The matter is now under submission with Judge Lee. A ruling could issue at any time, and it will be the first federal district-court decision on the fair-use question in the context of music-lyrics training data at the summary-judgment stage — a decision likely to anchor briefing in *In re OpenAI Copyright MDL* (No. 3:23-cv-03223, N.D. Cal.) and *NYT v. OpenAI/Microsoft* regardless of which way it comes out.

### Concord II and the Stay Question


The same music publishers filed a second, $3 billion suit against Anthropic in January 2026, which they say is necessary to hold Anthropic accountable for "brazen," newly discovered mass infringement of sheet music and songbooks
 — covering more than 20,000 works. That case, *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC et al.*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee), is the parallel proceeding Anthropic moved to stay.


Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending final resolution of Concord I, or in the alternative pending resolution of the Concord I summary judgment motions and any subsequent appeal; the motion was set for hearing on May 20, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. in Courtroom 7 of the San Jose Division before Judge Lee.
 
Anthropic's core argument was that the threshold legal issues in Concord I — including whether its use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — will determine the viability of most if not all of publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect.
 The outcome of that stay motion has not been independently confirmed from the search results available this run. Rights-holder counsel should pull the Concord II docket (CourtListener No. 72199828) for the May 20 hearing order before acting on the assumption that Concord II is proceeding on a parallel discovery track.

An additional complication: 
a California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged she used Claude.ai to help draft the expert declaration that included the erroneous citation, but argued the error was "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 That disclosure is directly relevant to any sanctions motion and to the court's assessment of Anthropic's litigation conduct going forward. It also has a certain symmetry: an AI company's counsel using the AI defendant's own product introduced a hallucinated citation into AI-copyright litigation.

### Parallel Docket Carry-Forward: System-Level Signals

**Shadow Library Strategy.** 
The Bartz settlement has given plaintiffs' lawyers a roadmap: firms like Susman Godfrey have developed what one observer calls the "Shadow Library Strategy," which skips the debate over whether AI training is fair use (judges so far seem receptive to fair-use defenses on training) and goes straight for the piracy angle, alleging defendants downloaded from shadow libraries. Most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies — nearly all class actions — now allege this theory.


**17 U.S.C. § 504 statutory damages exposure.** The piracy theory is strategically superior to an AI-training fair-use claim in one critical respect: if training-data copying is not fair use AND the works were timely registered under § 411, the plaintiff may elect statutory damages under § 504(c) of up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement. At 440,000+ registered works in the *Bartz* class alone, the theoretical ceiling on a fully litigated willful-infringement verdict was existential — which explains why Anthropic settled. The same dynamic governs every pending case where the works list is populated with pre-infringement registrations.

**AI licensing market as § 107(4) evidence.** 
The broader music industry has moved rapidly to establish licensed AI partnerships — creating precisely the kind of market infrastructure that amicus briefs argue Anthropic is undermining — while the AAP brief charts AI licensing deals for textual works involving OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and Perplexity, naming dozens of publisher partners from the Associated Press and the New York Times to Condé Nast and HarperCollins.
 Every new licensing deal announced by a competing AI developer tightens the § 107(4) vise on defendants who have not licensed, because it confirms an established and functioning licensing market that the defendant chose not to enter.
