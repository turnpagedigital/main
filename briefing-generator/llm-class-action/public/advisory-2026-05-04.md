# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | May 4, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The Bartz v. Anthropic fairness hearing is now ten days away, and the convergence of legislative, judicial, and international developments in the intervening period has materially altered the landscape against which Judge Martínez-Olguín will evaluate the $1.5 billion settlement's adequacy under Rule 23(e). The most consequential doctrinal development since yesterday's advisory is the CCIA's amicus brief filed in Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence, Inc. before the Third Circuit, urging the court to affirm fair use protections for generative AI training — the first major industry coalition amicus since appellate briefing opened in the case that produced the only federal ruling to date holding that AI training on copyrighted material does not constitute fair use. ([CCIA](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/04/ccia-urges-court-to-affirm-fair-use-protections-for-generative-ai-training/)) CCIA, joined by Chamber of Progress and NetChoice, argues that intermediate copying for the purpose of building a functional tool — where user-facing outputs are not the copied editorial prose — falls squarely within the transformative-use framework that the Supreme Court applied in Google v. Oracle, 593 U.S. 1 (2021). The brief directly engages the district court's rejection of fair use and its finding that Ross Intelligence's use of West headnotes to train a legal research AI was market-substitutive, contending that the lower court conflated the market for editorial annotations with the market for judicial opinions themselves. ([Foundation for American Innovation](https://www.thefai.org/posts/amicus-brief-in-thomson-reuters-v-ross)) This Third Circuit appeal is now the most likely vehicle for the first appellate fair-use ruling on AI training, and its outcome will reverberate directly into the Bartz settlement's valuation calculus: if the Third Circuit holds that intermediate copying for AI training is fair use, the settlement's $1.5 billion figure will appear generous; if it holds otherwise, the class's released claims may prove substantially undervalued.

On the parallel music docket, UMG Recordings, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO have filed a motion for partial summary judgment in Concord Music Group et al. v. Anthropic PBC (N.D. Cal.), arguing that Anthropic's infringement was "on a massive scale" and that its fair use defense should be rejected as a matter of law. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/infringement-on-a-massive-scale-umg-concord-and-abkco-ask-court-to-rule-against-ai-company-anthropic-ahead-of-trial/)) The RIAA, NMPA, and additional music industry organizations have filed a supporting amicus brief characterizing Anthropic's unlicensed copying of more than 20,000 songs as "inexcusable," and the competing cross-motions for summary judgment are now fully briefed. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) A ruling on these cross-motions — expected this summer — will constitute the first judicial determination on whether AI training on copyrighted musical compositions and lyrics constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107, and will directly inform the separate $3 billion UMG v. Anthropic damages action covering the broader catalog. The Sony Music Entertainment v. Suno summary judgment hearing, also set for July 2026, creates a second simultaneous test case: Sony's refusal to settle with either Suno or Udio, in contrast to Warner Music's November 2025 settlement with Suno and UMG's October 2025 deal with Udio, positions the District of Massachusetts proceeding as an independent fair-use determination that could either reinforce or diverge from whatever the Northern District of California decides in Concord.

The legislative terrain has shifted significantly with Senator Marsha Blackburn's TRUMP AMERICA AI Act gaining momentum, as announced by her office in April 2026. ([Blackburn Senate](https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/4/ai/what-they-are-saying-blackburn-announces-growing-momentum-for-trump-america-ai-act)) Title XIII of the Act — incorporating the TRAIN Act — would amend the Copyright Act to declare that unauthorized reproduction, copying, or computational processing of copyrighted works for AI training does not constitute fair use, effectively overriding pending judicial determinations in Bartz, Concord, the OpenAI MDL, and every other active AI copyright proceeding. ([Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/03/ai-legislation-copyright-hollywood-trump-1236759660/)) This provision creates an extraordinary tension with the Trump administration's own AI Framework, which recommends letting courts decide the fair-use question, and with the CLEAR Act (S. 3813) introduced by Senators Schiff and Curtis, which mandates disclosure of copyrighted training data without prejudging the fair-use analysis. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/3813/text)) For settlement class members in Bartz, the Blackburn bill's trajectory is a double-edged sword: if enacted before final approval, a statutory declaration that AI training is not fair use would dramatically increase the value of released claims and could provide grounds for objectors to argue that the $1.5 billion figure is inadequate; if enacted after, the release would lock in the settlement figure against a backdrop of significantly enhanced rights.

Internationally, the CJEU's March 10, 2026 oral hearing in Like Company v. Google Ireland Ltd. (C-250/25) — a six-hour session examining whether Google Gemini's extraction and display of Slovak press publications constitutes communication to the public under the InfoSoc Directive, whether LLM training constitutes reproduction, and whether the Article 4 DSM Directive text-and-data-mining exception shields such training — represents the first European judicial engagement with generative AI copyright at the highest court level. ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/like-company-v-google-cjeu-holds-first-ever-hearing-on-generative-ai-and-copyright-on-10-march-2026)) The Advocate General's opinion is expected September 3, 2026, and whichever path the Court takes, the ruling will establish the framework for how the DSM Directive's TDM exception interacts with generative AI outputs across all 27 EU member states. ([IP Helpdesk](https://intellectual-property-helpdesk.ec.europa.eu/news-events/news/cjeu-grand-chamber-rules-music-sampling-and-pastiche-first-cjeu-hearing-generative-ai-and-copyright-2026-04-24_en)) The UK Getty v. Stability AI trial, now set for late 2026, proceeds on a narrower basis following the High Court's November 2025 ruling that model weights are not "infringing copies" — a holding that, if upheld, would significantly limit the scope of secondary copyright claims in AI training cases across common-law jurisdictions.

## Recommended Actions

With the Bartz fairness hearing ten days out, class members should verify claim status and payment information through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com ahead of the June 11 distribution computation, and authors whose works were registered under group Copyright Office registrations should confirm that their individual works are properly enumerated given the live Bishop objection challenging the distribution plan's weighted methodology. Practitioners tracking the Thomson Reuters v. Ross Third Circuit appeal should calendar the CCIA amicus brief as a significant indicator of industry coalition alignment on the fair-use question and should evaluate whether their clients — whether rights-holders or AI developers — need to file supplemental amicus briefs before the appellate argument date. Rights-holders with claims against Anthropic in the music context should monitor the Concord cross-motions closely: a ruling on partial summary judgment could come as early as this summer, and the outcome will directly affect the viability of the separate $3 billion UMG v. Anthropic damages action and the broader licensing-deal market. AI developers should assess the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act's copyright provision against their current training practices — if the Blackburn bill advances, any company that has relied on fair-use opinions to justify unlicensed training will need to evaluate retroactive licensing obligations and potential statutory damages exposure under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c). International practitioners should add the CJEU Like Company v. Google AG opinion date (September 3, 2026) and the UK Getty v. Stability AI trial date to their calendars as the two most consequential non-U.S. proceedings for AI copyright doctrine in 2026.
