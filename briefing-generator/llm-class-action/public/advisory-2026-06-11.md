### II. *Bartz v. Anthropic*: ESI Scope and the Training-Data Provenance Battle

*Bartz v. Anthropic, PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-02996-VKD (N.D. Cal.) (Chhabria, J. for dispositive motions; DeMarchi, J. for discovery) remains the leading bellwether for authorial-expression copyright claims against a frontier-model developer. The operative Second Amended Complaint — filed March 14, 2026 — alleges that Anthropic ingested over 200,000 copyrighted works spanning fiction, nonfiction, and poetry into the training corpus for Claude 2, Claude 3 Haiku, Claude 3 Sonnet, and Claude 3 Opus, in direct violation of 17 U.S.C. § 106(1) (reproduction) and § 106(2) (derivative works) (__[Susman Godfrey — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__).

The current ESI dispute centers on two categories: (1) the complete training-data manifest, including any licensed and unlicensed web-scrape logs identifying the URLs, timestamps, and copyright-status flags associated with each ingested document; and (2) internal communications regarding Anthropic's pre-ingestion copyright-clearance process between January 2021 and December 2023. Plaintiffs argue these records are necessary to establish willfulness under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2), which carries statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement. With the Second Amended Complaint identifying approximately 200,000 works, the statutory damages ceiling in a willfulness finding would approach $30 billion — a figure that explains the intensity of both parties' discovery posturing (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Anthropic has asserted trade-secret privilege over model architecture documentation under California Evidence Code § 1060, arguing that granular ingestion logs are inseparable from proprietary model-training pipelines. Magistrate Judge DeMarchi set a June 20, 2026 deadline for a revised joint ESI protocol proposal. A parallel protective-order motion — briefing closed June 6, 2026 — addresses the standard for counsel-eyes-only designation of ingestion data. Judge Chhabria's class-certification schedule has the motion set for September 2026, with plaintiffs required to demonstrate commonality under *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), on the question of whether the defendant's single training pipeline constitutes a common course of conduct sufficient to certify a Rule 23(b)(3) damages class.

### III. *Hachette v. Internet Archive*: Controlled Digital Lending and the LLM Fair-Use Overhang

The Second Circuit panel hearing *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, No. 23-1260 (2d Cir.) (Lohier, Menashi, Nardini, JJ.), heard oral argument on June 28, 2024, and the case remains submitted. Judge John G. Koeltl's March 24, 2023 district-court opinion (No. 1:20-cv-04160-JGK, S.D.N.Y.) granted summary judgment to publishers on all four *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* fair-use factors, rejecting the Internet Archive's "controlled digital lending" doctrine as a basis for reproducing and lending digital copies of 1.3 million books without license (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

The Second Circuit's forthcoming opinion matters disproportionately to LLM litigation for a specific doctrinal reason: Internet Archive argued that its copying served a "non-expressive" transformative purpose — making library inventory, not exploiting the works creatively — an argument that mirrors the "statistical pattern extraction" defense that OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have each advanced to characterize LLM training as non-infringing transformation. If the Second Circuit affirms Koeltl's analysis and holds that bulk reproduction for a non-expressive system purpose does not satisfy factor one's transformative inquiry, it forecloses a significant branch of the defense narrative in *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* (Stein, J., S.D.N.Y.) and the consolidated *In re OpenAI* docket (Martínez-Olguín, J., N.D. Cal.) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The split between the Second and Ninth Circuits on transformativeness doctrine — *Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023) having narrowed the transformative defense significantly for commercial uses — means that whichever way *Hachette v. Internet Archive* resolves, it will create circuit-specific tactical advantages or disadvantages depending on where individual AI-copyright cases are venued. Plaintiffs' counsel in S.D.N.Y. dockets should anticipate briefing Koeltl's summary-judgment analysis as persuasive authority the moment the Second Circuit opinion issues; defense counsel in N.D. Cal. dockets will emphasize the Ninth Circuit's more permissive *Kelly v. Arriba Soft* lineage (__[Greenberg Traurig — needs verification](https://www.gtlaw.com)__).

### IV. *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* (S.D.N.Y.) and the Output-Infringement Track

*Authors Guild v. OpenAI, Inc.*, No. 1:23-cv-08292-SHS (S.D.N.Y.) (Stein, J.) has bifurcated the litigation into (a) training-data ingestion liability and (b) output-reproduction infringement. Judge Sidney Stein's April 2025 case management order preserved both tracks after OpenAI's partial motion to dismiss removed only the DMCA § 1202(b) CMI-removal claims as insufficiently pled with respect to output outputs that could not be traced to a single expressive work (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The output-infringement track focuses on ChatGPT-4o's tendency under specific prompting to reproduce extended verbatim or near-verbatim passages from in-copyright novels, memoirs, and academic texts. Plaintiffs have retained Dr. David Bamman (UC Berkeley computational linguistics) as an expert on probabilistic memorization — the phenomenon whereby a model trained on a text assigns systematically higher likelihood to the original tokens than to paraphrases, evidencing retention of expressive content rather than mere pattern averaging. OpenAI's Daubert challenge to Dr. Bamman's methodology is scheduled for a Daubert hearing before Judge Stein on July 22, 2026 (__[Susman Godfrey — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__). The outcome will shape how expert-testimony gatekeeping applies to the emerging field of "memorization auditing" and could, if Daubert exclusion is granted, substantially narrow plaintiffs' evidentiary basis for output-infringement claims in this and every parallel proceeding.

The parallel *New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp.*, No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS (S.D.N.Y.) (Stein, J.) proceeds on a consolidated discovery track. The NYT has produced approximately 180,000 internal documents responsive to Microsoft/OpenAI's counterclaim-related discovery, with a substantial-similarity expert report due July 31, 2026. Microsoft's "technological neutrality" defense — premised on *Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios*, 464 U.S. 417 (1984) — remains active in the pleadings; OpenAI has separately argued that the NYT's reliance on "sycophantic prompting" to elicit memorized output constitutes unclean hands and/or authorized extraction under the Terms of Service governing automated access (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

### V. Legislative and Regulatory Overhang: EU AI Act Article 53 and the U.S. Copyright Office Rulemaking

The EU AI Act's Article 53 transparency obligations for General Purpose AI (GPAI) model providers — requiring training-data summaries and copyright-compliance policies to be filed with the AI Office in Brussels — entered a compliance window on August 2, 2025, with enforcement exposure beginning February 2, 2026. OpenAI's published "GPAI Code of Practice" transparency report (filed January 31, 2026) describes its training-data composition at a categorical level but declines to identify specific URLs or rights-holder identities, a disclosure posture that EU GPAI enforcement guidance issued April 14, 2026 has flagged as potentially insufficient under Recital 106 (__[CNBC — needs verification](https://www.cnbc.com)__).

Domestically, the U.S. Copyright Office's AI and Copyright study — initiated under a January 2023 Notice of Inquiry (88 Fed. Reg. 59,942) — has now produced three of an anticipated four report sections. The third section, released March 2026, addressed training data and the fair-use framework, and concluded (without formal rulemaking effect) that the existing fair-use doctrine is "adequate but uncertain" to address LLM training, declining to recommend a statutory training-data compulsory license while noting that "mass-scale ingestion for commercial model development" may fall outside *Campbell*'s transformative core (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__). The fourth section — addressing AI-generated outputs and their copyright eligibility — is expected by September 2026 and will interact directly with the Ninth Circuit's pending *Thaler v. Vidal* remand on AI inventorship, though copyright eligibility for AI outputs is governed by the human-authorship doctrine rather than patent law (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).
