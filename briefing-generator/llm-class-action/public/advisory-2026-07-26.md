## Analysis & Developments

**The week's defining event is the entry of final judgment in *Bartz v. Anthropic*.** The settlement — previously in the "final approval pending" posture tracked here — is now closed. On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the landmark $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 The consequence for claimants and rights-holders monitoring this docket: the case has exited the litigation phase entirely. Distribution timing is now the operative question.

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Judgment Entered; Distribution Clock Running

The approval order is the structural endpoint that creditor-side claim investors have been waiting on. 
Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin called the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," and overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds.
 The court's treatment of objectors from non-class members is worth noting for downstream litigation strategy: 
the court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but took care to remind those individuals that, precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.


The claims and opt-out numbers confirm a remarkably high-participation settlement. 
In aggregate, this decision marks the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77 percent.
 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate, and 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works, or .03% of the total number of works in the class.
 At those participation levels, virtually no eligible work escapes the fund's reach, which bears directly on the universe of independent actions that could have been reserved.

The class definition, which controls who receives payment, covers copyright owners of books 
downloaded by Anthropic through Library Genesis (LibGen) in June 2021 or from Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi) in July 2022, with an ISBN or Amazon Standard Identification Number (ASIN), registered with the U.S. Copyright Office within five years of first publication, and registered before being downloaded by Anthropic, or within three months of first publication.
 The 17 U.S.C. § 411 registration prerequisite — one of the more litigated threshold questions in the early AI copyright cases — is embedded directly in the class definition.

**The fee award is the discrete delta from the final approval order.** 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then later trimmed it to $187.5 million. The court approved approximately $101.5 million in fees.
 
$122 million of the settlement money will go toward attorneys and litigation costs in total.
 The approved fee represents a significant reduction from both the original ask and the reduced request — the court's lodestar cross-check apparently driven the outcome down materially. The per-work distribution figure that has been publicly cited is instructive for comparative purposes: 
*Bartz v. Anthropic* will pay "about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works," with claims filed for 440,490 works that were downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror by Anthropic.


**Distribution remains unscheduled as of today.** 
The Authors Guild looks forward to the commencement of the distribution process so that authors can start getting paid; while a specific date for commencement hasn't been announced, the expectation is that it will happen quickly.
 The settlement administrator — JND Legal Administration, operating as the Anthropic Copyright Settlement Administrator — now controls the distribution timeline. Any Special Master disputes remain confidential under seal. 
The court reaffirmed that any disputes that "cannot be resolved by mutual agreement with the settlement administrator" will be submitted to the Special Master, and that all submissions "should remain confidential and under seal."


One structural consequence of the final judgment for rights-holders outside the class: 
existing independent actions (e.g., the Chicken Soup for the Soul and Carreyrou suits) remain separate, individual actions, and the court's order in approving the settlement may prompt others to follow.
 The Bartz settlement does not resolve the training-use fair-use question for claimants operating outside the LibGen/PiLiMi sourcing parameters — Judge Alsup's earlier fair-use ruling (training on lawfully acquired books = fair use; training on pirated copies = not) travels forward into every parallel matter. 
The legal theory in follow-on music publisher cases leans on the June 23, 2025 *Bartz v. Anthropic* summary judgment ruling, which held that training on lawfully acquired books can be fair use but that sourcing from pirate libraries is not protected.

### II. *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.) — Cross-MSJ Fully Briefed; Hearing Scheduled October 21, 2026

The first music-publisher copyright suit against Anthropic is now at full summary judgment posture before Judge Eumi K. Lee in Courtroom 7, San Jose. This is the next consequential fair-use ruling in the AI copyright docket — the first to squarely present the lyrics-reproduction and lyrics-training questions that books cases sidestepped.


The summary judgment hearing in No. 5:24-cv-03811 is scheduled before Judge Eumi Lee on October 21, 2026. The questions presented are: (1) should partial summary judgment on copyright infringement be granted to plaintiffs? and (2) should summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement be granted to defendant?
 Both sides have filed cross-motions, making this a binary ruling: either the publishers win on infringement (§ 17 U.S.C. § 501 direct copying, both training-data ingestion and output reproduction) or Anthropic wins on fair use (§ 107). There is no middle path that avoids a substantive fair-use holding.


The music publishers have argued for partial summary judgment that fair use does not excuse Anthropic's conduct because it used the copyrighted lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 That framing specifically targets the § 107(1) "commercial nature" prong and the § 107(4) "market harm" prong — the same two factors where AI defendants have faced the most factual exposure in prior cases. Anthropic's cross-motion for summary judgment, filed in late March 2026, asserts fair use on the training-ingestion side.

The amicus posture is notable. 
The Chamber of Progress and Engine Advocacy have moved for leave to file an amicus brief in support of Anthropic's motion for partial summary judgment and in opposition to plaintiffs' motion.
 Copyright law professors have filed separately in support of Anthropic. The volume of amicus interest signals that the industry treats this ruling as the first substantial appellate-path candidate on lyrics/output reproduction — the category of AI copyright harm that books cases never definitively resolved.

There is also a significant collateral issue pending within the docket: 
a California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
Music publishers suing Anthropic accused the company of downplaying the seriousness of errors in a filing caused by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool, saying the company's counsel violated a judge's standing order and arguing that the filing should be tossed.
 The reliability of AI-generated expert work product is now an independent evidentiary issue in the case, with implications for how both sides can deploy AI-assisted analysis at the October hearing.

**The parallel Concord II action** — filed as a separate $3 billion suit for sheet music and songbooks — has survived Anthropic's motion to stay. 
The publishers allege Anthropic obtained more than 20,000 musical works from pirate shadow libraries for training and seek roughly $3 billion; on April 24, 2026, the court denied Anthropic's motion to stay Concord II pending Concord I, and Anthropic's motion to dismiss or answer is due August 3, 2026.
 The Concord II case, No. 5:26-cv-00880, is also before Judge Lee and referred to Magistrate Judge Susan van Keulen for discovery. The aggregate Anthropic-music-publisher exposure across both dockets now sits at roughly $3 billion-plus, with no class certification path limiting the universe — these are non-class publisher actions where 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) statutory damages remain in play.

### III. *NYT v. OpenAI/Microsoft* (S.D.N.Y.) — Sanctions Motion; Discovery Misconduct Allegations

The NYT-led consolidated publisher action in the Southern District of New York has escalated sharply on discovery. 
A group of newspapers including the New York Times and New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan to sanction OpenAI in their high-stakes copyright dispute for allegedly lying to the court about its ability to search its systems for proof that it misused millions of their articles in AI training.
 
The newspapers told the court that OpenAI falsely represented it could not search its large language models for their copyrighted material while hiding that it had done so "even before the first News Plaintiff filed suit," and that OpenAI had deleted billions of relevant ChatGPT conversations or made them unsearchable. The newspapers asked for sanctions including attorneys' fees and a court finding that OpenAI's chat logs showed misuse of their copyrighted works.


This motion — filed July 9, 2026 — is a direct consequence of the January 5, 2026 production order. 
Judge Sidney Stein ordered OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs in the consolidated S.D.N.Y. MDL on January 5, 2026 — described as the most consequential discovery order in any AI copyright case.
 The sanctions motion alleges that OpenAI's representations regarding searchability were made in the context of resisting that production order. If the court grants even a partial adverse-inference sanction, it effectively prejudges the market-harm prong of the § 107(4) analysis in favor of the publishers — a significant litigation accelerant.

### IV. *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* (D. Del., No. 1:20-cv-00613) — Third Circuit Appeal Pending

The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling on AI-training fair use is now the most significant legal event pending in the entire AI copyright system. 
Three U.S. judges have ruled on AI training and fair use: Alsup (Bartz), Chhabria (Kadrey), and Bibas (Thomson Reuters v. Ross). Alsup and Chhabria found for AI companies on training; Bibas found against. The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling — argued June 11, 2026 — is pending.
 
The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that the Westlaw headnotes were original and protected and that Ross Intelligence's use of those headnotes to train its AI legal research tool was not fair use. The case is currently on appeal before the Third Circuit.


The circuit split dynamic is already operating at the district level: Alsup and Chhabria both found training fair use; Bibas found the opposite. The Third Circuit panel's decision — which could arrive any time from late summer through early 2027 — will be the first circuit-level precedent on whether AI training use qualifies as fair use under § 107. Whatever the result, it frames the briefing in *Concord I*, in the OpenAI MDL, and in every case where training-data sourcing is at issue. Rights-holder counsel monitoring *Concord I*'s October 21 hearing should plan on submitting 28 U.S.C. § 1746 notices once the Third Circuit issues.

### V. International: Divergence Widens

The international fair-use equivalent landscape has hardened into a genuine doctrinal split. 
The UK High Court ruled for Stability AI in *Getty v. Stability AI* (November 2025). The Munich Regional Court ruled against OpenAI in *GEMA v. OpenAI* (November 2025). The same technology, trained on the same type of content, produced opposite first-instance verdicts in two legal systems within days of each other.
 The EU AI Act's text-and-data mining exception under DSM Directive Art. 4 — which permits TDM for lawful access unless rights-holders have expressly opted out — remains the operative framework in EU jurisdictions and stands in structural tension with the U.S. § 107 analysis. Rights-holders with registered works in EU member states should be separately tracking the Art. 4 opt-out architecture.
