### I. *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence* — Third Circuit Now Under Submission

The Third Circuit on June 11 heard oral argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence* — the first appellate review of a decision on whether the use of copyrighted works to train an AI model constitutes fair use. The appeal, docketed as No. 25-2153, is drawn from the District of Delaware at docket 1:20-cv-00613. Counsel for ROSS Intelligence encountered a barrage of probing questions from the panel, which expressed skepticism over the company's bid to overturn the AI-training fair-use decision against it in the case over copied Westlaw headnotes. Although counsel for Westlaw owner Thomson Reuters also faced critical questions, the panel jumped immediately into questions about the impact of ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes on the market for the legal search engine's work.

The doctrinal stakes are significant. On February 11, 2025, the district court granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that (1) Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and (2) ROSS Intelligence's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use. The district court specifically found that ROSS had infringed 2,243 Westlaw headnotes and upheld the validity of Thomson Reuters's copyrights, with the exception of those that may have expired. On May 23, 2025, the court granted ROSS Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard"; in its appeal brief filed September 2025, ROSS argued that if the decision stands, it will have "sweeping consequences for innovation" in both legal research and artificial intelligence.

Heading into argument, both sides had invoked the Third Circuit's own recent decision in *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc.* as supporting their respective positions. The Third Circuit ordered the parties to file supplemental briefs addressing the impact of the *UpCodes* ruling, and both sides filed those supplemental briefs on May 11 — each arguing that *UpCodes* supports its position. The panel members identified from the docket record include Circuit Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves, and Bové. On the critical fourth-factor market-harm analysis: ROSS argues the district court made a categorical error by treating "legal research platforms" as the relevant market, rather than examining what West actually derives from the specific works at issue, its headnotes — and under *UpCodes*, ROSS says, the court should have looked at the market for headnotes specifically and the percentage of Westlaw revenues attributable to them. ROSS further argues that West conceded it has no evidence of actual market harm — no documented lost subscribers, no lost revenue traceable to ROSS — and that *UpCodes* requires courts to examine the record closely rather than inferring harm from the mere fact of competition. Thomson Reuters counters with record evidence: customers who switched from Westlaw to ROSS, ROSS's CEO acknowledging that switch, and evidence of harm not just to the original Westlaw subscription market but to the emerging market for AI training data — a harm the *UpCodes* panel never had to address (__[Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/health-law-and-business/first-ai-copyright-appeals-reach-hinges-on-market-impact-issue)__).

The non-generative character of ROSS's tool is central to how this decision will travel to broader AI-training cases. The district court emphasized that ROSS's tool is not generative AI, finding that it does not write legal research memos or opinions and is merely a search tool that identifies cases in response to legal research questions; the factors regarding the nature of the work and the amount used weighed in ROSS's favor, given the narrow scope of protection afforded the headnotes and the fact that they did not appear to ROSS's end users. The case differs from the more sprawling copyright lawsuits against Anthropic, Meta, and OpenAI in a number of ways: its AI is non-generative and uses relatively low-creativity legal summaries. This means a Third Circuit ruling adverse to ROSS — particularly if it foregrounds the commercial-market-displacement theory — could map onto generative-AI defendants precisely because those systems produce outputs that more directly substitute for the licensed originals.

The *UpCodes* structural question is the live interpretive hinge. Whether the *UpCodes* reasoning extends to AI training on editorial legal content, or whether it is restricted to incorporated-standards cases, is exactly the question the Third Circuit now has to answer. Thomson Reuters's invocation of *Bartz* adds a cross-circuit dimension: TR invoked the California district court's ruling in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* for the proposition that using a proprietary system for finding court opinions to create a competing AI tool for finding court opinions "was not transformative." A decision from the panel is expected within 90 days of argument, though the scope — whether the Third Circuit resolves the copyrightability question alongside fair use, or limits itself to the § 107 factors — remains open.

### II. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* — Distribution Calculation Date Passed; Final Approval Still Pending

Per the schedule established in the case, June 11, 2026 was the date on which the settlement administrator was scheduled to calculate distributions, subject to change. The case, filed August 19, 2024 in the Northern District of California at docket 3:24-cv-05417, carries the $1.5 billion settlement — negotiated in August 2025 and described by multiple practitioners as the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history. That distribution-calculation date has now passed with the final approval order still outstanding, which keeps disbursement timing contingent on entry of the order and the resolution of any appeal.

The precise current posture: after a 75-minute hearing held May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, presiding judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly (__[Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/digital/copyright/article/100438-little-drama-at-anthropic-s-settlement-hearing.html)__). Judge Martínez-Olguín took the motions under submission and ordered: by no later than May 21, 2026, Anthropic may file a supplemental brief of no more than 2 pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; by that date, plaintiffs shall file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 [ECF 646-1]. The most recent docket activity is consistent with the matter remaining under submission awaiting the final approval order.

The claims economics are now fully crystallized. Class counsel filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10%) (__[Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/anthropic-settlement-update-91-percent-of-books-claimed/)__). Class counsel Justin Nelson confirmed at the May 14 hearing that the payout per work in the class will be $3,100. Class counsel urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million. Named plaintiff service awards are set at $50,000 per plaintiff ($150,000 total for the three class representatives), and settlement administration is handled by JND Legal Administration. Anthropic is funding the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.

A structural caveat bears emphasis for rights-holders monitoring payment timing. Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved. The class-definition scope, confirmed at preliminary approval and re-affirmed at the May 14 hearing, is limited. The court certified a class of all rightsholders of books Anthropic acquired from LibGen and PiLiMi, provided the books were registered with the U.S. Copyright Office in a timely manner and have ISBN or ASIN numbers; the class was certified only for piracy, not the act of AI training, and as such the fair-use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers.

The opt-out litigation vector is already live. Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. at docket 3:26-cv-04482, demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works. A follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.

### III. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* — Cross-MSJ Posture; Awaiting Docket Sync

*Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC* (M.D. Tenn. → N.D. Cal.) remains in cross-motion-for-summary-judgment posture, with Anthropic's cross-motion for summary judgment on fair use recorded as the last known public filing (April 22, 2026). The music-publisher AI-training track is analytically distinct from *Bartz* in one critical respect: the class in *Bartz* was certified only on the piracy-from-shadow-libraries theory, whereas *Concord* challenges AI training on licensed and commercially distributed musical compositions — placing the transformativeness inquiry directly in contest for content that was never housed in LibGen. The Anthropic fair-use cross-motion argues that its Claude training on lyrics and other musical text is transformative under the *Warhol* framework, a position that the Third Circuit's forthcoming *ROSS* decision will either complicate or lend support to, depending on how the panel characterizes market-displacement analysis. A class-notice milestone is calendared for July 8 in the related music-publisher track.

### IV. Broader AI-Copyright Ecosystem: Opt-Out Plaintiffs, Parallel Actions, and the § 1201 Dimension

For many authors implicated in *Bartz*, the litigation around AI training and book piracy is not ending; it is shifting to other cases — there are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages, including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others. Several of those cases appear to be adopting the strategy that proved effective in *Bartz*: rather than challenging AI training as such, they focus on the underlying piracy and on the shadow-library-ingestion pipeline as a point of no-controversy copying. This litigation architecture is significant because it allows plaintiffs to sidestep the contested transformativeness question that will be resolved in *ROSS* and instead anchor claims in reproduction of entire works from criminal piracy repositories — conduct that presents no viable fair-use defense at the § 107(1) purpose-and-character step. Disney v. Midjourney class-certification briefing in the C.D. Cal. image-model track is calendared for June 30, widening the front from text to image-generation training data.

The *ROSS* panel's skepticism toward ROSS on the market-harm factor signals a potential doctrinal path that would be devastating for AI-training defendants in the generative category: if the Third Circuit affirms the district court's finding that the fourth fair-use factor (17 U.S.C. § 107(4)) weighs against ROSS because its tool was a direct commercial substitute for Westlaw, that same logic applies with even greater force to general-purpose LLMs that compete across virtually every information-product market simultaneously. A Thomson Reuters affirmance would represent the first published circuit court opinion establishing that AI training on proprietary editorial content fails the fair-use defense — and would be immediately citable by plaintiffs in the consolidated OpenAI and Meta actions pending in the N.D. Cal. and S.D.N.Y.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and recovery counsel should take the following steps in light of the current posture: (1) Monitor the CourtListener docket for *Bartz* (No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) daily for entry of the final approval order from Judge Martínez-Olguín — its entry triggers both the 30-day appeal window under Fed. R. App. P. 4(a)(1) and the formal commencement of JND's distribution process; rights-holders with multiple registered works in the claims database should confirm that all works are properly attributed given the $3,100 per-work payout rate. (2) Opt-out plaintiffs and counsel in the *Cruz/Eggers/Vida* action (No. 3:26-cv-04482) and *Kwon v. Anthropic* should track the *ROSS* decision closely: an adverse ruling for ROSS on the market-harm factor would substantially strengthen the statutory-damages theory in non-class cases where opt-out plaintiffs can prove individual market displacement and seek the $150,000-per-work ceiling under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2). (3) Publishers and author estates with works in *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* should ensure that counsel has flagged the supplemental-brief framework from *ROSS* — specifically whether Anthropic's fair-use cross-motion engages the *UpCodes* dissemination-of-law carve-out, which Thomson Reuters has moved to confine to public-law incorporated-standards contexts — ahead of the July 8 class-notice milestone. (4) Any rights-holder who did not file a claim in *Bartz* before the March 30, 2026 deadline should now evaluate whether their works are implicated in one of the pending opt-out or parallel actions, as the shadow-library piracy theory remains live in those proceedings independent of the settlement.
