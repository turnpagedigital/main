# 🤖 LLM / Copyright | Friday, June 12, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**DELTA — Yesterday's Main Event: Third Circuit Oral Argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence*, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.)**

The single most consequential LLM-training copyright proceeding in the U.S. federal courts held oral argument in Philadelphia on Thursday, June 11, 2026 — one day ago. The Third Circuit heard argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence*, the first appellate review of whether the use of copyrighted works to train an AI model constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. Argument was calendared for 10:00 a.m. in the Maris Courtroom, Philadelphia. The panel — Circuit Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves, and Bove — took the bench for what Bloomberg Law characterized as a skeptical session for ROSS. Counsel for ROSS Intelligence encountered a barrage of probing questions from Third Circuit judges who expressed skepticism over the company's bid to overturn the AI-training copyright fair-use decision against it in the case over copied Westlaw headnotes. Although Thomson Reuters' counsel also faced critical questions, the panel jumped directly into questions about the impact of ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes on the market for the legal search engine's work.

Judges spent much of Thursday's oral argument probing whether ROSS built a market substitute for Westlaw and whether its use of Thomson Reuters' copyrighted materials was transformative — a focus that could signal a willingness to confront the AI-training fair-use question head on, rather than decide the appeal on narrower copyrightability grounds.

**Background and Procedural Posture.** The underlying district court case is *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence Inc.*, D. Del. No. 1:20-cv-00613. Circuit Judge Stephanos Bibas, sitting by designation in the District of Delaware, initially denied both sides' motions for summary judgment but revised his 2023 opinion in February 2025, finding that Westlaw's headnotes are original and copyrightable works. Judge Bibas granted summary judgment for Thomson Reuters, holding that Westlaw's headnotes are sufficiently original to qualify for copyright protection and that Ross's use of them did not constitute fair use. In May 2025, however, Bibas granted ROSS's motion for interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), acknowledging the two controlling questions were "hard under existing precedent" and noting that resolution would "change the shape of the trial — and possibly avoid a copyright trial altogether." The Third Circuit docketed the appeal as No. 25-2153 after granting the § 1292(b) petition.

**The Two Certified Questions.** ROSS framed its case summary as follows: "Is a short quote or paraphrase of judicial holding copyrightable and does the fair use doctrine protect ROSS's internal use of Westlaw's headnotes in memos that served as training data for an AI legal search engine?" Thomson Reuters framed the question as: "Whether a legal-research company that copied original editorial content from its competitor to build its own commercial product is not liable for copyright infringement because the copied content is not copyrightable or its copying was fair use." Those competing framings telegraphed the argument — ROSS cast its training as internal, functional, and narrow; Thomson Reuters cast it as naked commercial substitution.

**The UpCodes Pivot.** A critical pre-argument procedural development shaped Thursday's session. By text order, the Third Circuit directed supplemental briefing on the impact, if any, of its own recent decision in *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes Inc.*, 2026 WL 935309 (3d Cir. April 7, 2026), on this appeal. Both sides filed their supplemental briefs on May 11, and each argued that *UpCodes* supported its position. Whether the *UpCodes* reasoning extends to AI training on editorial legal content — or whether it is restricted to incorporated-standards cases — is exactly the question the Third Circuit now must answer. ROSS argued that training a machine to understand legal language is analogous to *UpCodes*' public-interest dissemination rationale; ROSS also argued that Thomson Reuters conceded it has no evidence of actual market harm — no documented lost subscribers, no lost revenue traceable to ROSS — and that *UpCodes* requires courts to examine the record closely rather than inferring harm from the mere fact of competition. Thomson Reuters fired back that ROSS's conduct was explicitly commercial and substitutive: TR's account portrays ROSS as a commercial actor that knowingly copied Westlaw to build a rival product; after being denied a Westlaw license, ROSS allegedly hired the outsourcing firm LegalEase Solutions to scrape Westlaw data and convert headnotes into "question and answer" pairs for training its AI model.

**Fourth-Factor Dominance.** The panel's concentration on market harm is legally significant. Under *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), the fourth factor (market effect) is "undoubtedly the single most important element of fair use." ROSS argued on the fourth factor that the district court made a categorical error by treating "legal research platforms" as the relevant market, rather than examining what West actually derives from the specific works at issue — its headnotes — and that under *UpCodes* the court should have looked at the market for headnotes specifically and the percentage of Westlaw revenues attributable to them. Thomson Reuters countered that the record shows customers who switched from Westlaw to ROSS, ROSS's CEO acknowledging that switch, and evidence of harm not just to the original Westlaw subscription market but to the emerging market for AI training data — a harm the *UpCodes* panel never had to address. The panel's focus on this fourth-factor market-harm analysis — rather than the threshold originality question — may indicate the judges intend to reach the fair-use merits and issue an opinion with broad applicability to AI-training cases across the circuit. A decision is expected within three to six months of the June 11 argument date.

**Cross-Case Significance: Bartz Cited Into Thomson Reuters.** Notably, Thomson Reuters invoked the Bartz v. Anthropic fair-use record in its Third Circuit briefs. Thomson Reuters cited a California district court's recent ruling in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* for the proposition that using a proprietary system for finding court opinions to create a competing AI tool for finding court opinions "was not transformative." The cross-pollination of Bartz's § 107 analysis into the Third Circuit's first AI-copyright appellate opinion underscores why the two proceedings are analytically linked: a Thomson Reuters win affirming non-transformativeness would add persuasive weight to any challenge to the Bartz settlement's embedded fair-use assumptions about competitive AI products.

**DELTA — *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, N.D. Cal. No. 3:24-cv-05417: Distribution Calculation Milestone Hits Today**

The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place on Thursday, May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California. After a 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to issue the formal final approval order, but given the tone of the hearing most observers expect final approval relatively quickly. Lead plaintiffs' counsel Justin Nelson updated the court that the opt-in rate had inched up from 91.3% the prior month to 92.77% — a participation rate that is essentially without precedent in consumer copyright class litigation. Class counsel stated the payout per work in the class will be $3,100.

Following the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered: (1) Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages by May 21, 2026, addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; (2) plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026; and (3) the court will consider no further submissions from objectors. Those supplemental filings have now been submitted and the court is poised to issue its written final approval order. As of today, June 11-12, 2026, the settlement administrator is performing per-work and per-claimant distribution calculations pursuant to the plan of allocation. Per the plan of allocation and distribution, the calculation of per-work and per-claimant distributions — assuming no appeal of the final approval order — is scheduled for June 11, 2026. Payment disbursement is expected to begin June 2026 or later, subject to whether appeals are filed.

**Settlement Structure.** Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, distributed in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027. The attorney fee request was revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million. The settlement class encompasses approximately 500,000 titles out of the 7 million copies of books Anthropic reportedly downloaded from LibGen and PiLiMi after accounting for duplicates and non-eligible works. Under the settlement's destruction covenant, Anthropic must destroy all works downloaded from the LibGen or PiLiMi datasets and copies thereof for works for which claims were filed. Critically, settlement participation does not grant Anthropic a license to use a class member's work in the future.

**Key Bartz Antecedent: The Alsup Fair-Use Split.** The Bartz settlement rests on the underlying liability landscape shaped by Judge William Alsup's June 2025 ruling on summary judgment in the N.D. Cal., which found that using books without permission to train AI was fair use if they were acquired legally, but denied Anthropic's summary judgment request related to piracy — finding that piracy was not fair use. Judge Alsup's ruling held that training on legally purchased books is "quintessentially transformative" and a green light; but the massive payout for downloading and storing books from LibGen raises open questions about websites scraped online without obvious permission. That split is the doctrinal fissure driving plaintiff strategy in every subsequent AI-copyright filing: shadow-library allegations (piracy track) are pursued because the transformativeness defense is substantially weakened on those facts, even where the broader fair-use argument for licensed-data training succeeds.

**CARRY-FORWARD — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, N.D. Cal. No. 5:26-cv-00880 (Judge Susan G. Van Keulen)**

The Concord music-publisher action — representing a distinct copyright theory grounded in lyric reproduction and output-level infringement, rather than shadow-library input copying — continues in active status before Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen in the Northern District of California. The docket is currently awaiting its initial substantive filings and no new entries were recorded in the 72-hour scan window. This matter tracks the question whether AI model outputs that reproduce or closely paraphrase song lyrics constitute direct infringement independent of any training-data question — a liability theory that, if accepted, would survive the fair-use analysis that substantially protected Anthropic at the Bartz summary-judgment stage. The distinction matters acutely for music publishers: unlike book authors, who hold reproduction rights over static textual expression acquired at training time, music publishers face an additional output-side theory that Anthropic's Claude model generates lyric reproductions on demand, a claim not resolved by Judge Alsup's ruling on input-side piracy.

Rights-holders tracking this case should monitor for a scheduling conference and any motion practice concerning standing, choice of law between California and Nashville copyright-adjacent venue norms, and Anthropic's expected invocation of the Bartz § 107 fair-use record as precedential authority for its training-data defense. The *Thomson Reuters* Third Circuit decision — expected by fall 2026 — may also furnish doctrinal tools for Anthropic if it frames the lyric-reproduction question as transformative output use rather than verbatim copying.

**MACRO DOCTRINE — The § 107 Landscape Entering Summer 2026**

The three proceedings addressed this week collectively define the operative fault lines in AI-training copyright litigation. First, the *transformativeness axis*: both *Bartz* (at the district level via Judge Alsup) and *Kadrey v. Meta Platforms* found training-set use of legally acquired works to be highly transformative under § 107(1); a law-professor amicus brief filed in the Third Circuit cited both *Bartz* and *Kadrey* for the proposition that "the use of in-copyright materials to train an AI model is a highly transformative fair use." Second, the *commercial-substitution axis*: the Third Circuit's oral argument questions signal the panel may give substantial weight to the § 107(4) factor, potentially overriding transformativeness where the defendant is building a product that directly competes with the plaintiff's commercial offering. Third, the *shadow-library axis*: even where training-data fair use succeeds, piracy liability survives as an independent and potentially catastrophic exposure vector, as Bartz's $1.5 billion resolution demonstrated.

The Bartz settlement has given plaintiffs' lawyers a roadmap — firms like Susman Godfrey have developed what observers call a "shadow library strategy," which skips the debate over whether AI training is fair use and goes straight for the piracy angle; most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies now allege the defendants downloaded from shadow libraries. GCs advising AI developers on training corpus acquisition should treat shadow-library exposure as the primary litigation risk posture entering the second half of 2026, irrespective of the Third Circuit's ruling on ROSS's fair-use defense.

No. 25-2153), counsel advising AI developers should monitor the Third Circuit docket daily: yesterday's oral argument before Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves, and Bove, with its evident focus on § 107(4) market-harm analysis and the question of direct commercial substitution, suggests the court may issue a relatively narrow precedential holding that reaches the fair-use merits while leaving originality questions to the remand; any such ruling will immediately require re-evaluation of AI-training corpus licensing decisions across the legal-tech sector. Cal. 5:26-cv-00880) should prepare for Anthropic to invoke the Bartz § 107 record at the earliest scheduling opportunity and should draft parallel arguments grounded in output-side direct infringement under § 106(1) and § 106(3) that are analytically independent of any training-data fair-use defense.
