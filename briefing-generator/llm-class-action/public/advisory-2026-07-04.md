## Analysis & Developments

### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Imminent; Distribution Clock Running

The controlling development entering this weekend is that Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín has taken the $1.5 billion settlement in *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC* under submission and — as of the last confirmed docket activity on July 1, 2026 (CourtListener docket ID 69058235) — has not yet issued the final approval order. The post-hearing procedural sequence is closed: 
following the May 14 fairness hearing, the court ordered Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, with a deadline of May 21, 2026.
 
Plaintiffs were simultaneously required to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the April 30, 2026 proposed order; the court stated it would not require anything further from objectors and would consider no additional submissions from them.
 Both deadlines passed over six weeks ago. The docket shows one new filing dated July 1, 2026, but its description is not publicly enumerated in the CourtListener sync — the substantive nature of that entry is unverified and should be confirmed directly via PACER before acting on it.

The settlement metrics as entered into the record are decisive. 
Lead counsel Justin Nelson reported at the May 14 hearing that the opt-in rate had grown to 92.77% of covered works, with no indication that the per-work payout of approximately $3,000 to $3,100 would change.
 
Attorneys told the court that the opt-out and objection rates were "minuscule," a strong sign that the deal meets the standard of being "fair, reasonable, and adequate."
 
After a 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined to approve the settlement from the bench, but most observers expect final approval relatively quickly given the tone of the hearing.


The financial structure of the fund warrants precision. 
Plaintiffs' counsel — Lieff Cabraser Heimann & Bernstein and Susman Godfrey — are seeking 25% of the fund, or $375 million in attorneys' fees, with service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff and settlement administration by JND expected to cost several million dollars.
 
Anthropic is funding the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Anthropic is paying into the fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.
 That installment structure means the September 25, 2026 tranche — the third of four — will not land until after distribution calculations are complete, compressing the administrator's window between final approval and first distribution.

The release perimeter matters for rights-holders evaluating post-settlement exposure. 
Participating in the settlement does not give Anthropic a license to use works in the future, and the release does not apply to anything Anthropic does after August 26, 2025, or to claims related to infringing outputs from Anthropic's AI models.
 That carve-out is the litigation seam: output-side claims — the theory that Claude reproduces copyrighted material in responses — remain live for any class member who filed a claim, and they are the engine driving Concord II (addressed below).

The § 107 fair-use framework established in this docket by Judge William Alsup (who handled preliminary approval before reassignment) is worth holding. 
In June 2025, Judge Alsup ruled on summary judgment that using books without permission to train AI was fair use if they were acquired legally, but he denied Anthropic's request for summary judgment related to piracy — finding the piracy itself was not fair use.
 That bifurcation — lawful acquisition = fair use, piracy = not — is the doctrinal foundation the Bartz settlement was built on, and it is the dividing line the Concord II complaint is designed to exploit.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee) — Concord II Piracy Theory Advances; Cross-MSJ Posture

The CourtListener docket for the original Concord action (CourtListener docket 68889092) was last updated June 22, 2026. 
The case is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, with referral to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen.
 The cross-MSJ posture on the original 499-composition claim set (Concord I, covering lyrics reproduction in Claude outputs) remains pending; no order has been publicly reported since the tracked-case ground truth's April 22, 2026 Anthropic cross-motion for summary judgment on fair use.

The more aggressive litigation vector is *Concord II*: the January 28, 2026 complaint filed by Concord Music Group, Universal Music Corp., and ABKCO Music against Anthropic PBC, CEO Dario Amodei, and co-founder Benjamin Mann. 
That complaint is framed as a second, distinct action and centers on two categories of alleged wrongdoing: (1) BitTorrent-based downloading of millions of books from LibGen and PiLiMi, including songbooks and sheet-music and lyrics books containing at least 714 publisher-controlled musical works listed in Exhibit A; and (2) post–Concord I scraping and copying to train newer Claude models, allegedly involving 20,517 publisher works in Exhibit B.
 
The complaint also asserts output-side infringement and DMCA § 1202 copyright management information (CMI) removal theories.


The individual-defendant theory is notable. 
The suit names Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann as defendants.
 
The complaint alleges scale — approximately five million books from LibGen and two million from PiLiMi — and attributes leadership awareness, including internal descriptions of LibGen as a "blatant violation of copyright" and messages about Z-Library mirroring, with the allegation that Amodei acknowledged legal purchase options but chose torrenting to avoid a "legal/practice/business slog."


The procedural origin of Concord II is important for assessing its litigation risk profile. 
Through the Bartz discovery process, the publishers say they learned that Anthropic had illegally downloaded thousands of their works; they then tried to amend Concord I to address the piracy claims, but the court denied that motion in October 2025, ruling they had failed to investigate the piracy claims earlier.
 The denial-to-amend forced the fresh filing. 
The plaintiffs are explicit that this lawsuit exists because the prior case could not be amended without fundamentally transforming it, so they carved out the torrenting as a clean, high-clarity claim set.


The damages theory is oriented toward §§ 504 and 1202. 
The publishers identified 714 works in Exhibit A — including compositions such as "Wild Horses," "Sweet Caroline," "Bennie and the Jets," and "Eye of the Tiger" — and an additional 20,517 works in Exhibit B, alleging Anthropic unlawfully torrented these works to build a vast central library of written texts.
 Statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504 for willful infringement run up to $150,000 per work; at that ceiling, the 714 Exhibit A works alone represent $107.1 million in potential exposure, and the 20,517 Exhibit B works represent exposure in excess of $3 billion — figures that explain the TechCrunch headline rounding to $3B and the individual-defendant naming as a deterrence and settlement-pressure tactic. 
The monetization theory is unusually concrete: API pay-per-word revenues are tied to lyric prompts and outputs, bolstering vicarious-liability "direct financial benefit" allegations, with requested relief including an accounting of training data and methods — a discovery and transparency lever that, if granted, can reshape negotiating power far beyond this one case.

### Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. ROSS Intelligence Inc., No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.) — First Federal Appellate AI-Copyright Argument Heard; Decision Pending

The docket of record for this beat has moved to the Third Circuit. 
The Third Circuit heard argument on June 11, 2026, in *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. ROSS Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153 — the first federal appellate argument on whether using copyrighted material to train an AI system can be fair use.
 No decision has been issued as of the date of this briefing. The panel's ruling, when it comes, will be the first appellate-level § 107 fair-use framework applied to AI training data and will carry persuasive (and potentially precedential) weight in the Ninth Circuit cases tracked above.

The district court posture below: 
on February 11, 2025, the Delaware district court granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and that Ross Intelligence's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use.
 
On May 23, 2025, the court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal.


The appellate argument has two live doctrinal threads. 
ROSS tried to use the Third Circuit's recent decision in *American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes*, issued April 7, 2026, as a fresh doctrinal lever — arguing that decision confirms that law-adjacent factual and functional works receive a fair-use analysis sensitive to public access and thin protection.
 
Thomson Reuters frames the case as a straightforward instance of commercial copying to build a substitute, a framing that aligns with judicial instinct that fair use should not bless a competitor's decision to take protected editorial work after being unable to license it.


The structural asymmetry with *Bartz* is worth naming explicitly. The *Alsup* fair-use ruling (lawful acquisition = fair use for LLM training) and the *Ross* district court ruling (unlicensed, competitive copying of curated editorial content = infringement) are not in conflict — they track the § 107(4) market-harm factor in opposite directions: diffuse, transformative, non-competitive use vs. direct-substitute copying with a failed licensing attempt. The Third Circuit's treatment of "transformative use" in the context of intermediate copying — where the headnotes never appeared to end users — is the question most likely to generate circuit guidance applicable across the LLM docket. 
The Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* on March 2, 2026, reaffirming human authorship as a foundational requirement of U.S. copyright law
 — but that ruling does not touch the training-data question and should not be conflated with it.

### Parallel Docket Notes — In re OpenAI Copyright MDL (N.D. Cal., No. 3143); NYT v. OpenAI/Microsoft

*In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation* (MDL No. 3143, N.D. Cal.) continues on a parallel track. No new court-order developments have been verified this cycle. The most recent confirmed discovery development from this docket remains the November 2025 Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang order requiring OpenAI to produce approximately 20 million ChatGPT logs — 
a stark reminder that product logs can become discoverable evidence in AI litigation.
 That order is directly relevant to the Concord II "output-side" theory: if ChatGPT log production is the discovery template, a similar request for Claude output logs covering lyric-reproduction events is a near-certain motion in Concord II once discovery opens.

## Recommended Actions

Claims investors and rights-holder counsel should treat the next thirty days as the critical window in *Bartz*: the final approval order is overdue and could land any court-business day; any objector seeking Ninth Circuit review must file a notice of appeal within 30 days of entry of that order under Fed. R. App. P. 4(a)(1)(B), meaning the appellate challenge window is short and the downstream effect on distribution timing — which the settlement administrator has pegged to a June 11, 2026 distribution-calculation date, now already passed — is compressible. Rights-holders with works in both the *Bartz* class and the Concord II Exhibit B universe should audit their claim posture carefully: the *Bartz* release is limited to pre-August 26, 2025 conduct and piracy-related training claims, leaving output-side infringement and post-cutoff training claims fully open. On the *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS* appeal (No. 25-2153, 3d Cir.), decision timing is indeterminate but the panel's treatment of "transformative use" for intermediate copies — headnotes that never appeared in model outputs — will be the most useful doctrinal signal since *Alsup*'s June 2025 training/fair-use ruling; monitor the Third Circuit's docket for the opinion drop and flag it immediately, as it is the first appellate ruling that will need to be harmonized or distinguished across all active N.D. Cal. LLM matters. For Concord II specifically, the individual-defendant naming of Amodei and Mann and the requested training-data accounting order make this the highest-leverage discovery fight in the music/AI space; watch for Anthropic's motion-to-dismiss briefing, which will test whether officer liability attaches under secondary-infringement theories at the pleading stage.
