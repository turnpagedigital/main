### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 — Final Approval Pending; Distribution Clock Running

The structural history of this matter remains as briefed. 
Judge William Alsup ruled in June 2025 that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and also concluded that Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired.
 
He simultaneously ruled, however, that Anthropic's creation and retention of a "central library" comprised in part of pirated works was not transformative and constituted infringement.
 
Because statutory copyright damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504 can reach $150,000 per infringed work, the exposure theoretically extended into the hundreds of billions of dollars, raising existential risk for Anthropic.
 That litigation posture produced the settlement.


The fairness hearing took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement.
 
After a 75-minute hearing in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect she will give final approval relatively quickly.
 
The hearing was one of the last major procedural steps before the court decides whether to grant final approval; the Judge took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench, and indicated that short orders may follow.


The post-hearing procedural record is precise. 
By the order signed May 14, Anthropic was permitted to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages by May 21 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; by the same date, plaintiffs were to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 at Dkt. 646-1.
 
The court stated it would not require anything further from objectors nor consider further submissions from them.
 No final approval order has posted to CourtListener as of June 18, 2026 (docket last updated June 12, 2026). (__[CourtListener, Bartz v. Anthropic PBC, Dkt. 69058235](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69058235/bartz-v-anthropic-pbc/)__)

**Fee reduction.** 
Plaintiff attorneys reduced their fee request from $300 million to $187.5 million after pushback, meaning more of the settlement fund flows to authors; individual eligible authors expect approximately $3,000 per work.
 The original ask had been 25% of fund, or $375 million. (__[Kluwer Copyright Blog / Wolters Kluwer, Nov. 10, 2025](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-understanding-americas-largest-copyright-settlement/)__) The reduced fee, if adopted by the court, materially improves per-work recovery from the originally modeled figures.

**Claims participation.** 
Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19 motion for final approval filed 11 days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 
Class Counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work will be $3,100; the class had a 92.77% participation rate.


**Distribution mechanics and payment schedule.** Per the settlement administrator's published timeline, distributions were to be calculated June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later, contingent on final approval and expiration of any appeal window. (__[Authors Guild, Bartz v. Anthropic Settlement Timeline](https://authorsguild.org/advocacy/artificial-intelligence/what-authors-need-to-know-about-the-anthropic-settlement/)__) 
Per the Plan of Allocation and Distribution, Anthropic's $450 million scheduled payment is due September 25, 2026 — 12 months after the preliminary approval order — with a second $450 million payment due September 25, 2027.
 
If the settlement is not appealed, the earliest class member payments could arrive in June 2026; if there are objectors but no appeals, summer 2026 is the earliest projected window.


**Scope limitation — practitioners must not overread this settlement.** 
The scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood: Bartz is not a settlement about AI training. Judge Alsup ruled that AI training use was fair use, and nothing about the settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
The settlement only releases Anthropic from liability for past conduct — specifically, its acquisition, retention, and use of identified pirated works before August 25, 2025 — and does not create any ongoing licensing scheme for future AI training, cover claims based on AI outputs, or affect Anthropic's ability to train on lawfully acquired materials.
 Rights-holder counsel advising on future claims — including AI-output reproduction and systematic text-and-data mining under 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) market-harm analysis — must separate the piracy-derived settlement from the unresolved training-use question.

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (Concord I), No. 5:24-cv-03811 — Cross-MSJ Fully Briefed; Fair-Use Ruling Now Imminent

This is the most consequential live merits question in the AI-copyright docket. 
Plaintiffs — a group of music publishers led by Concord Music Group Inc. and Universal Music Corp. — brought claims for direct and secondary copyright infringement and removal of copyright management information (CMI) against Anthropic PBC, alleging it infringes their exclusive rights to musical lyrics by using those works to train Claude and by distributing lyrics in Claude's outputs when prompted.
 The case is before Judge Eumi K. Lee, N.D. Cal., with Magistrate Judge Susan van Keulen handling discovery. (__[CourtListener, Concord Music Group v. Anthropic PBC, No. 5:24-cv-03811](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/68889092/concord-music-group-inc-v-anthropic-pbc/)__)

Anthropic's fair-use theory in this matter is structurally distinct from the piracy-derived liability resolved in *Bartz*. 
Anthropic claims fair use of the publishers' copyrighted lyrics when used to train its AI model, citing judgments where courts have held that making "intermediate" copies of copyrighted materials to develop new technologies does not violate copyright law.
 The publishers respond by documenting an established licensing market. 
The broader music industry has moved rapidly to establish licensed AI partnerships — creating precisely the kind of market infrastructure that plaintiffs argue Anthropic is undermining; an amicus brief from the Association of American Publishers presented an extensive chart of AI licensing deals for textual works involving OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and Perplexity.
 That licensing-market evidence is the publishers' core 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) argument: the existence of a functioning market for AI-training licenses forecloses a fair-use finding.


The RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, SONA, BMAC, the Music Artists Coalition, and the Artist Rights Alliance filed an amicus brief on March 30 urging the court to reject Anthropic's fair use defense.
 
That brief characterizes Anthropic as a "holdout" that has opted to scrape copyrighted content rather than license it like its peers.
 
Dispositive motions were to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026 under the court's scheduling order at Concord I Dkt. 587.
 Judge Lee now has the cross-MSJ under consideration. A ruling against Anthropic on training-use fair use in this posture — reaching a different conclusion than Judge Chhabria in *Kadrey v. Meta*, 3:23-cv-03417 (N.D. Cal., fair use for training upheld June 25, 2025) — would create an intra-district split and materially reshape the merits risk profile across the entire AI-copyright docket.

**Concord II escalation.** A second, far larger suit was filed January 28, 2026: *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal., Judge Lee), covering more than 20,000 songs and seeking over $3 billion in statutory damages. (__[CourtListener, No. 5:26-cv-00880](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/72199828/concord-music-group-inc-v-anthropic-pbc/)__; __[Music Business Worldwide, Apr. 1, 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)__) 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay Concord II, with a motion hearing set for May 20, 2026, before Judge Lee in San Jose Courtroom 7.
 
Anthropic's stay motion argues that Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment on threshold legal issues — including whether use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — that will determine the viability of most or all of publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect; Concord II should be stayed so that claims in both cases are resolved in an orderly and consistent manner.
 The outcome of the stay motion for Concord II is unverified in the current run; no post-May 20 docket entry has been confirmed.

### III. *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein) — Discovery Production Phase


The MDL docket was last updated June 16, 2026; it is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein with referral to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang in the Southern District of New York.
 
The consolidated MDL brings together 16 copyright lawsuits against OpenAI, with lead cases including The New York Times (filed December 27, 2023), the New York Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting.


The operative 2026 development remains the discovery order. 
On January 5, 2026, Judge Sidney Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20 million-log sample of anonymized ChatGPT conversations — not just the cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' works that OpenAI had proposed to produce.
 
OpenAI had objected, arguing the full dataset was unduly burdensome and risked exposing user data, and proposed instead searching only for conversations referencing plaintiffs' works; Stein rejected this, noting that no case law mandates the "least burdensome" discovery method.
 
OpenAI had sought to limit production to conversations mentioning plaintiffs' specific works; legal analysts suggest the logs — once analyzed by plaintiffs' experts — will likely determine settlement terms across the consolidated litigation, as they may reveal patterns of systematic output that mirrors copyrighted training content.


On the pleadings front, Judge Stein has granted OpenAI's motion to stay as to certain newer model generations. 
OpenAI's motion to stay was granted as to models not already in the MDL — specifically the o1, o1 mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 models.
 This limits near-term discovery scope but does not foreclose future claims as those models enter broader deployment. The court also sustained a DMCA § 1202(b) claim: 
the court held that the First Amended Complaint alleges a harm bearing a close relationship to traditional copyright infringement, satisfying the Article III injury-in-fact requirement, and that OpenAI removed CMI from copies of Ziff Davis's works with actual or constructive knowledge that such removal could facilitate end-user infringement.

### IV. Fair-Use Architecture — The Diverging District-Level Framework

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should hold this framework clearly. Two N.D. Cal. judges reached the same result on training-use fair use but through different reasoning, while Concord I now presents the first meaningful opportunity for judicial divergence:

- **Judge Alsup** (*Bartz*, N.D. Cal.): Training on lawfully acquired books = "quintessentially transformative," fair use. Training on pirated source copies = infringement. Settlement does not disturb the training-use ruling. (__[Norton Rose Fulbright, Inside Tech Law, Sep. 5, 2025](https://www.insidetechlaw.com/blog/2025/09/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-reached-after-landmark-summary-judgment-and-class-certification)__)
- 
**Judge Chhabria** (*Kadrey v. Meta*, 3:23-cv-03417, N.D. Cal.) squarely held that LLM training constitutes fair use regardless of whether the underlying materials were obtained from legitimate sources or shadow libraries.

- **Judge Lee** (*Concord I*, N.D. Cal.): Cross-MSJ now under submission. The distinguishing facts are (a) lyrics, not books — shorter works whose market value is directly bound to reproduction and display; (b) Claude's actual output of full lyric text to users; and (c) a documented, functioning licensing market for AI training across major tech companies. 
In her earlier preliminary-injunction ruling, Judge Lee stated that the publishers' argument essentially sought a judicial definition of a licensing market for AI training where the threshold question of fair use remains unsettled
 — a framing she may revisit now that the market is substantially more developed.

The § 107(4) market-harm factor is where Concord I diverges most sharply from *Bartz* and *Kadrey*. The amicus record now before Judge Lee documents dozens of executed AI-training licenses across the publishing and news industries, directly undermining any argument that no market exists for the defendant to have displaced.

The reduced fee award in *Bartz* (from $375M to $187.5M, if adopted) is precedent worth tracking for fee negotiations in the OpenAI MDL when that case moves toward resolution. In the MDL (No. 1:25-md-03143, S.D.N.Y.), analysis of the 20 million ChatGPT logs is the discovery phase most likely to drive the next major development; any expert report tying log outputs to specific copyrighted works will be the first material event to follow.
