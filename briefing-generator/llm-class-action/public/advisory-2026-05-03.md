# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | May 3, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The Bartz v. Anthropic fairness hearing is now eleven days away, and as the May 14 proceeding before Judge Martínez-Olguín approaches, the broader AI copyright docket is converging on a summer of first-impression rulings that will define the contours of fair use for generative AI training across multiple media. The Sony Music Entertainment v. Suno case in the District of Massachusetts has a summary judgment hearing set for July 2026, with Sony declining to settle with either Suno or Udio — in contrast to Warner Music's November 2025 settlement with Suno and UMG's October 2025 deal with Udio — positioning this as the first federal fair-use determination on AI music generation. ([Chartlex](https://www.chartlex.com/blog/business/music-industry-ai-lawsuits-tracker-2026)) Suno's licensing negotiations with remaining major labels have reportedly stalled over download-related revenue sharing, with the company maintaining its position that training generative models on copyrighted recordings constitutes transformative fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107 — the same argument that Anthropic's Concord v. Anthropic cross-motions tee up in the lyric-reproduction context, where competing summary judgment briefing is complete and a ruling is expected this summer. ([Winbuzzer](https://winbuzzer.com/2026/04/09/suno-licensing-talks-with-labels-stall-over-ai-music-downloa-xcxwbn/)) A ruling against Suno on fair use would force every AI music company to license training data or shut down; a ruling for Suno would gut the labels' negotiating leverage and reset the licensing market overnight, with ripple effects across the literary and visual-arts dockets where similar fair-use theories are in play.

In the OpenAI MDL (No. 3143, S.D.N.Y.), discovery continues to expand dramatically. On March 9, 2026, Magistrate Judge Wang granted plaintiffs' motion to compel production of reservoirs of 78 million and 10 million additional ChatGPT logs — on top of the 20 million log sample already ordered on January 5 — constituting the most sweeping compelled disclosure of AI system outputs in any copyright proceeding to date. ([ChatGPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/03/10/court-orders-openai-to-turn-over-greg-brockmans-journal-to-plaintiffs-in-mdl-copyright-suit-plus-oai-employee-vinnie-monaco-was-unprepared-for-deposition-re-project-giraffe-guardrails/)) The following day, the court ordered production of co-founder Greg Brockman's personal journal — a document that first surfaced in the Musk v. OpenAI fraud litigation — and noted that OpenAI employee Vinnie Monaco was insufficiently prepared for his Rule 30(b)(6) deposition regarding "Project Giraffe," OpenAI's internal initiative to prevent ChatGPT from reproducing copyrighted training material verbatim. The Brockman journal and Project Giraffe depositions bear directly on the plaintiffs' theory that OpenAI knew its outputs infringed and deliberately designed inadequate guardrails, a theory that, if substantiated, could support claims for willful infringement and enhanced statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2) — up to $150,000 per work rather than the standard $30,000 ceiling.

The Supreme Court's March 2, 2026 denial of certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter has settled one significant question in the AI copyright landscape: works generated entirely by artificial intelligence, without meaningful human creative contribution, cannot receive copyright protection under the Copyright Act. ([Baker Donelson](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/supreme-court-denies-certiorari-in-thaler-v-perlmutter-ai-cannot-be-an-author-under-the-copyright-act)) ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case)) The cert denial leaves intact both the D.C. Circuit's affirmance and the Copyright Office's longstanding "human authorship" requirement, but crucially does not address the threshold of human involvement necessary to support registration for AI-assisted works — a question that the Copyright Office's pending rulemaking and the ongoing litigation over AI-generated code in Doe v. GitHub will continue to develop. For the Bartz settlement class, the Thaler resolution reinforces the doctrinal framework: the copyrighted works that Anthropic allegedly ingested were indisputably human-authored and thus unquestionably protected, while Anthropic's AI outputs receive no reciprocal copyright protection absent substantial human creative input by the end user.

On the Bartz settlement administration front, $600 million of the $1.5 billion fund has now been disbursed following Anthropic's second $300 million installment on April 30, and the six-plus unsealed objections — including Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's challenge to the distribution plan's alleged publisher favoritism and the group-registration collapse theory — remain the primary contested issues for the May 14 hearing alongside class counsel's $187.5 million fee petition under Rule 23(h). ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) The settlement administrator's distribution computation is scheduled for June 11, the claims closure rate stands at 91.3%, and class members who wish to be heard at the hearing may attend in person at the San Francisco Federal Courthouse (Courtroom 12, 19th Floor) or join via Zoom at 2:00 p.m. PT. The Rule 23(e) fairness factors — adequacy of representation, adequacy of relief, equitable treatment of class members, and the reasonableness of attorney fees — remain the framework against which Judge Martínez-Olguín will evaluate the objections, with the Bishop objection presenting the most substantive challenge to the distribution plan's weighted methodology.

On the legislative front, the NO FAKES Act (H.R. 2794 / S. 1367) continues to advance through the 119th Congress with bipartisan support from Senators Blackburn, Coons, Tillis, and Klobuchar, proposing a federal "digital replication right" extending 70 years post-mortem with $5,000 per-replica statutory damages, while the CLEAR Act (S. 3813) — introduced by Senators Schiff and Curtis with 25-plus endorsing organizations — would mandate that AI developers disclose copyrighted works used in training. ([IP Watchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/02/11/clear-act-establish-notice-requirements-copyrighted-works-ai-training-data/)) ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/3813/text)) The Norton Rose Fulbright 2026 litigation survey identifies AI copyright as the single largest emerging litigation category for corporate legal departments, and the firm's "Six Key Rulings" analysis characterizes the current landscape as one where "no appellate court has yet ruled on whether AI training constitutes fair use" — a gap that the Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence Third Circuit appeal and the Sony v. Suno District of Massachusetts proceedings are each positioned to fill before year-end. ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026))

## Recommended Actions

With the Bartz fairness hearing eleven days out, class members who filed claims should verify their claim status through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and confirm that payment information is current ahead of the June 11 distribution computation — authors whose works were registered under group Copyright Office registrations should be particularly vigilant given the live Bishop objection. AI music companies that have not yet settled with Sony should evaluate whether the July Suno summary judgment hearing creates a licensing window: the stalling of Suno's negotiations suggests that a pre-ruling deal may still be achievable on more favorable terms than a post-adverse-ruling posture would permit, and companies relying on fair-use defenses should assess whether OpenAI's discovery difficulties — particularly the Project Giraffe revelations and the Monaco deposition deficiency — create analogous exposure regarding their own internal guardrail documentation. Rights-holders tracking the OpenAI MDL should note that the 78-million-log production order dramatically expands the universe of potentially infringing outputs that plaintiffs can identify, and any content creator whose works may appear in ChatGPT outputs should consider preservation of evidence and potential intervention before summary judgment briefing commences. Practitioners should calendar the Thomson Reuters v. Ross Third Circuit oral argument and the Sony v. Suno July hearing as the two most likely vehicles for the first fair-use rulings on AI training in 2026.
