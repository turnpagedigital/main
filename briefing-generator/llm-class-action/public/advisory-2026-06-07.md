### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* (N.D. Cal. No. 5:23-cv-03223-AMO) — Discovery Dispute and Class Certification Briefing

The *Bartz* litigation, captioned *Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:23-cv-03223, remains pending before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the San Francisco Division of the Northern District of California (__[Court Docket — PACER](https://www.pacer.gov)__). Plaintiffs — represented by Susman Godfrey and Baker & Hostetler — allege that Anthropic's use of the "Books" corpus to train its Claude large language model constitutes direct copyright infringement of their registered literary works, with secondary claims under 17 U.S.C. § 106(1) (reproduction), § 106(2) (derivative works), and § 1202(b) (removal of copyright management information). Anthropic's fair-use defense under 17 U.S.C. § 107 is the doctrinal heart of the case.

As of the last confirmed docket activity, the parties were engaged in a contested discovery dispute over Anthropic's production of its training-data pipeline documentation — specifically, the provenance records, data-card metadata, and internal legal-review memos associated with the Books corpus ingestion (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Plaintiffs have moved to compel, arguing that Anthropic's privilege assertions over the legal-review memos are overbroad because the memos were prepared in the ordinary course of business as engineering-compliance documents, not in anticipation of litigation — the *Upjohn* / *In re Grand Jury* line. Anthropic counters that the memos reflect counsel's specific legal analysis performed under the direction of outside counsel at O'Melveny & Myers (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

Class certification briefing is the next major procedural milestone. Plaintiffs must establish numerosity (straightforward given the size of the Books corpus), commonality (contested — Anthropic argues each author's claim requires individualized fair-use analysis under *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994)), typicality, and adequacy. The central Rule 23(b)(3) predominance question — whether common issues predominate over individual ones when fair-use is a four-factor, work-by-work inquiry — will likely determine the case's settlement trajectory more than any other single issue. If Judge Martínez-Olguín certifies even a narrow damages class, the economic exposure for Anthropic escalates dramatically (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

Damages theory deserves close attention. Plaintiffs' counsel has indicated a preference for statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c), which allows $750–$30,000 per infringed work (and up to $150,000 for willful infringement). With the Books corpus estimated at several hundred thousand registered literary works, the aggregate statutory damages floor at $750/work would reach at least $225 million, and a willfulness finding could push exposure into the tens of billions — a figure that would dwarf Anthropic's current $18.4 billion valuation (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://www.bloomberg.com)__).

### II. *Getty Images v. Stability AI Ltd.* (D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA; and parallel D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-JLH) — Settlement Posture and Pending Summary Judgment

The *Getty Images* litigation in the District of Delaware — where Getty alleges that Stability AI scraped and trained on approximately 12 million Getty-watermarked images, violating the Copyright Act, the DMCA (17 U.S.C. § 1202), the Lanham Act, and Getty's Terms of Service — has attracted settlement-posture reporting that, if accurate, would represent the first major negotiated resolution in the generative-AI image-training space (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The case was consolidated before Judge Jennifer L. Hall, who denied Stability AI's motion to dismiss in January 2024, holding that Getty's DMCA § 1202(b) claim — based on the trained model's capacity to reproduce degraded versions of the Getty watermark in generated images — stated a plausible claim for relief (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). That ruling was analytically significant because it opened the door to statutory damages of $2,500–$25,000 per § 1202 violation, potentially on a per-image basis. Getty's counsel at Jenner & Block has argued that 12 million images × $2,500 = a $30 billion floor on DMCA exposure alone — a figure that dwarfs Stability AI's balance sheet.

Settlement discussions are reportedly mediated through JAMS, with a retired federal judge presiding (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Key sticking points include: (1) the royalty rate for a prospective licensing structure that would permit Stability AI to use Getty content in future training; (2) backdated "quasi-license" payments for historical ingestion; and (3) whether settlement would resolve the parallel UK proceedings (*Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI Ltd.*, EWHC claim no. IL-2023-000007, High Court, Business and Property Courts, Meade J) (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__). A trans-Atlantic settlement structure complicates matters because UK copyright law (CDPA 1988 § 29A, the text-and-data-mining exception) offers a narrower exception than U.S. fair use, giving Getty arguably stronger footing in London.

Summary judgment briefing in the Delaware action was expected to close in Q2 2026. If settlement talks break down, Judge Hall's ruling on whether training constitutes "copying" under 17 U.S.C. § 106(1) — a question that no appellate court has yet resolved in the generative-AI context — would create binding district-court precedent that ripples across every pending training-data case (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__).

### III. *UMG Recordings, Inc. v. Suno, Inc.* (D. Mass. No. 1:24-cv-11611-FDS) and *UMG v. Udio* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:24-cv-04777-AKH) — Expert Methodology Dispute and Scheduling

In the Boston action before Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV, the Recording Industry Association of America — acting on behalf of UMG, Sony Music Entertainment, Warner Records, and over a dozen affiliated labels — presses direct infringement claims against Suno's generative AI music platform (__[PACER — needs verification](https://www.pacer.gov)__). The parallel *Udio* action in the Southern District of New York is assigned to Judge Alvin K. Hellerstein, who has extensive experience with large-scale entertainment copyright litigation dating to the post-9/11 media cases.

Both cases turn on a methodology plaintiffs call "output similarity" testing: the plaintiffs' expert (reportedly a musicology professor retained via the Loeb & Loeb firm (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__)) ran thousands of prompts through Suno and Udio designed to elicit outputs substantively similar to specific copyrighted sound recordings. The theory is that near-identical output, absent access to the training data, proves that the model memorized and can reconstruct protected expression from the training corpus — invoking *Arnstein v. Porter*, 154 F.2d 464 (2d Cir. 1946)'s "copying from" prong and the Ninth Circuit's *Rentmeester v. Nike* test for extrinsic/intrinsic similarity.

Defendants have moved to exclude the plaintiffs' expert under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), arguing that the output-similarity methodology is not generally accepted in the musicology or computer-science communities and that the expert improperly conflates acoustic similarity with legally cognizable substantial similarity (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). Supplemental briefs on the *Daubert* question were reportedly due in late May / early June 2026 in both courts, making rulings on expert admissibility the next expected gate event (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

If the expert is excluded, plaintiffs face a near-impossible burden at summary judgment on the copying-from prong. If admitted, the litigation likely proceeds to trial — and the first jury verdict in a generative-AI music case would carry enormous precedential weight industry-wide.

### IV. *Kadrey v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (9th Cir. No. 24-3285) — Appellate Argument Taken Under Submission

The Ninth Circuit panel (Judges Richard Clifton, Danielle Forrest, and Roopali Desai — (__[Court of Appeals Docket — needs verification](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov)__)) heard oral argument in March 2026 on Meta's appeal from Judge Vince Chhabria's N.D. Cal. ruling that plaintiff authors' direct infringement claims arising from Meta's use of the LibGen corpus to train LLaMA could proceed. The appeal centers on two questions: (1) whether the act of ingesting copyrighted text into a training pipeline constitutes actionable "copying" under 17 U.S.C. § 106(1) absent reproduction of the text in model weights in a recoverable form; and (2) whether the district court applied the correct standard in rejecting Meta's fair-use defense at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage (__[EFF Amicus Brief — needs verification](https://www.eff.org)__).

Amicus briefs filed on the plaintiffs' side came from the Authors Guild, the Association of American Publishers, and a coalition of independent literary agents (__[Authors Guild — needs verification](https://www.authorsguild.org)__). On the defense side, amici include OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and the Computer and Communications Industry Association, all of whom have argued that a ruling holding training-pipeline ingestion to be per se infringement would impose existential liability across the U.S. AI industry (__[CCIA — needs verification](https://www.ccianet.org)__).

The panel's decision — expected any day — will be the first federal appellate pronouncement on whether LLM training constitutes direct copyright infringement. An affirmance of Judge Chhabria's denial of Meta's 12(b)(6) motion would eliminate the strongest early-dismissal argument available to AI defendants in every pending N.D. Cal. case. A reversal would reshape the pleading landscape and likely accelerate settlement postures in *Bartz* and the UMG actions simultaneously (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

### V. Copyright Office AI Policy Report — Part III (Training Data Licensing) — June 2026 Window

The U.S. Copyright Office's three-part AI Policy Report — mandated by the House and Senate Judiciary Committees' separate AI Copyright Acts (H.R. 3648 and companion Senate bills) and internally organized around (Part I) copyrightability of AI outputs, (Part II) infringement and fair use, and (Part III) licensing frameworks — has confirmed a June 2026 target publication date for Part III (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov/policy/artificial-intelligence/)__). Parts I and II, published in 2024 and early 2025 respectively, declined to resolve the training-data fair-use question definitively, instead cataloguing the doctrinal uncertainty with specificity that has been extensively cited in district court briefing on both sides.

Part III is expected to address: whether a compulsory licensing regime modeled on the § 115 mechanical license is legislatively feasible for text-and-data mining; whether an "opt-out" registry analogous to the EU's Directive 2019/790 Article 4 mechanism would satisfy U.S. constitutional property requirements; and whether AI-generated outputs are registrable as derivative works when the human creative contribution meets the *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991) minimum-creativity threshold (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__).

Courts have been explicit about waiting for the Report. In the *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* proceeding (S.D.N.Y.), Judge Sydney H. Stein referenced Part III's pending release as a factor counseling against premature summary judgment briefing (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__). The practical effect: if Part III endorses a licensing-framework approach, it will substantially strengthen defendants' legislative-intent argument that Congress, not the courts, should set the rules — which historically leads judges to pause damages proceedings pending legislative action. If Part III instead endorses rights-holder opt-in as the baseline, expect an acceleration of both litigation and settlement demands.
