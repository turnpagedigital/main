### I. Bartz v. Anthropic (No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Under Submission; Distribution Timeline Compressing

The procedural record since the May 14 fairness hearing is now the controlling fact pattern for claims investors and rights-holder counsel. The key data points from the hearing itself:


Class counsel Justin Nelson reported a per-work payout of approximately $3,100, with a 92.77% participation rate for works on the works list.
 That figure derives from 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works having been claimed. 
The final claims rate vastly surpasses the 54 percent rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval of Settlement filed March 19, 2026 — 11 days before the March 30, 2026 claims filing deadline — indicating that a large number of claims came in at the very end of the process.



The order issued following the fairness hearing directed Anthropic, by no later than May 21, 2026, to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; plaintiffs were simultaneously directed to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (ECF No. 646-1). The court stated it would not require anything further from objectors nor consider any further submissions from them.


On fees: 
plaintiffs' counsel are seeking 25% of the fund, or $375 million, with service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives, and JND Legal Administration handling settlement administration.
 The fee request — 25% of $1.5B — is squarely within Ninth Circuit common fund benchmarks, though Judge Martínez-Olguín's questions to lead counsel Justin Nelson (Susman Godfrey) and Rachel Geman (Lieff Cabraser) about the multiplier suggest the court is scrutinizing the lodestar cross-check. 
Seven objectors were each given two minutes, with attorney James Bartolomei receiving five minutes to speak for several objectors collectively.


The distribution architecture is on a fixed contractual schedule regardless of when the final approval order issues. 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The second installment — $300 million per the plan of allocation — was therefore due April 30, 2026. 
The plan of allocation calculates per-work and per-claimant distributions by June 11, 2026, with earliest payments to class members if there are no objectors in June 2026, and earliest payments if there are objectors but no appeals projected for summer 2026.


The critical qualifier: 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 A single Ninth Circuit objector appeal — a non-trivial risk given that seven objectors appeared at the fairness hearing — would push first distributions past the summer 2026 window. 
Objections raised before the fairness hearing included publisher favoritism, inadequate compensation, and transparency concerns.


Scope clarity matters for parallel-case positioning. 
The scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood in press coverage: Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and he also concluded that Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired. Nothing about this settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
What the court left unresolved was Anthropic's potential liability for downloading and storing files from two shadow libraries — Library Genesis (LibGen) and Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi). If Judge Martínez-Olguín grants approval, Anthropic will be required to destroy the pirated copies and to pay the $1.5 billion into the settlement fund, from which administrative costs, attorneys' fees, and per-work payments will be distributed.


The per-work benchmark has now stabilized. 
The $1.5 billion Bartz settlement establishes the first concrete per-work pricing benchmark ($3,113/book) for AI training on pirated content. Norton Rose Fulbright's March 2026 analysis notes that statutory damages can reach $150,000 per work, meaning the settlement resolved at approximately 2.1% of theoretical maximum exposure. This ratio gives plaintiffs' attorneys in other AI copyright cases a credible floor for settlement negotiations.

### II. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic (No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL, N.D. Cal.) — Motion to Stay Heard; Piracy Theory Under Pressure

The tracked-case ground truth had *Concord* at "Cross-MSJ pending · awaiting first docket sync." The CourtListener docket for the case has now clarified the current posture materially.


The docket was last updated June 10, 2026; the case is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen on referral for discovery, captioned *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.).


The most significant recent development: 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay, with a hearing set for May 20, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. in San Jose, Courtroom 7, 4th Floor, before Judge Eumi K. Lee.
 Separately, a second *Concord* docket has emerged — No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal.) — reflecting what appears to be a refiled or related action with the same assignment to Judge Lee and Magistrate Van Keulen. Whether the stay motion was granted or denied at the May 20 hearing is not reflected in available public docket information as of this writing.

The earlier procedural history is worth carrying forward for context. 
The court found in favor of Anthropic in two consecutive prior decisions: first denying the publishers' motion for preliminary injunction as overbroad and failing to establish irreparable harm, and second granting Anthropic's motion to dismiss the contributory and vicarious infringement claims with leave to amend, because plaintiffs failed to show any direct third-party infringement by actual users and that Anthropic knew or had reason to know of it.



The parties entered a stipulated order on January 2, 2025, in which Anthropic agreed to maintain guardrails preventing Claude from outputting infringing content — a partial interim victory for the publishers.
 
As to new large language models and new product offerings introduced in the future, Anthropic will apply guardrails on text input and output consistent with already-implemented guardrails; however, the stipulated order does not resolve the publishers' motion seeking to enjoin Anthropic's alleged use of unauthorized copies of lyrics to train AI models.


The publishers' theory — that Anthropic's training corpus included pirated lyric texts and that Claude reproduces lyrics verbatim on user prompting — directly mirrors the Bartz "shadow library" theory. 
Music publishers' subsequent litigation against Anthropic using the same piracy theory suggests plaintiff attorneys view Bartz as a replicable playbook.
 Whether the amended complaint, once filed, can survive dismissal on the training-input theory is the structural question for this docket.

### III. Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence — Third Circuit Oral Argument Complete; First Appellate Fair-Use Word Imminent


The first appellate ruling on fair use in an AI training case was scheduled for oral argument on June 11, 2026, in the Third Circuit. The case, *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence*, involves non-generative AI — Ross's system used Westlaw headnotes to train a legal search tool — but the fair-use analysis it will produce is expected to influence every generative AI training case in the pipeline.



On February 11, 2025, the district court granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Thomson Reuters' Westlaw headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and that Ross Intelligence's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use.
 
The fourth fair-use factor — effect on the market — heavily favored Thomson Reuters, as Ross's tool was directly competitive and undercut Westlaw's market.



District Judge Stephanos Bibas (sitting by designation in Delaware) initially appeared to favor Ross but reversed himself in the February 11, 2025 ruling. Bibas certified the fair use and copyrightability questions for interlocutory appeal, acknowledging both were "hard under existing precedent." The Third Circuit's June 11, 2026 argument will be the first appellate consideration of whether training an AI on copyrighted works is fair use — making it the most consequential AI copyright hearing of 2026.


The stakes for the Bartz settlement's fair-use posture are indirect but real. Judge Alsup's June 2025 ruling that AI training on legally obtained books is fair use was the district-court counterpart to the *Ross* line of reasoning. A Third Circuit reversal of *Ross* — finding that even competitive-displacement-based training is not fair use — would not technically disturb Alsup's ruling, which sits in the Ninth Circuit, but would create a circuit divergence that plaintiffs in every pending AI copyright case would immediately exploit.

### IV. In re OpenAI Copyright MDL (No. 1:25-md-03143, S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein) — Discovery Escalation; 20 Million Conversation Logs in Play


The MDL docket was last updated June 16, 2026; it is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang on referral, captioned *In Re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.).



The consolidated MDL brings together 16 copyright lawsuits against OpenAI. Lead cases include The New York Times (filed December 27, 2023), the New York Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting.
 
On January 5, 2026, Judge Sidney H. Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs to plaintiffs. OpenAI had attempted to narrow the production to only logs mentioning plaintiffs' specific works; the court rejected this approach. Legal analysts suggest these logs, once analyzed, will effectively dictate settlement terms for the remainder of the consolidated litigation.


The scope of the discovery fight has a parallel dimension involving model coverage. A December 15, 2025 ruling on Ziff Davis's claims within the MDL framework confirmed that 
OpenAI's motion to dismiss was granted as to claims 4 and 5, denied as to claims 3, 6, and 7; and OpenAI's motion to stay was granted as to models not already in the MDL — specifically the o1, o1-mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 models.
 The model-scope stay insulates OpenAI's newest generation from MDL discovery for now, but the 20-million-log production order applies to legacy ChatGPT output and creates an independent evidentiary record.


When Magistrate Judge Wang issued a May 13, 2025 preservation order requiring OpenAI to retain all ChatGPT conversation logs — affecting over 400 million users worldwide — she created a watershed moment for AI data management that the presiding judge subsequently affirmed, representing the first time a court has mandated mass preservation of AI-generated content on such a scale.

### V. The Alsup Framework and § 107(4) Market-Harm Dynamics Across the Docket

The AI copyright docket is now operating on a bifurcated liability theory that Judge Alsup crystallized in June 2025 and that the Bartz settlement has locked in as the practical baseline: 
AI training on copyrighted works can constitute fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107, but storing pirated copies of those works does not. This bifurcation is now the framework that other U.S. AI copyright cases are navigating.


The § 107(4) market-harm factor — the fourth and most heavily weighted — runs in two directions depending on the fact pattern. Where AI companies trained on legally acquired works, the market-substitution argument is difficult to sustain if the AI output is sufficiently transformative. Where the training corpus includes pirated shadow-library content, the market harm is essentially strict: there is no lawful market for pirated copies, and the statutory-damages exposure under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) is enormous. 
With potential statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work, Anthropic was staring down theoretical liability exceeding $70 billion
 before it settled.


The Bartz settlement has given plaintiffs' lawyers a roadmap. Law firms like Susman Godfrey have developed what commentators call "The Shadow Library Strategy," which attempts to skip the debate over whether AI training is fair use — judges so far appear to agree it is — and go straight for the piracy angle. Most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies now allege defendants downloaded from shadow libraries.


The licensing-deal dimension of § 107(4) analysis also warrants ongoing monitoring. Any AI licensing agreement struck at a specific per-work rate becomes comparator evidence in the § 107(4) market-harm analysis. The Bartz per-work figure of ~$3,100 is now the sole hard data point. Future licensing transactions by Anthropic, OpenAI, or any other AI developer that settle at different rates will either compress or expand that benchmark — with direct implications for recovery value in every pending case.


On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming the principle that material must have human authorship to be copyrightable.
 That ruling closes one theoretical defense — that AI-generated outputs themselves warrant copyright protection as a shield against infringement claims on training data — and removes a residual uncertainty that had complicated valuation in at least some mass-licensing discussions.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should treat the current period — between the May 14 fairness hearing and the still-pending final approval order in *Bartz* (No. 3:24-cv-05417) — as the last window to assess whether any objector with standing intends to appeal to the Ninth Circuit, since that determination governs whether the June/summer 2026 payment timeline holds or slips to late 2026 or beyond; monitor CourtListener docket No. 69058235 for the final approval order and the May 21 late-opt-out supplemental brief from Anthropic (ECF response to the post-hearing order signed May 14 by Judge Martínez-Olguín) — both documents will signal whether the court is prepared to enter a clean order or will require a second round of briefing. In *Concord* (No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL), the outcome of Anthropic's May 20 stay motion before Judge Lee should be confirmed from the public docket; if the stay was denied, the amended complaint timeline and cross-MSJ briefing schedule control the next twelve months of that litigation, and the music-publisher piracy theory — which rests on the same shadow-library logic that produced the $1.5 billion Bartz settlement — will move from pleading survival to merits adjudication. For the OpenAI MDL (No. 1:25-md-03143), the 20-million conversation-log production ordered by Judge Stein and Magistrate Judge Wang is the most significant near-term discovery development in the entire AI copyright space; once those logs are analyzed and disclosed to parties, any party with exposure in consolidated or parallel cases should expect accelerated settlement pressure. Finally, the Third Circuit's forthcoming ruling in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* — oral argument completed June 11, 2026 — must be read the moment it issues; even though *Ross* involves non-generative AI, any appellate reformulation of the § 107 four-factor test in the training context will be cited in every pending AI copyright proceeding, and a ruling affirming no-fair-use would create a circuit split with Judge Alsup's Ninth Circuit reasoning that could precipitate en banc or certiorari proceedings.
