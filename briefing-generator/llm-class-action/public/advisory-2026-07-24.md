### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Judgment Entered; Appeal Window Open


On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 
The court ruled the proposed settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," finding it was "negotiated at arm's length" and "treats Class Members equitably relative to each other," and overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds.


The economics of the final order matter for claims-recovery positioning. 
Per a Bloomberg report, *Bartz v. Anthropic* is "the largest copyright class action settlement in US history," paying "about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works," with claims being filed for 440,490 works downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror by Anthropic.
 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate, and 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works — or .03% of the total number of works.


On fees, the court drew a sharp line. 
The court denied class counsel's requested 12.5% of the $1.5 billion fund — which would have yielded $187,500,000 — as too high, instead granting a lodestar multiplier of 3.75 for a total fee award of $101,561,111, plus expense reimbursements of close to $3 million, with a cost reserve of $18.2 million in anticipated claims administration expenses.
 
The court determined that "a multiplier of 3.75, for a total fee award of $101,561,111 is reasonable," rejecting the 6.92 multiplier class counsel had sought on an estimated lodestar of $27,082,963.


On service awards, 
the court found class representatives Andrea Bartz, Kirk Wallace Johnson, and Charles Graeber "adequately represented the Class" but rejected their request for $50,000 each, reducing those awards to $15,000 per representative.


The non-monetary terms are structurally important for the parallel music and image dockets: 
beyond the money, the settlement requires Anthropic to destroy all
 training data copies sourced from the shadow libraries — a remedial data-destruction obligation that sets a precedent the music publishers in *Concord I* are already invoking. 
The court emphasized that the settlement provides "substantial benefits to the Class in light of the novel claims asserted," and that taking the claims to trial could have left the authors "no recourse" despite their strong positions.


**30-day objector-appeal window.** 
The Authors Guild notes it looks forward to the commencement of the distribution process, but that a specific date for the commencement of payouts has not yet been announced.
 Distribution cannot begin until the 30-day appellate window — running from the July 20 entry of judgment to August 19, 2026 — closes without a notice of appeal, or until any appeal is resolved. 
Every dollar not paid in fees stays in the non-reversionary fund for authors; if money remains after all valid claims are paid, the parties anticipate redistributing it to class members, with any final economically infeasible balance going — subject to court approval — to charitable purposes.


For claims investors holding assignments from eligible class members: the operative risk through August 19 is a 9th Circuit objector appeal under Fed. R. App. P. 23(e). With 53 objections overruled and only 350 opt-outs across 1,802 works, the statistical record is thin for any objector seeking standing to mount a credible appeal, but the exposure is not zero — one structured objector with Article III standing could delay distributions for 12–18 months.

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (Concord I), No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL (N.D. Cal.) — October 21 Summary-Judgment Hearing

The most consequential live merits question in AI copyright is now before Judge Eumi K. Lee in San Jose. 
The cross-MSJ hearing in *Concord I* (No. 5:24-cv-03811, Judge Eumi Lee) is set for October 21, 2026 — the central questions being whether partial summary judgment on copyright infringement should be granted to plaintiffs, or whether summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement should be granted to Anthropic.


The briefing posture: 
Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO filed their motion for partial summary judgment on March 23 in the N.D. Cal., the latest step in a case first brought in October 2023 over alleged infringement of 499 copyrighted musical works.
 
On the infringement side, the filing states that Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works," and that Anthropic "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."


On the fair-use factors, publishers frame their § 107(4) market-harm argument precisely. 
Publishers argue Anthropic's use was purely commercial — noting the company is "a for-profit technology company valued at $380 billion or more" with a revenue run rate approaching $14 billion — that it copied lyrics in full, and that its output directly competes with licensed lyrics services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch.
 That last point is § 107(4) market-substitution evidence in concentrated form: if Claude can reproduce and generate lyrics in competition with a licensed market, the fourth factor cuts hard against fair use regardless of how factors one through three resolve.

**Concord II** — the follow-on suit — is also live. 
Publishers allege Anthropic obtained more than 20,000 musical works from pirate shadow libraries for training and seek roughly $3 billion, which plaintiffs describe as the largest non-class-action copyright case in U.S. history; the court denied Anthropic's motion to stay *Concord II* on April 24, 2026, with Anthropic's motion to dismiss or answer due August 3, 2026.
 The $3 billion figure in *Concord II* is calculated at statutory damages rates under 17 U.S.C. § 504 — the same threat arithmetic that drove *Bartz* to settlement at $1.5 billion.

The *Bartz* framework hangs directly over *Concord I*. 
The legal theory in Concord leans on the June 23, 2025 *Bartz v. Anthropic* summary judgment ruling, which held that training on lawfully acquired books can be fair use but that sourcing from pirate libraries is not protected.
 If Judge Lee applies the same piracy exception at summary judgment, publishers could secure a finding of infringement as a matter of law — stripping away Anthropic's fair-use defense and leaving only damages to be tried.

### III. *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein) — Sanctions Motion and Evidence Spoliation

The most structurally dangerous development for OpenAI this cycle is not a merits ruling — it is a discovery sanctions motion that could determine whether OpenAI is permitted to control its own narrative on training data at all.


On July 9, 2026, plaintiff publishers including *The New York Times* and the *Daily News* filed a motion for sanctions against OpenAI in the Southern District of New York, in the consolidated copyright multidistrict litigation, No. 1:25-md-03143, before Magistrate Judge Ona Wang.
 The two-pronged theory: 
first, that for more than two years OpenAI told the court it had no ability to search its training data and ChatGPT output logs for the plaintiffs' works, when in fact it had already built that search capability before the litigation began; second, that despite a court preservation order, deletion of chatbot conversation logs continued.



The *New York Times*, the *New York Daily News*, and 15 other media organizations alleged in the filing that OpenAI was withholding evidence that could play a key role in the lawsuits, calling for legal sanctions and accusing the company of violating court rules and acting in bad faith during the fact-discovery phase.
 
Lead counsel for the plaintiffs, Ian Crosby, argues that "for over two years, OpenAI lied to The Times and Daily News plaintiffs, the public, and the Court."



The Times has spent more than $28 million on litigation costs against AI companies, $4.2 million of which came during 2026's first financial quarter
 — a cost structure suggesting the sanctions motion was filed strategically, not reactively. An adverse sanctions ruling against OpenAI could yield adverse-inference instructions, evidence preclusion, or in an extreme case structural remedies under Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(b). The motion is pending before Magistrate Judge Wang; any ruling will be subject to de novo review by Judge Stein on objection.

The discovery stakes extend beyond this case. 
The NYT v. OpenAI discovery framework matters for every AI training-data lawsuit — the court-ordered preservation and production of ChatGPT logs sets the template that the book-author cases (Bartz, MDL 3143, Kadrey) all operate against.
 A finding of spoliation here creates cross-case risk for any defendant that made similar representations about its ability to audit training data.

### IV. Circuit Landscape — The Third Circuit and the Emerging Split

The doctrinal fault lines clarified this week. 
Three U.S. judges — Alsup (*Bartz*), Chhabria (*Kadrey*), and Bibas (*Thomson Reuters v. Ross*) — have all ruled on AI training and fair use; Alsup and Chhabria found for AI companies on training, while Bibas found against; the Third Circuit's first appellate ruling, argued June 11, 2026, is pending.


The *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* posture is worth tracking closely. 
On May 23, 2025, the court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard."
 
Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence held that training a competing legal-research tool on Westlaw headnotes was not fair use.
 If the Third Circuit affirms Bibas, there will be a formal circuit-ready split between the Ninth Circuit district-level consensus (training on lawfully acquired works = fair use) and the Third Circuit (training on a commercial rival's product = not fair use). The Supreme Court's subsequent certiorari posture would define the landscape for every settlement in progress.

Internationally: 
the UK High Court ruled for Stability AI in *Getty v. Stability AI* (November 2025), while the Munich Regional Court ruled against OpenAI in *GEMA v. OpenAI* (November 2025) — the same technology, trained on the same type of content, produced opposite first-instance verdicts in two legal systems within days of each other.
 EU DSM Directive Art. 4 TDM opt-outs remain the operative mechanism for rights-holders seeking to preserve exclusivity in EU jurisdictions while litigation develops in the U.S.

### V. *Disney / Universal v. Midjourney* and the Image Docket


In *Disney et al. v. Midjourney*, the parties filed their joint Rule 26(f) report, in which Disney requested a non-expert discovery cut-off of August 17, 2026, with all dispositive motions filed by December 21, 2026.
 
Midjourney did not agree, asking instead for dates approximately three months later.
 The scheduling dispute reflects Midjourney's incentive to maximize time before any summary-judgment ruling that might land after a Third Circuit or Ninth Circuit appellate ruling on fair use shifts the law.
