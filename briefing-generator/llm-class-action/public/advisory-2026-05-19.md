## Analysis & Developments

Four developments shape the AI copyright docket on May 19: the Bartz v. Anthropic final-approval order remains pending after Judge Alsup took the matter under submission on May 14, with the customary 7–21 day window now extending into early June; the Elsevier v. Meta complaint filed May 5 alleges that Mark Zuckerberg personally authorized the torrenting of 267 TB of pirated academic research from LibGen and Sci-Hub, including allegations of deliberate concealment of a licensing-to-piracy pivot; the Third Circuit has scheduled oral argument in Thomson Reuters v. ROSS for June 11 (moved up from Q3), with both parties filing supplemental briefs on May 11 addressing how the UpCodes fair-use standard applies to AI training on legal editorial content; and the Kadrey v. Meta seeding-theory discovery window continues through June, with Meta executive depositions already completed. The OpenAI MDL discovery track has produced the full 20 million ChatGPT conversation-log dataset, which plaintiffs' counsel is now analyzing for patterns of competitive substitution against The New York Times and other news publishers.

The Bartz v. Anthropic settlement fairness hearing on May 14 closed the record on what would be the largest copyright class settlement in U.S. history. Author-side claims activity through the close of the bar date set the rate at roughly 91.3% of identified class members—a figure that effectively forecloses any meaningful adequacy-of-notice objection on appeal. With the fairness hearing behind it, the docket pivots to Judge Alsup's final-approval order (customary 7–21 days post-hearing, placing it in the May 22 to early-June window) and the parallel fee petition under Rule 23(h). The settlement structure—pooled fund with author-by-author allocation based on book counts in the Anthropic training corpus—sets the benchmark settlement structure for the rest of the AI training-data class action docket. Banner Witcoff and Norton Rose Fulbright have both flagged the per-book pricing implicit in the settlement (roughly $3,000 per book at the high end of the claimed pool) as the operative comparator for OpenAI MDL, Concord/UMG, and the Disney v. Midjourney negotiating posture.

## Elsevier v. Meta — 267 TB Piracy Complaint, Executive Knowledge Allegations

On May 5, 2026, Elsevier, Hachette, Macmillan, McGraw Hill, Cengage, and author Scott Turow filed a putative class-action complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York alleging systematic copyright infringement by Meta in the training of its Llama models. The complaint alleges that between April and July 2024, Meta downloaded 134.6 TB via torrenting while uploading 40.42 TB, predominantly copyrighted academic and trade publications sourced from Library Genesis and Sci-Hub. The critical distinction from earlier AI copyright filings is the executive-knowledge element: the complaint alleges that in early April 2023, Meta abruptly abandoned its licensing strategy, and that the decision to pivot from licensing to piracy was "escalated" to Mark Zuckerberg. Following that escalation, Meta's business development team received "verbal instructions" to cease licensing efforts. The 267 TB figure represents the total alleged pirated corpus, equivalent to hundreds of millions of publications and many times the entire print collection of the Library of Congress. Meta admits in filings that at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) copyrighted works and that it has never sought nor obtained a license. The complaint seeks unspecified monetary damages on behalf of the putative class of copyright holders whose works were downloaded without authorization.

## Thomson Reuters v. ROSS — Third Circuit Oral Argument June 11, Supplemental Briefs Filed May 11

The Third Circuit Court of Appeals has scheduled oral argument in Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence (No. 25-2153) for June 11, 2026—an advance from the previously anticipated Q3 window. The case is the first appellate review of a district-court fair-use ruling applied to AI model training. The district court initially granted ROSS partial summary judgment on fair use, then reversed and held that ROSS's use was not transformative because ROSS built a competing legal-research tool from Westlaw headnotes. Both parties filed supplemental briefs on May 11 addressing the Third Circuit's April 2026 decision in American Society for Testing & Materials v. UpCodes, Inc., which addressed fair use in the context of standards that had been legally incorporated into law and made freely available for access-to-law purposes. ROSS argues that UpCodes clarifies the first factor calls for an objective inquiry into purpose (not subjective), and that ROSS's objective use—training an AI model on the relationships among legal words—is at least as transformative as what UpCodes did. Thomson Reuters counters that UpCodes simply applies the settled framework distinguishing commercial, substitutive uses from those serving a genuinely different purpose, and that the fundamental distinction is that UpCodes involved standards legally incorporated into law, whereas ROSS trained on Westlaw headnotes to build a competing research tool. The Third Circuit's framing of "transformativeness" against substitutionary use will set the standard for the rest of the docket and likely influence settlement postures across the music-publishing and news-publisher AI cases.

## Kadrey v. Meta — Seeding-Theory Discovery Continues Through June

The Kadrey v. Meta docket continues to move on the seeding theory—the allegation that Meta's independent act of uploading (seeding) torrented copyrighted material is actionable copyright infringement separate from the fair-use defense applicable to the training itself. Judge Chhabria's earlier denial of summary judgment on the LibGen/PiLiMi piracy issue kept the seeding theory alive for discovery and trial. Meta executive depositions, including the deposition of Mark Zuckerberg (completed December 17, 2024), have been completed through the fact-discovery period. In newly released deposition snippets, Zuckerberg cited YouTube's battle to remove pirated content as a defense to Meta's own acquisition and use of pirated e-books, distinguishing between platforms that host user-generated content (where third-party upload enables piracy) and platforms that directly acquire and store pirated material (Meta's posture). The seeding theory—that downloading and storing copyrighted material from shadow libraries is independently actionable—survived the Bartz settlement and remains the most viable plaintiff-side theory in cases where the training-corpus formation involved torrenting. The discovery window closes in June, setting up trial readiness briefing in Q3 2026.

## OpenAI MDL — 20M ChatGPT Logs in Plaintiff Hands, Pattern Analysis Underway

Judge Sidney Stein affirmed in January 2026 the magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the full 20 million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs in the multidistrict litigation. The New York Times and co-plaintiffs have now received the entire dataset and are conducting discovery pattern-analysis to identify instances where ChatGPT outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted news content. The logs—anonymized but comprehensive—cover a broad range of user queries and ChatGPT responses, providing a granular record of what the model produces across the news, entertainment, reference, and commercial domains. This dataset is critical to plaintiffs' fair-use defense counter-argument: if ChatGPT outputs directly compete with or substitute for news articles, that is strong evidence against fair use under the fourth factor (market harm). The parties are working through the logistics of producing summaries and statistical analyses of the 20 million logs while maintaining privacy protections for the individual users who submitted the queries.

## AI Copyright Legislation — TRAIN, NO FAKES, CLEAR in Committee, TRUMP AMERICA AI as Counterweight

Committee activity on the TRAIN Act (training data transparency), NO FAKES Act (digital replica rights), and CLEAR Act (AI content labeling) has accelerated through May, with markup activity in House Judiciary and Senate Commerce. The TRAIN Act would create a federal mechanism allowing copyright holders to use subpoena power to determine if their work was used to train an AI model, modeled on internet-piracy discovery procedures. The NO FAKES Act would establish a federal property right protecting voice, image, and likeness from generative AI deepfakes and clones without consent. The CLEAR Act would require companies to file notice with the Copyright Office detailing copyrighted works used in training datasets before public release, with retroactive application to existing models and a public database maintained by the Copyright Office. Simultaneously, the administration has pushed the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act as a competing framework that would preempt state AI laws and limit class-action remedies for AI training—a provision that would dramatically narrow the liability exposure of AI developers by capping damages or eliminating class actions altogether. The May markup activity in House Judiciary and Senate Commerce is expected to clarify which statutory framework will prevail: creator-protection statutes (TRAIN/NO FAKES/CLEAR) or developer-preemption statutes (TRUMP AMERICA AI).

## Recommended Actions

AI-developer GCs should monitor for the Bartz final-approval order through early June and prepare distribution communications and claims-intake systems in anticipation of Judge Alsup's entry. Publisher and rightsholder counsel should treat the Bartz per-book pricing ($3,000 per book at the high end) as the operative settlement comparator and model damages calculations using that baseline across OpenAI MDL, Concord/UMG, Disney v. Midjourney, and any newly filed cases. Music-publishing counsel watching Concord/UMG v. Anthropic should expect the impact of the Elsevier v. Meta executive-knowledge allegations to reset settlement signaling in the music context, since the Elsevier complaint's detailed executive-authorization narrative materially strengthens the non-fair-use position. Plaintiff-side firms in AI training-data cases should align discovery on the seeding theory where shadow-library acquisition is alleged, since the fair-use defense does not reach the seeding question post-Bartz and the Elsevier complaint provides a granular template for executive-knowledge discovery. Companies developing generative AI models should begin preparing compliance-documentation for the contingent-liability scenarios presented by TRAIN Act subpoena procedures and CLEAR Act notice-filing requirements, and should model scenarios under both creator-protection statutes (current trajectory) and developer-preemption statutes (TRUMP AMERICA AI passage scenario). In-house counsel at news publishers and academic publishers should begin analyzing the 20 million ChatGPT logs data through a market-substitution lens, identifying specific query-response patterns where ChatGPT directly competes with or substitutes for publisher content, as those patterns will form the core of fourth-factor fair-use analysis in the OpenAI MDL and feed ammunition for TRAIN Act discovery requests.
