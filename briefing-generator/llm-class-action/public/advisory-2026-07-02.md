### Bartz v. Anthropic, 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Still Pending; Objector Activity Continues

The $1.5 billion settlement remains the central matter. 
The fairness hearing took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California.
 
Following the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín issued an order requiring Anthropic to file, by May 21, 2026, a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and requiring plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.
 No final approval order appears on the docket as of July 2, 2026. The tracked-case ground truth confirms docket entry No. 676, filed June 30, 2026 — objections directed at the court's prior scheduling and deadline orders — as the most recent filing, indicating that the approval record remains open. That is the new development as of this briefing.

The claim record heading into the approval decision is notable. 
Class counsel reported that 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% participation rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval, indicating a surge of late claims.
 
The high claim rate means per-work payouts will track closer to the original $3,000 figure rather than the elevated projections based on the March participation data.
 On fees, 
class counsel urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised downward from $300 million to $187.5 million.
 
Separately, Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees requested by nonclass counsel as part of the settlement is far too high, citing "scant justification" for the request.


The structural dynamics heading into the distribution phase carry specific risk for claimants. 
Anthropic funds the $1.5 billion in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Payouts will only be issued after the court grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved, with no exact timeline available — payments likely commencing no earlier than late fall at the best case.
 The 9th Circuit appeal window is the critical path. The fairness hearing record includes objections heard from seven class members, 
with attorney James Bartolomei speaking five minutes on behalf of several objectors, while Judge Martínez-Olguín questioned co-lead counsel Justin Nelson and Rachel Geman on service awards, cost reserves, the attorney fee multiplier, and the fee amount.
 Any timely objector who properly preserved the record may appeal to the Ninth Circuit after a final approval order issues — which could extend the payment timeline well into 2027 or beyond regardless of when the order drops.

The class is bounded by 17 U.S.C. § 411 registration: 
the class covers all rightsholders of books Anthropic acquired from LibGen and PiLiMi that are registered with the U.S. Copyright Office with ISBN or ASIN numbers — the class was certified only for the piracy claim, not the fair-use-resolved AI training claim.
 
Foreign authors whose works were not U.S.-registered remain outside the class definition even if their works were pirated from the same shadow libraries
 — an objection that was live at the hearing and remains unresolved in the record.

The underlying liability architecture that drove this settlement: 
in June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled on summary judgment that using books for AI training was fair use if they were legally acquired — but denied Anthropic's request for summary judgment on piracy, finding that the piracy was not fair use.
 That asymmetric ruling — § 107 fair-use protection for the training act itself, exposure for the means of acquisition — is what made settlement the rational outcome. It is also the ruling that sets the market-harm comparator for the rest of the docket: training on legitimately sourced data is transformatively protected; wholesale copying from LibGen is not.

### *Cox Communications, Inc. v. Sony Music Entertainment*, No. 24-171 — The Supreme Court Ruling Reshaping the Entire Docket


On March 25, 2026, the Supreme Court issued a unanimous decision in *Cox Communications, Inc. et al. v. Sony Music Entertainment et al.* (No. 24-171), reversing a Fourth Circuit decision that Cox bore contributory liability for infringement committed by its subscribers.
 
The Court held that a company is not liable for merely providing a service to the general public with knowledge it will be used by some to infringe; contributory liability turns on whether providers *intend* their services to be used for infringement, established in only two ways: (1) actively inducing infringement through specific acts, or (2) offering a service "not capable of substantial or commercially significant noninfringing uses."
 Justice Thomas wrote for the Court; 
Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justice Jackson, concurred in the judgment while disagreeing with the rationale.


The downstream litigation effect has been immediate and comprehensive. 
In *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic*, the music publishers narrowed their secondary liability claims across both the N.D. Cal. docket (Case No. 5:24-cv-03811, before Judge Eumi K. Lee) and the related refiled action (5:26-cv-00880); in the older case they dropped both contributory and vicarious infringement with prejudice as Anthropic's summary judgment motion loomed; in the newer one they dropped their vicarious infringement claim without prejudice.
 
In the OpenAI MDL, OpenAI filed a motion for judgment on the pleadings on the contributory infringement claims, based directly on *Cox v. Sony*, arguing that contributory copyright infringement has been foreclosed by the decision.


The NYT front was the most aggressive strategic response. 
The New York Times filed a motion on June 25 to amend its copyright infringement lawsuit against Microsoft and OpenAI in the Southern District of New York, voluntarily dropping its contributory infringement claim against OpenAI and its trademark dilution claim against all defendants — while simultaneously reframing Microsoft's infrastructure investment as active inducement satisfying the *Cox* inducement prong.
 
The amended complaint alleges that Microsoft built a custom supercomputer equipped with more than 285,000 CPU cores and 10,000 GPUs "specifically designed" to enable the allegedly infringing conduct.
 The theory is precise: if OpenAI's own training constitutes *direct* infringement rather than secondary infringement, the *Cox* framework is irrelevant to that claim — but Microsoft's role as infrastructure provider requires the inducement hook the *Cox* standard demands.


*Cox*'s holding is narrow in two critical respects: it concerns only contributory liability, not vicarious liability, and it applies only to defendants who do not themselves allegedly copy, host, distribute, or publish infringing material.
 That boundary is everything for AI training litigation. Anthropic, OpenAI, and their peers trained on the data directly — they are not passive conduit providers. The primary infringement / fair-use question under § 107 remains fully open and is the determinative issue in every open MSJ proceeding.

### *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic*, N.D. Cal. — Cross-MSJ on Training-Data Fair Use; Claude Hallucination Problem on the Record

The consolidated Concord matters now present the cleanest pending test of § 107 fair-use factors applied to AI-training on licensed musical works and lyric reproduction. 
The operative docket (5:24-cv-03811) is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, referred to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen, in the Northern District of California.
 
Music publishers moved for partial summary judgment that fair use does not excuse Anthropic's conduct because it used those lyrics to build a competing commercial product; Anthropic cross-moved arguing Claude's learning is "transformative" fair use.


A discrete but significant pretrial record issue arose in the Concord litigation. 
A California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged she used Claude.ai to help draft an expert's declaration that included the erroneous citation, characterizing it as "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 The sanctions posture is not trivial: any jury instruction or adverse inference flowing from the stricken report will color the § 107(4) market-harm factor presentation, since Anthropic's expert was presumably addressing the licensing-market evidence central to factor four.

The secondary-liability narrowing post-*Cox* — with Concord dropping contributory and vicarious claims — means the case now runs entirely on direct infringement: the training-input theory (reproduced lyrics ingested during training) and the output theory (Claude reproduces recognizable lyric passages on demand). 
Plaintiffs are a group of music publishers led by Concord Music Group and Universal Music Corp., alleging Anthropic infringes their exclusive rights to musical lyrics both by using the works to train Claude and by distributing the lyrics in Claude's outputs when prompted.
 The output theory survived early motion practice: 
music publishers can pursue previously dismissed copyright claims after Judge Lee found that their updated complaint plausibly alleges Anthropic knew people were using its AI system to create song lyrics.

### *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.) — Discovery Expansion, *Cox* Motion, and New Entrants


The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein, referred to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, in the Southern District of New York.
 
The last known filing is dated June 26, 2026.


On discovery: 
when OpenAI proposed producing 20 million anonymized ChatGPT logs in January 2026, that assumption backfired — Judge Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Wang's order compelling production of the entire 20-million-log sample, not just conversations implicating plaintiffs' works.
 
On relevance, Judge Wang found that even output logs without reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's fair-use defense; logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works — directly implicating § 107(4).


On the output-similarity issue, 
the court found that "a more discerning observer could easily conclude that [a] detailed summary is substantially similar to [the author's] original work, including because the summary conveys the overall tone and feel of the original work by parroting the plot, characters, and themes of the original."
 That standard for output-side substantial similarity — applied to fiction — diverges from the court's treatment of news-article summaries.

The *Cox* motion is the most acute pending issue. 
OpenAI told Judge Stein that the Supreme Court's *Cox v. Sony* decision bars a contributory infringement claim brought by four news companies accusing OpenAI of using their copyrighted materials to train ChatGPT, arguing the ruling eliminates the legal theory on which the plaintiffs rely.
 That motion remains pending. The *Cox* holding is factually distinguishable — OpenAI's direct copying during training is not analogous to an ISP's passive role — but the *Cox* argument creates a briefing obligation for plaintiffs before the court has issued any ruling on the substantive fair-use questions.

New docket entrants are expanding the MDL's surface area: 
Encyclopedia Britannica and Merriam-Webster filed complaints against OpenAI for copyright infringement, joining as the latest entrants in the MDL.
 Additionally, 
on June 8, 2026, a court severed Google, xAI, Perplexity, Apple, and Nvidia from a multi-defendant complaint
 — a severance order that routes the OpenAI-related claims into the MDL while leaving non-OpenAI defendants to manage parallel proceedings.

Sony* front, counsel with active secondary-liability theories in any of the six live AI-copyright dockets should audit claim viability under the inducement/tailoring two-prong test (No. 24-171, 607 U.S. ___ (2026)) before OpenAI's judgment-on-the-pleadings motion in the OpenAI MDL forces briefing that could produce adverse precedent — the Concord and NYT tactical retreats are the model: voluntary narrowing before a hostile ruling is preferable to a court-ordered dismissal with prejudice. For the Concord cross-MSJ, watch the expert-report sanctions posture (the partially stricken Latham declaration is now part of the appellate record), because any adverse § 107 ruling on training-input fair use in that case will function as persuasive authority in *In re OpenAI* MDL given both matters sit under N.D. Cal. law. Finally, claimants with non-U.S.-registered works who were excluded from the Bartz class should consult separately: the settlement release runs only through August 26, 2025, and a post-approval direct action based on post-cutoff conduct remains structurally available under 17 U.S.C. §§ 411 and 504.
