# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | May 9, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

Five days before the Bartz v. Anthropic fairness hearing, the docket has entered a period of procedural quiet — but the litigation landscape surrounding the hearing continues to intensify in ways that will shape Judge Martínez-Olguín's Rule 23(e)(2) analysis. The May 14 hearing at the San Francisco Federal Courthouse (Courtroom 12, 2:00 p.m. PT, with Zoom participation available) remains the single most consequential event on the AI copyright calendar: it will produce the first judicial determination of whether a billion-dollar AI copyright settlement satisfies the adequacy, reasonableness, and fairness standard of Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(e), setting the benchmark for every subsequent AI-training settlement negotiation. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/)) The claim statistics remain at their historic levels — 440,490 of 482,474 eligible works claimed, a 91.3% claim rate that dwarfs the typical 10% participation in consumer class actions — and the distribution computation is still scheduled for June 11, when the settlement administrator will apply the weighted methodology that Professor Lea Victoria Bishop's objection has challenged on adequacy grounds. ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/anthropic-settlement-update-91-percent-of-books-claimed/)) The five principal objection themes catalogued by the Authors Alliance — foreign-work exclusion, publisher-author allocation, notice deficiencies, per-work compensation relative to § 504(c) statutory damages, and class-counsel conflict concerns from simultaneous representation in the Concord and UMG music-publisher dockets — will each require individualized findings, and the court's treatment of the conflict-of-interest objection will be watched closely given class counsel's role in the parallel music-publisher litigation where cross-motions for partial summary judgment remain fully briefed and a ruling is expected this summer. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/infringement-on-a-massive-scale-umg-concord-and-abkco-ask-court-to-rule-against-ai-company-anthropic-ahead-of-trial/))

The reverberations of the May 5 publisher class action against Meta and CEO Mark Zuckerberg continue to reshape the docket's trajectory. The Elsevier, Cengage, Hachette, Macmillan, McGraw Hill, and Scott Turow complaint — filed in S.D.N.Y. and alleging that Meta torrented 267 terabytes of pirated material from LibGen and Anna's Archive after Zuckerberg personally authorized abandoning a $200 million licensing budget — is now the subject of sustained coverage from every major legal and business outlet and has prompted the Association of American Publishers to issue a formal statement of support. ([Variety](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/meta-ai-mark-zuckerberg-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-publishers-scott-turow-1236738383/); [AAP](https://publishers.org/news/publishers-and-authors-file-class-action-lawsuit-against-meta-and-zuckerberg-for-willful-copyright-infringement-to-develop-llama-ai-models/)) The complaint's personal-liability theory against a CEO — novel in AI copyright litigation — raises director-and-officer insurance exposure questions across the industry, and the evidentiary showing of abandoned licensing strengthens the willfulness theory under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) for all pending piracy-based cases. Critically, the Meta complaint validates the input-side infringement theory that proved successful in Bartz (where Judge Alsup held that training on legally obtained works constitutes fair use under § 107, but downloading pirated copies from shadow libraries constitutes per se infringement independent of any fair-use defense), while distinguishing the failed Kadrey v. Meta action where Judge Chhabria granted summary judgment for Meta based on the absence of substantially similar outputs. ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026))

The broader litigation calendar continues to accelerate toward a summer of consequential rulings. The Third Circuit's oral argument in Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence on June 11 — the first federal appellate consideration of whether AI training on copyrighted works constitutes fair use — will occur on the same day as the Bartz distribution computation, creating a single day that could simultaneously establish the appellate framework for AI fair-use analysis and begin disbursing the largest copyright settlement in history. ([Chat GPT Is Eating the World](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/04/14/third-circuit-sets-oral-argument-on-june-11-for-1st-appeal-of-decision-on-fair-use-in-ai-training-case-follows-another-recent-third-circuit-decision-on-fair-use-in-american-society-for-testing-v-upc/)) In the Concord Music v. Anthropic docket, the RIAA and NMPA have filed an amicus brief backing the publishers and arguing that Anthropic's unlicensed copying of 100+ compositions is "inexcusable," and a ruling on the cross-motions for partial summary judgment could arrive at any point this summer — potentially before or after the Bartz settlement achieves finality. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) Internationally, the French Senate's unanimous adoption on April 8 of a bill introducing a rebuttable presumption that AI systems have exploited copyrighted works — shifting the burden of proof from rights-holders to AI providers — represents the most aggressive legislative response to the transparency problem that has defined AI copyright litigation since its inception, and Jones Day's May 2026 comparative analysis of EU, French, and UK approaches notes that French law could become a model for burden-shifting mechanisms in U.S. legislative proposals such as the TRAIN Act. ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/navigating-copyright-in-the-age-of-generative-ai-eu-french-and-uk-developments-and-approaches); [British Copyright Council](https://www.britishcopyright.org/rightsholders-gain-a-significantly-stronger-litigation-position-as-frances-senate-unanimously-adopts-the-bill-introducing-a-presumption-of-use-of-cultural-content-by-ai-providers/))

On the legislative front, Senator Blackburn's TRUMP AMERICA AI Act — the 291-page omnibus that incorporates the NO FAKES Act, the TRAIN Act, and the Kids Online Safety Act — continues to gain momentum with an April roundtable featuring over 20 artists supporting the legislation's creator-protection provisions. ([Blackburn Senate Office](https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/4/ai/blackburn-welch-hold-roundtable-with-over-20-artists-who-support-no-fakes-act-and-train-act-to-protect-creators-from-ai-harms)) The TRAIN Act's administrative-subpoena mechanism — modeled on existing internet-piracy procedures — would give copyright holders a direct means to determine whether their works were used in AI training, which the French rebuttable-presumption approach addresses through a different procedural mechanism but toward the same transparency objective. Whether either legislative approach advances to committee markup before the Bartz settlement achieves finality (likely September 2026 if no appeals are filed after May 14 approval) will determine whether the settlement's per-work valuation becomes a de facto market benchmark or is overtaken by a statutory framework that redefines the negotiating landscape entirely.

## Recommended Actions

Class members in the Bartz settlement should complete final verification of their claim status and payment information through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com by Monday, May 11, at the latest — three business days before the fairness hearing — with particular attention to works filed under group Copyright Office registrations that may require individual enumeration before the June 11 distribution computation, and should prepare to attend or monitor the May 14 hearing via Zoom to understand the court's treatment of the five principal objection categories, which will signal whether appellate challenges are likely and whether the distribution timeline will hold. Practitioners advising rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz class should reassess expected value of individual claims in light of the Meta publisher complaint's evidentiary showing of deliberate piracy with CEO authorization: the willfulness theory under § 504(c) is materially strengthened, but the industry's willingness to pursue coordinated enforcement also validates the Bartz settlement's core per-work valuation for cases resolved cooperatively. AI developers facing training-data litigation should conduct immediate audits of data-provenance documentation and internal licensing-decision communications — the Meta complaint demonstrates that discovery of abandoned-licensing communications will be a priority in every pending case — and should monitor both the TRAIN Act's administrative-subpoena provision and the French rebuttable-presumption bill as signals of where U.S. and EU transparency mandates are heading, as compliance architecture built now will be substantially less costly than retroactive disclosure obligations imposed by statute or court order.
