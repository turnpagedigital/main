# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 28, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

With Anthropic's second $300 million settlement installment due in forty-eight hours — on April 30, 2026 — the Bartz v. Anthropic class settlement is approaching a critical inflection point that now intersects with a Supreme Court ruling whose downstream effects are only beginning to register across the AI copyright docket. The unanimous Cox v. Sony Music decision, handed down on March 25, fundamentally narrowed the scope of contributory copyright liability by holding that a service provider is not liable for users' infringement unless it either actively induces that infringement or offers a service "not capable of substantial or commercially significant noninfringing uses" — and in April, a wave of major law-firm analyses from Arnold & Porter, Holland & Knight, Jones Day, and Sterne Kessler have begun mapping those holdings onto the pending AI cases. ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/04/supreme-court-narrows-secondary-copyright-liability-in-cox)) ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/what-the-scotus-ruling-in-cox-v-sony-means-for-tech-providers)) The implications are particularly acute for the output-side contributory infringement theories that plaintiffs are pressing in the OpenAI MDL, Kadrey v. Meta, and Doe v. GitHub: defendants will invoke Cox to argue that merely training AI models on copyrighted material and making those models available to users does not meet the inducement standard, while plaintiffs will counter that AI companies' deliberate choices about training data, output guardrails, and system prompts distinguish them from the passive ISPs at issue in Cox and constitute the kind of purposeful conduct that satisfies even the narrowed standard. Whether courts will treat AI platforms as closer to Cox's internet pipes or to Grokster's peer-to-peer networks — where the Supreme Court found inducement based on advertising and business-model evidence — will likely become the central doctrinal question in output-side AI copyright litigation for the remainder of 2026. ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/04/isps-off-the-hook-supreme-court-narrows-contributory-copyright-liability))

The Cox ruling arrives against the backdrop of an extraordinary claim-rate milestone in the Bartz settlement itself. The Authors Guild has confirmed that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works have been claimed, producing a 91.3 percent participation rate that dwarfs the typical class action rate of roughly 10 percent and that will weigh heavily in Judge Martínez-Olguín's Rule 23(e)(2) adequacy analysis at the May 14 fairness hearing. ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/anthropic-settlement-update-91-percent-of-books-claimed/)) The claim rate jumped from 54 percent on March 19 to 91.3 percent by the March 30 deadline, suggesting that publishers and institutional rights-holders filed bulk claims in the final days — a pattern that will likely draw scrutiny from objectors like Professor Lea Victoria Bishop, who has argued that the publisher-versus-author allocation formula structurally disadvantages individual authors. The Free Software Foundation has staked out a distinctive position by declining monetary compensation altogether, instead demanding that Anthropic release its complete training inputs, model weights, training configuration, and source code — a "freedom over money" posture that, while unlikely to alter the settlement mechanics, introduces a novel open-source framing into the fairness-hearing record and underscores the ideological breadth of the class. ([FSF](https://www.fsf.org/blogs/licensing/2026-anthropic-settlement))

The most significant new litigation vector continues to be the SEIU Pension Plans Master Trust derivative action against Adobe's board of directors, filed April 24, alleging that directors breached fiduciary duties by knowingly permitting Adobe to train its SlimLM model on pirated copyrighted works and adopting an "ask forgiveness not approval" strategy that exposed the company to multiple copyright lawsuits and a 25-percent-plus share price decline. ([D&O Diary](https://www.dandodiary.com/2026/04/articles/artificial-intelligence/ai-related-ip-litigation-triggers-follow-on-do-lawsuit/)) ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/securities-law/adobe-investor-sues-board-over-ai-training-copyright-accusations)) This first shareholder derivative suit arising from AI copyright exposure establishes a new category of risk: the Bartz settlement's $1.5 billion valuation for unauthorized training-data acquisition creates a benchmark that plaintiff-side D&O attorneys will cite in demand letters to boards across the industry, and the interaction with Cox is noteworthy — even as Cox narrows direct contributory liability for AI platforms, it does nothing to shield corporate directors from breach-of-duty claims premised on the knowing decision to train on pirated material, since the fiduciary duty theory runs through Delaware corporate law rather than the Copyright Act's secondary liability framework.

In the Concord Music Group v. Anthropic docket, the RIAA, NMPA, and six additional music industry organizations have filed an amicus brief characterizing Anthropic as a "holdout" that scrapes rather than licenses — a framing that deliberately contrasts Anthropic's litigation posture with competitors' licensing deals, including OpenAI's Disney partnership, and the Suno/Udio settlements with UMG and WMG. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) The amicus filing lands as both the publishers' motion for partial summary judgment and Anthropic's cross-motion (filed April 22, arguing training on lyrics is categorically transformative fair use and that DMCA § 1202 CMI claims fail for lack of proximate causation) are being briefed ahead of the July 15 hearing before Judge Eumi K. Lee. The "holdout" characterization invites the court to weigh the fourth fair-use factor — effect on the market for the copyrighted work, 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) — through the lens of an emerging licensing market that Anthropic's competitors have validated through deal-making, and Cox's narrowing of contributory liability may paradoxically strengthen this argument: if AI companies can no longer be held vicariously liable for users' output-side infringement under Cox, rights-holders' primary recourse becomes the direct-infringement training-side theory, which makes the market-substitution analysis under factor four — and the existence of willing licensors — all the more central.

On the legislative front, the three-bill package championed at the Recording Academy's 25th GRAMMYS on the Hill — the TRAIN Act, the CLEAR Act (S. 3813), and the NO FAKES Act — continues to build bipartisan momentum alongside Senator Blackburn's comprehensive 291-page TRUMP AMERICA AI Act, which would legislatively declare that AI training on copyrighted works does not constitute fair use under the Copyright Act. ([Blackburn Senate Office](https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/4/ai/blackburn-welch-hold-roundtable-with-over-20-artists-who-support-no-fakes-act-and-train-act-to-protect-creators-from-ai-harms)) The White House National Policy Framework for AI, released March 20, takes the opposite posture — recommending that Congress leave fair use to the courts and instead focus on voluntary licensing and collective-rights frameworks with antitrust safe harbors — creating a genuine tension between the executive and legislative branches that will play out through committee markups this summer. ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-03-25-white-house-releases-ai-regulatory-blueprint-what-the-national-policy-framework-means-for-companies)) The legislative timeline creates a possibility that statutory changes could arrive before the Concord v. Anthropic PSJ ruling on July 15 or before the OpenAI MDL's anticipated fair-use motions, which would fundamentally alter the litigation calculus for all parties.

## Recommended Actions

Class members who submitted claims by the March 30 deadline should verify their confirmation status through anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and calendar two imminent milestones: the April 30 second installment payment, which upon deposit will bring the escrow to $600 million under the settlement's Rule 23(e)(2)(D) financial-commitment framework, and the May 14 fairness hearing at 2:00 PM PT, accessible via Zoom, at which Judge Martínez-Olguín will address the unsealed objections, the revised $187.5 million fee petition, and the publisher-versus-author allocation formula. Practitioners litigating output-side contributory infringement theories in any AI copyright case should immediately assess the impact of Cox v. Sony Music on their claims: defendants will move to raise the inducement bar, and plaintiffs should develop factual records demonstrating that AI companies' training-data curation decisions, output tuning, and marketing constitute the kind of purposeful facilitation that distinguishes their conduct from passive internet carriage. AI developers and their D&O insurers should treat the SEIU v. Adobe Board derivative suit as a leading indicator and immediately assess whether their training-data provenance practices create the same fiduciary-duty exposure — Cox's narrowing of contributory liability does not insulate directors from state-law breach-of-duty claims premised on knowing authorization of unauthorized training. Rights-holders with works in both the Bartz settlement class and other AI training datasets should track the emerging licensing-market benchmarks — Disney-OpenAI, AP-OpenAI, Reuters-Google, UMG/WMG-Suno/Udio — as comparators for valuing their own claims under 17 U.S.C. § 504 and for benchmarking any future settlement offers in the Concord v. Anthropic and OpenAI MDL matters.
