### *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Discovery Scope Battle and Section 1202 Viability

*Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:23-cv-04625-VC (N.D. Cal.), remains the cleanest vehicle for testing 17 U.S.C. § 1202 copyright management information (CMI) removal claims against a frontier-model developer. Plaintiffs — represented by Baker & Hostetler and Susman Godfrey — allege that Anthropic's preprocessing pipeline for Claude's training corpus systematically stripped authorship metadata and copyright notices from ingested text, constituting a per-work violation carrying statutory damages of $2,500–$25,000 under § 1202(b). With an estimated putative class of several hundred thousand works, the potential exposure ceiling exceeds $6 billion at maximum statutory rates before any individualized damages analysis (__[Baker & Hostetler — needs verification](https://www.bakerlaw.com)__).

The immediate battleground is Anthropic's objections to Requests for Production Nos. 12–34, which seek production of training-data manifests, deduplication logs, and preprocessing scripts used to construct the Constitutional AI training corpus. Anthropic argues that these materials constitute protected trade secrets under California Civil Code § 3426.1 and that a blanket production would irreversibly reveal model architecture details. Plaintiffs counter that a tailored protective order under Judge Chhabria's standard ESI protocol — which the court issued in its February 2026 case management order — fully addresses confidentiality concerns without wholesale non-production. A discovery hearing is scheduled before Magistrate Judge Alex Tse on June 19, 2026 (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

The § 1202 theory's viability hinges on whether plaintiffs can survive the scienter requirement: § 1202(b) requires that the defendant acted "knowing, or … having reasonable grounds to know," that CMI removal "will induce, enable, facilitate, or conceal" infringement. Anthropic's motion to narrow this claim, filed April 2026, argued that large-scale automated preprocessing of internet-crawled data cannot satisfy the knowing-inducement standard because no human reviewer individually decided to strip any specific work's metadata. Judge Chhabria has not yet ruled but noted at the April 22 hearing that *Mango v. BuzzFeed* (S.D.N.Y. 2023) and *Fischer v. Forrest*, 968 F.3d 216 (2d Cir. 2020) suggest a defendant need not target specific works if the policy itself is designed with the requisite knowledge (__[Susman Godfrey — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__).

### *Kadrey v. Meta Platforms* — Class Certification Approaching and Fair Use Briefing

*Kadrey v. Meta Platforms Inc.*, No. 3:23-cv-03417-VC (N.D. Cal., now Judge Chhabria), consolidates claims from Richard Kadrey, Sarah Silverman, Christopher Golden, and other literary authors alleging that Meta's LLaMA model series was trained on datasets including LibGen and shadow-library corpora containing plaintiffs' copyrighted works without license or consent. The operative complaint asserts direct infringement under 17 U.S.C. § 106(1) (reproduction) and § 106(3) (distribution), vicarious infringement, and § 1202(b) CMI removal (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

The June 2026 scheduling order, issued June 2, 2026, sets the following key dates: class-certification motion due November 14, 2026; opposition due January 23, 2027; reply due March 6, 2027; certification hearing April 9, 2027. This timeline positions *Kadrey* as a potential benchmark ruling on Rule 23(b)(3) predominance in AI copyright litigation — specifically whether individual fair-use defenses destroy predominance, the same question that led to class-certification denials in several music-sampling cases before the Ninth Circuit's clarification in *Seltzer v. Green Day*, 725 F.3d 1170 (9th Cir. 2013) (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

Meta's dispositive fair-use argument — that LLaMA training constitutes transformative use under *Authors Guild v. Google*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015) — faces significant headwinds in this district. Judge Chhabria signaled at the March 2026 CMC that commercial deployment of a competing expressive-output product is analytically distinct from a digital card-catalog system, and that the *Google Books* line may not translate automatically. The fourth fair-use factor — effect on the market for the copyrighted work — is particularly live: plaintiffs' expert Dr. Paul Goldstein (Stanford) is expected to offer a damages model quantifying lost licensing revenue from hypothetical synthetic-training-data markets, a theory that has gained traction following the Senate Judiciary Committee's AI Licensing Working Group hearings in March 2026 (__[Tech Policy Press — needs verification](https://techpolicypress.com)__).

### Second Circuit — *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* Briefing Complete; Oral Argument Expected Fall 2026

*Authors Guild v. OpenAI Inc.*, No. 24-2416 (2d Cir.), is the appellate track to watch for a potentially circuit-splitting ruling on the scope of fair use in generative-AI training. Plaintiffs — the Authors Guild, John Grisham, Jodi Picoult, George R.R. Martin, and over a dozen additional named authors — appeal from the S.D.N.Y.'s partial dismissal order (Hon. Colleen McMahon, No. 1:23-cv-08292-CM) entered September 2024, which dismissed direct-infringement claims premised on ChatGPT's output similarity but preserved the training-data reproduction claims (__[Authors Guild — needs verification](https://authorsguild.org)__).

OpenAI's response brief, filed May 30, 2026 by counsel at Latham & Watkins and Morrison Foerster, makes three core arguments: (1) training-data ingestion is intermediate copying protected under *Sega Enterprises Ltd. v. Accolade, Inc.*, 977 F.2d 1510 (9th Cir. 1992), and *Sony Computer Entertainment v. Connectix Corp.*, 203 F.3d 596 (9th Cir. 2000), by analogy; (2) the transformative-use analysis favors OpenAI because ChatGPT output does not substitute for any individual training work in any recognizable market; and (3) there is no circuit precedent holding that commercial gain alone displaces a substantial-transformative finding. Plaintiffs' reply brief is due July 11, 2026 (__[Latham & Watkins — needs verification](https://www.lw.com)__). The Second Circuit has a panel of Judges Chin, Lee, and Robinson — a configuration significant because Judge Denny Chin authored *Authors Guild v. Google* and brings the deepest institutional knowledge of the transformative-use doctrine in this context (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__).

If the Second Circuit issues a merits ruling rather than remanding procedural questions, it will be the first federal appellate decision on the training-data/fair-use question — a potential circuit split with the Ninth Circuit's trajectory if N.D. Cal. rulings go the other way.

### Getty Images v. Stability AI — S.D.N.Y. Discovery and Parallel U.K. Proceedings

*Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI, Inc.*, No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA (D. Del., transferred to S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-04591-LJL, Hon. Lewis J. Liman), involves claims that Stability AI's Stable Diffusion models were trained on approximately 12 million Getty images, including works bearing Getty watermarks, without license. The § 1202(b) claim is especially concrete here because plaintiffs allege Stable Diffusion outputs were found to reproduce recognizable fragments of Getty watermarks — direct evidence of CMI removal or alteration (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__).

Discovery is ongoing before Magistrate Judge Robert W. Lehrburger, who in April 2026 granted Getty's motion to compel production of Stability AI's LAION-5B dataset filtering records and training-run logs, rejecting Stability AI's trade-secret objection as premature absent a particularized harm showing. Stability AI's UK parallel proceeding — *Getty Images (UK) Ltd v. Stability AI Ltd* in the UK Intellectual Property Enterprise Court (IPEC Claim No. IL-2023-000007) — recently saw Stability AI seek a stay pending the US litigation's development; the IPEC denied the stay in April 2026, meaning dual-track discovery obligations continue (__[FT — needs verification](https://www.ft.com)__). The US damages claim has been estimated by plaintiffs' counsel at Covington & Burling (plaintiffs' side) at over $1.8 billion on a per-image statutory damages theory if willfulness is found; Stability AI disputes both the per-image theory and willfulness predicate (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__).

### Copyright Office AI Report — Imminent Release and Legislative Implications

The Copyright Office's *Report on Artificial Intelligence and Copyright*, mandated by Congress in the fiscal year 2024 omnibus and initially expected by January 2026, now appears imminent following confirmation that embargoed copies have circulated to staff on the Senate Judiciary IP Subcommittee (Chair Thom Tillis) and the House IP Subcommittee (Chair Darrell Issa) (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__). Prior interim guidance (Copyright Office Policy Statement, 88 Fed. Reg. 16190, Mar. 16, 2023) established that AI-generated works absent human authorship are not copyrightable, but the forthcoming report is expected to address four additional questions: (1) whether training on copyrighted works requires a statutory license; (2) whether a new licensing framework analogous to the Section 115 mechanical license is advisable; (3) what disclosure obligations should attach to AI-generated or AI-assisted works; and (4) how existing § 512 safe harbors interact with AI model hosting (__[Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov/policy/artificial-intelligence/)__).

Legislative watch: the *Content Origin Protection and Integrity from Edited and Deepfaked Media Act* (COPIED Act, S. 2770 / H.R. 5605) and the *Generative AI Copyright Disclosure Act* (H.R. 6932, Rep. Adam Schiff) remain in committee but are expected to be reintroduced or marked up in light of the Office's final findings. Any statutory license framework would materially affect the litigation calculus: a prospective license would not resolve past ingestion claims but would signal congressional disapproval of a blanket fair-use defense, potentially influencing pending fair-use analysis under the fourth *Campbell* factor (__[Tech Policy Press — needs verification](https://techpolicypress.com)__).

### Carrier Rulings and SDNY Visual Artists Track

*Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.*, No. 3:23-cv-00201-WHO (N.D. Cal., Hon. William H. Orrick) — covering visual artists including Sarah Andersen, Kelly McKernan, and Karla Ortiz — continues on remand following Judge Orrick's February 2024 order dismissing most claims with leave to amend and granting the second amended complaint's narrowed § 1202 and direct-infringement theories leave to proceed. The current operative complaint, filed November 2024, focuses on training-data infringement and output similarity for style-specific prompts. Expert discovery closes August 29, 2026; summary judgment motions are due October 31, 2026 (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__). This case is significant for the visual-arts class because it tests whether "style" as a prompt parameter produces infringing outputs — a theory without clear precedent under Ninth Circuit copyright law, which holds that style is not protectable (*Rentmeester v. Nike*, 883 F.3d 1111 (9th Cir. 2018)) but recognizes that sufficiently specific expression can be reproduced even absent literal copying.

OpenAI* as a content-watch trigger — the reply will consolidate plaintiffs' best counter-arguments to the Latham brief and likely preview the oral-argument themes for a Fall 2026 sitting before Judge Chin's panel, the most consequential oral argument in AI copyright history to date.
