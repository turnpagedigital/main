### I. Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Settlement Administration Window

**Current posture: Awaiting final approval order; distribution calculation milestone passed June 11.**


The fairness hearing took place on May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement.
 
After a 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement — but given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect her to give final approval relatively quickly.


The hearing turned on three axes: claims participation, attorneys' fees, and late opt-outs. 
Lead plaintiffs' counsel Justin Nelson reported that the opt-in rate had climbed from 91.3% the prior month to 92.77%, and there was no indication that the per-work payout — approximately $3,000 to $3,100 — would change if the settlement is approved.
 
Attorneys told the court that opt-out and objection rates were "minuscule," a strong sign the deal meets the "fair, reasonable, and adequate" standard.



At one point during the hearing, the judge asked counsel to identify three leading cases supporting the size of the fee request; class counsel's responses leaned on the result for the class — a $1.5 billion settlement and the high claims rate — and on the difficulty of litigating an untested area of law.
 
Plaintiffs' counsel are seeking 25% of the fund, or $375 million, in attorneys' fees.
 Whether the court accepts, reduces, or modifies that request is the main variable left open under submission.


Post-hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered that by May 21, 2026, Anthropic could file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and that plaintiffs shall file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026.
 
The court stated it would not require anything more from the objectors, nor would it consider further submissions from them.


**Payment mechanics — where the money flows.** 
The settlement fund must cover attorneys' fees, JND Legal Administration costs, $50,000 service awards per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for three class representatives), and Special Master fees for resolving disputes between class members.
 
Anthropic is paying into the $1.5 billion fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.
 Per the plan of allocation confirmed in pre-hearing filings, 
distribution calculations were scheduled for June 11, 2026 — the earliest payments to class members if there are no objector appeals; summer 2026 is the earliest if objectors are present but file no appeals.
 A second Anthropic payment tranche of $450 million is scheduled for September 25, 2026 (twelve months after the preliminary approval order), with a further $450 million due September 25, 2027. (__[Independent Publishers Guild Timeline](https://www.independentpublishersguild.com/Common/Uploaded%20files/IPG%20document%20library/IPG%20email%20documents/Anthropic%20settlement_Timeline.pdf)__)

**Scope caveat that transfers to parallel litigation.** 
The scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood: *Bartz* is not a settlement about AI training. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic's use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and that Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired. Nothing about this settlement disturbs those rulings.
 
What the settlement resolves is Anthropic's potential liability for downloading and storage of files from two shadow libraries — Library Genesis (LibGen) and Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi).
 That distinction is analytically important for claims counsel evaluating shadow-library exposure across other AI defendants: Judge Alsup's training-data fair-use ruling remains intact, and Bartz's $1.5 billion is precedent-free on the merits.

**Objector-appeal risk.** 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 Seven objectors spoke at the May 14 hearing; attorney James Bartolomei spoke for five minutes on behalf of multiple objectors, which is the most organized objector presence. Whether any of those objectors — or an absent opt-out — pursues a Ninth Circuit appeal is the single largest timeline variable. Counsel tracking distribution timing should assume a six-to-twelve-month appeal buffer remains live.

### II. Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal.) — Cross-MSJ Under Submission

**Current posture: Publishers' partial summary judgment motion fully briefed; Anthropic cross-MSJ outstanding; ruling pending before Judge Eumi K. Lee.**


CourtListener last updated this docket June 22, 2026; the case is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee, with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen referred for discovery, in the Northern District of California (No. 5:24-cv-03811).



Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO have asked Judge Lee to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrights — and to reject the fair-use defense. The partial summary judgment motion was filed March 23, 2026, in a case first brought in October 2023 over 499 copyrighted musical works.


The publishers' brief makes the § 107 case in explicit commercial-competitor terms. 
On fair use, publishers argue Anthropic's use was purely commercial — noting it is "a for-profit technology company valued at $380 billion or more" with a revenue run rate approaching $14 billion — that it copied lyrics in full, and that its output directly competes with licensed lyrics services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch.
 This framing maps directly onto the fourth *Campbell* factor — market substitution under 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) — and is the most aggressive § 107(4) argument yet briefed in the AI-training docket.


On the infringement side, the filing states that Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works," and that Anthropic "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."
 
The publishers also allege that Anthropic's human reviewers "chose" model output that accurately reproduced copyrighted lyrics while "rejecting" responses with inaccurate lyrics, with internal Anthropic communications featuring prominently.
 
An August 2023 internal memo is cited as stating that AI models like Claude "memorize A LOT, like a LOT"; CEO Dario Amodei is quoted as stating in an April 2024 interview that AI models should not be "verbatim outputting copyrighted content," while also testifying in deposition that doing so is "against the law."


The amicus front is noteworthy. 
The coalition — which includes the RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, SONA, BMAC, the Music Artists Coalition, and the Artist Rights Alliance — filed an amicus brief on March 30, 2026, urging Judge Lee to reject Anthropic's fair-use defense.
 
A second amicus brief, filed by the Association of American Publishers, News/Media Alliance, the International Association of Scientific, Technical & Medical Publishers, and the Authors Guild, reinforced the point by presenting an extensive chart of AI licensing deals involving OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and Perplexity — naming dozens of publisher partners.
 
The briefs characterize Anthropic as a "holdout" that has opted to scrape copyrighted content rather than license it like its peers.


**The second, larger lawsuit.** 
The original *Concord v. Anthropic* case — filed in October 2023 over 499 works — has been joined by a second, far larger lawsuit filed in January 2026, covering more than 20,000 songs and seeking over $3 billion in statutory damages.
 
That second action is No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal.), also assigned to Judge Lee and Magistrate Judge Van Keulen, date filed January 28, 2026.
 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay that second case; a motion hearing was set for May 20, 2026, before Judge Lee in Courtroom 7, 4th Floor, San Jose.
 Outcome of that stay motion is not yet confirmed in the public record as of this writing.

**BMG pile-on.** 
BMG Rights Management filed its own separate copyright infringement lawsuit against Anthropic earlier this year.
 The proliferation of music-industry plaintiffs means Anthropic's training-data exposure on the lyrics front is now multi-fronted, with potential 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) statutory damages of $150,000 per willful infringement across tens of thousands of works — a figure that dwarfs the *Bartz* settlement if publishers prevail.

**The Claude-as-its-own-expert problem.** 
A California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic confirmed she used Claude to help draft the expert's declaration that included the erroneous citation, characterizing the error as "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 This episode has evidentiary implications beyond mere embarrassment: any argument that Claude's outputs are reliable and non-substitutable for licensed sources becomes harder to make in the same courtroom.

### III. In re: OpenAI, Inc., Copyright Infringement Litigation, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein) — Discovery Active

**Current posture: Fact discovery ongoing; privilege ruling entered February 2026; 20 million ChatGPT logs ordered produced January 2026.**


CourtListener last updated this docket June 26, 2026; the MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, in the Southern District of New York (No. 1:25-md-03143).



On January 5, 2026, Judge Stein affirmed a magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20-million-log sample of anonymized ChatGPT conversations — not just the cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' works that OpenAI wanted to hand over. The ruling marked a significant discovery victory for news organizations and authors suing OpenAI.


On privilege, OpenAI fared better. 
On February 6, 2026, Judge Stein sustained OpenAI's objection under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 72(a) and set aside the magistrate judge's privilege-waiver order in its entirety, finding each basis for waiver to be "clearly erroneous" or "contrary to law."
 
Judge Stein held that OpenAI's statements that the datasets were deleted "due to non-use" did not reveal any legal advice from an attorney; because the statements were not privileged themselves, disclosing them could not trigger a waiver.
 For defendants in similar positions — AI companies facing training-data deletion inquiries — this ruling offers a structured defense against at-issue waiver claims.


In April 2025, the U.S. Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated twelve cases against OpenAI originating in the Southern District of New York and the Northern District of California.
 
The MDL encompasses class actions originally filed in California by authors, New York suits brought by news organizations, DMCA-focused suits, and a case brought by an online video creator.
 Discovery scope is contested: 
the court granted a stay as to models not already in the MDL — specifically the o1, o1 mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 models
 — limiting, at least temporarily, the scope of document discovery obligations across OpenAI's newer model generations.

**Licensing deals as § 107(4) comparators.** 
In a related development, Disney and OpenAI signed a deal allowing OpenAI's Sora to generate AI videos featuring Disney's iconic characters; under the three-year agreement, Disney will invest $1 billion in OpenAI and become a major customer.
 
This development may spark a trend toward formal licensing of copyrighted material for generative AI use, and may also affect the fair-use analysis.
 The practical point for litigation counsel: every negotiated AI licensing deal at scale — whether Disney/OpenAI or the dozens catalogued in the music publishers' amicus brief — strengthens the § 107(4) market-harm argument by establishing that a functioning licensing market exists and that defendants chose not to participate in it.

### IV. System-Level Observations — What Transfers Across the Docket

Three analytical threads carry across the full AI-copyright docket as of June 29:

**Shadow-library liability vs. training-data fair use.** Judge Alsup's June 2025 ruling in *Bartz* that training on lawfully-acquired books is fair use remains undisturbed. The *Bartz* $1.5 billion settlement is exclusively about piracy from LibGen and PiLiMi. 
Law firms like Susman Godfrey have developed what analysts call a "Shadow Library Strategy" — attempting to skip the debate over whether AI training is fair use and go straight for the piracy angle. Most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies now allege that defendants downloaded from shadow libraries.
 Claims investors should track which defendants have documented shadow-library exposure and which are confined to the training-data fair-use debate, where the judicial trend (Alsup in *Bartz*; Chhabria's signals in *Kadrey*) currently favors defendants.

**Verbatim output as the pivotal § 107(4) fact.** The *Concord* publishers' MSJ turns heavily on evidence that Claude reproduces lyrics verbatim in response to user prompts — i.e., that the model substitutes for a licensed lyrics service. This is categorically different from the book-summarization theory in the OpenAI MDL, where Judge Stein has already narrowed claims premised on outputs that merely summarize or outline rather than reproduce. Rights-holder counsel should identify at the outset whether their claim rests on verbatim reproduction (stronger; *Concord* posture) or on downstream market cannibalization through summarization (weaker; current *OpenAI MDL* posture under Stein's motion-to-dismiss rulings).

**Human-authorship doctrine — closed.** 
On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming the principle that material must have human authorship to be copyrightable.
 That ruling forecloses AI-output copyright claims from the AI developer's side and has no near-term appellate offramp.

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should treat the next two to four weeks in *Bartz* as the critical distribution-timeline window: if the final approval order has issued — or issues shortly — the 60-day non-filing claimant notice deadline and first distribution wave follow mechanically from that date, with Anthropic's initial $300 million tranche due within five business days of the order. Verify the order status directly on CourtListener docket 69058235 before communicating any payment timeline to claimants. In *Concord*, the cross-MSJ is the most consequential pending ruling in the music-training vertical; monitor Judge Lee's docket (No. 5:24-cv-03811) for any order on the publishers' partial MSJ and the motion to stay the second action (No. 5:26-cv-00880) — a stay denial would immediately trigger discovery obligations across 20,000+ additional works and $3 billion in potential statutory exposure. In the OpenAI MDL, the 20-million-log production is in plaintiffs' hands; counsel should evaluate whether any forthcoming expert analysis of those logs surfaces verbatim-reproduction evidence that would sharpen the § 107 factor-four argument and potentially prompt additional consolidation or claim-value adjustments across the class. Finally, any rights-holder whose works appear in both the *Bartz* class and a parallel AI proceeding against a different defendant should confirm that the *Bartz* release is narrowly scoped to Anthropic's LibGen/PiLiMi conduct — the release does not bar claims against OpenAI, Meta, or any other AI developer for independent training-data acts.
