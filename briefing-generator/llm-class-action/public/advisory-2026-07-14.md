### I. Bartz v. Anthropic, 3:24-cv-05417 — Final Approval Under Submission; Five Late Opt-Outs at Issue


Presiding Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, leaving final approval pending.
 The blocking issue is compact: 
in a supplemental filing ordered after the fairness hearing, Anthropic urged the court not to permit five untimely opt-outs, arguing that the two objectors who sought relief failed to show "excusable neglect" and that the remaining three late opt-out requests should also be denied for lack of any record supporting relief and because other rightsholders have not consented.


The fairness hearing itself went smoothly on the merits. 
Attorneys told the court that the settlement claims rate had grown to nearly 93%, while the opt-out and objection rates were "minuscule," a strong sign that the deal meets the standard of being "fair, reasonable, and adequate."
 Specifically, 
the claims rate stood at 91.3%, with claims filed for 440,490 of the 482,460 works on the Works List (Dkt. 643), up from the 54% reported in the March 19 motion for final approval.


The fee structure drew the sharpest pre-hearing scrutiny. 
Authors urged the California federal judge to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 A parallel dispute over non-class counsel fees also crystallized: 
Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees that nonclass counsel requested as part of the settlement is far too high, with "scant justification" for the request.


The five late opt-out requests are individually distinct. The tracked-case ground truth confirms that Dkt. 678 (filed July 10, 2026) is a brief re a Passman late opt-out request — the most recent entry on the docket as of the July 13 sync. 
Among the late opt-out movants, the Mexican author of *Como agua para chocolate* challenges Class Counsel's novel "co-owner consent" requirement for her late opt-out, arguing the Long Form Notice expressly provides that a single rightsholder's decision to opt out binds co-authors and publishers. She also argues she meets the *Pioneer* excusable-neglect standard: she did not receive notice until March 3, 2026 — three weeks after the deadline — lives in Mexico and reads Spanish but received no Spanish-language notice, and suffered a stroke during the relevant period.


The structural tension embedded in this settlement — publisher vs. author allocation — remains unresolved at the individual-claim level. 
James Sills (Dkt. 600) reported that Elsevier proposed to take 88% of the per-work settlement amount for one of his books, leaving 12% for the author.
 The settlement's default framework 
presumes a 50-50 split between authors and publishers where no documentation supports a different allocation; if no parties contest that default, the settlement money will be divided equally.


For rights-holder counsel tracking the distribution timeline: 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The September 25, 2026 installment is the next tranche due. A final approval order must issue before the administrator can calculate per-work distributions; 
the settlement administrator was projected to calculate distributions around June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement beginning June 2026 or later, subject to change.
 That timeline has slipped with the submission period still open.

The macro liability backstory bears repeating because it anchors the settlement's pricing: 
in June 2025, Judge William Alsup issued a pivotal summary judgment ruling, holding that Anthropic's use of lawfully acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and thus protected by fair use.
 The shadow-library piracy track was carved out. 
Judge Alsup certified a class of U.S. copyright holders whose works Anthropic downloaded from shadow libraries — potentially numbering in the millions — with statutory copyright damages reaching up to $150,000 per infringed work, placing Anthropic's theoretical exposure in the hundreds of billions of dollars.
 That existential exposure drove the settlement; the $1.5 billion figure, at roughly 
$3,000 per work covered by the class
, represents a fraction of maximum statutory damages but a settlement of unprecedented scale for copyright claims.

**Practical note for claims investors:** the five untimely opt-out requests are individually de minimis to the overall fund but are holding up the approval order. Judge Martínez-Olguín's ruling on them will likely set the scope of any 9th Circuit objector appeal risk. A court that denies all five late opt-outs on excusable-neglect grounds closes that avenue; one that grants even one admission of defective notice could encourage broader post-approval challenges.

### II. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Concord I Cross-MSJ Fully Briefed; Concord II Stay Motion Pending

*Concord Music Group, Inc., et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.), before Judge Eumi K. Lee (Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen), is now the most consequential AI fair-use proceeding with a live, unresolved ruling. 
The first-filed action has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues governing these claims.
 Cross-motions were scheduled to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026. (__[Anthropic Motion to Stay, 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Motion-to-Stay-Concord-Music-II-1_59.pdf)__)

The publishers filed their motion for partial summary judgment on March 23, 2026. 
Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO asked the court to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrights and to reject Anthropic's fair use defense; the motion covers 499 copyrighted musical works and is accompanied by a 47-page statement setting out 218 "undisputed facts" supported by deposition testimony, internal Anthropic documents, and the company's own admissions.
 On the key fair use prong four, 
the publishers argue that Anthropic's use was purely commercial — noting the company is a "for-profit technology company valued at $380 billion or more" with a revenue run rate approaching $14 billion — that it copied lyrics in full, and that its output directly competes with licensed lyrics services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch.


The Anthropic output-infringement evidence is stark: 
the publishers claim that in many cases, "Claude output Publishers' lyrics even when users did not actually ask for those lyrics or when users requested 'new' content."
 
The filing also cites data showing AI-generated songs are increasingly flooding streaming platforms, with a Deezer study estimating over 60,000 AI-generated tracks were being submitted to the platform daily by January 2026, accounting for 3% of total streams.


The amicus coalition aligned against Anthropic is substantive. 
A coalition including the RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, SONA, BMAC, the Music Artists Coalition, and the Artist Rights Alliance filed an amicus brief urging the court to reject Anthropic's fair use defense.
 
A second amicus brief, filed by the Association of American Publishers, News/Media Alliance, the International Association of Scientific, Technical & Medical Publishers, and the Authors Guild, makes similar arguments from the perspective of the book, news, and academic publishing industries.
 Critically, the coalition's market-harm framing is pointed: 
it characterizes Anthropic as a "holdout" that has opted to scrape copyrighted content rather than license it like its peers.
 
The publishing coalition's brief presents an extensive chart of AI licensing deals involving companies including OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and Perplexity — naming dozens of publisher partners from the Associated Press and the New York Times to Condé Nast and HarperCollins.
 That chart is 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) market-harm evidence: the existence of a functioning licensing market undercuts Anthropic's claim that AI training creates no substitution effect.

Anthropic simultaneously faces a second, far larger action. 
Major music publishers filed a second, $3 billion suit against Anthropic covering newly discovered mass infringement of sheet music and songbooks
 — designated *Concord II*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK. 
Anthropic moved to stay *Concord II* pending final resolution of *Concord I* or, alternatively, pending resolution of the parties' motions for summary judgment and any subsequent appeal in *Concord I*.
 The stay hearing was noticed for May 20, 2026, before Judge Lee in Courtroom 7, San Jose. The stay motion is logical: 
the threshold legal issues in *Concord I* — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — will determine the viability of most if not all of the publishers' claims in *Concord II* and could have preclusive effect.


The Concord I expert misconduct episode also merits tracking for discovery-integrity purposes. 
A California federal magistrate judge partially stricken an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic confirmed she used Claude.ai to help draft the expert's declaration containing the erroneous citation, arguing the error was "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 The episode has procedural significance beyond the embarrassment: it tested Judge Lee's standing orders on AI-assisted drafting and set a record that publisher counsel will cite in any future credibility disputes over Anthropic's expert submissions.

### III. In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.) — Sanctions Motion on Training-Data Search Concealment

The most consequential new filing in the AI copyright docket this week originated in the OpenAI MDL, not the Anthropic cases. 
On July 9, 2026, a group of newspapers including the New York Times and the New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan to sanction OpenAI in their high-stakes copyright dispute for allegedly lying to the court about its ability to search its systems for proof that it misused millions of their articles in AI training.


The factual allegation is direct: 
the newspapers told the court in a filing that OpenAI falsely told the court it could not search its large language models for their copyrighted material while hiding that it had done so "even before the first News Plaintiff filed suit."
 
The newspapers said OpenAI had also deleted billions of relevant ChatGPT conversations or made them unsearchable, and they asked for sanctions including attorneys' fees and a court finding that OpenAI's chat logs showed the company misused their copyrighted works.


The MDL is before Judge Sidney H. Stein, S.D.N.Y., with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang handling discovery. 
The docket was last updated July 10, 2026; the case is styled *In Re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.).
 The preservation order context is critical: 
Magistrate Judge Wang issued a May 13, 2025 preservation order requiring OpenAI to retain all ChatGPT conversation logs — affecting over 400 million users worldwide.
 The sanctions motion argues OpenAI violated the spirit of that order, or worse, anticipated the litigation and destroyed evidence before it issued.

If the court grants even a permissive-inference sanction — short of case-ending default — the evidentiary posture in the news-publisher track shifts materially. The chat-log production was already the most consequential discovery ruling in the AI copyright ecosystem: 
the court-ordered preservation and production of ChatGPT logs sets the template that the book-author cases (Bartz, MDL 3143, Kadrey) all operate against.
 A finding of concealment would validate the publishers' § 504 statutory-damages theory and complicate OpenAI's ability to assert transformative-use defenses credibly.

### IV. Parallel Docket Context: Thomson Reuters v. Ross on Appeal; Thaler Cert. Denied

Two framework-level developments from earlier in 2026 continue to shape briefing across the ecosystem.


In *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* (D. Del.), the court granted summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Ross Intelligence's use of headnotes to train its AI legal research tool was not fair use. The case is currently on appeal before the Third Circuit.
 
On May 23, 2025, the court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard." In its appeal brief filed September 2025, Ross argued that if the decision stands it will have "sweeping consequences for innovation" in both legal research and artificial intelligence.
 The Third Circuit briefing represents the first circuit-level test of AI training fair use and will be closely watched alongside the Concord I summary judgment ruling from Judge Lee — the two together will produce the first multi-court body of appellate and district authority that practitioners can cite against each other.


On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari of Dr. Stephen Thaler's appeal in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming the principle that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable.
 The *Thaler* cert. denial closes the AI-authorship question at the Supreme Court for the foreseeable future and eliminates one potential avenue by which AI developers might have argued that AI-generated training outputs carry independent copyright protection.


Book-author copyright suits against the major LLM developers remain concentrated in two venues: the Northern District of California — where the Bartz settlement and the *Kadrey v. Meta* fair-use ruling came down — and the Southern District of New York, where MDL 3143 now bundles the Authors Guild, Tremblay, Silverman, Chabon, Alter/Sancton, and Basbanes cases.
 A third cluster has emerged: 
the plaintiff class in a December 22, 2025 N.D. Cal. filing (25-cv-10897) includes Bartz settlement opt-outs led by Pulitzer winner John Carreyrou, with the complaint alleging defendants copied authors' books from LibGen, Z-Library, and OceanofPDF.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should do three things before September. First, monitor the *Bartz* docket for Judge Martínez-Olguín's ruling on the five untimely opt-out requests — that order will define the final-approval scope and start the distribution clock toward the September 25, 2026 Anthropic installment; any class member with an unresolved allocation dispute (especially publisher-vs.-author splits on the order of the Elsevier/Sills 88/12 split) should engage with the JND Legal Administration administrator at AnthropicCopyrightSettlement.com now, before per-work calculations are locked. Second, track the *Concord I* summary judgment ruling from Judge Lee — this will be the first contested fair-use ruling on AI training of musical works and will generate the § 107(4) market-harm analysis that every subsequent AI-training defendant and rights-holder will cite; if the court rules against Anthropic on the training-data prong, it forecloses the argument that Bartz's "lawfully acquired" carve-out generalizes to scraper-assembled datasets. Third, watch the *In re OpenAI* sanctions proceeding before Judge Stein — a finding of bad-faith spoliation or discovery concealment would be the most significant procedural development in the AI copyright litigation system since the May 2025 preservation order, and it would materially shift the settlement posture for the news-publisher claims within MDL 3143.
