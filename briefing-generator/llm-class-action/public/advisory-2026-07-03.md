### *Bartz v. Anthropic*, 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal., Judge Martínez-Olguín) — Settlement Administration Posture

The settlement fund and its distribution mechanics are now set. 
Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
The first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 The claims population is effectively final: 
the hearing updated the claims rate to 92.77% — 447,576 of 482,460 works — with roughly 53 objections logged.
 That rate is extraordinary by class-action norms; 
the final claims rate vastly surpasses the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed on March 19, 2026, indicating that a large number of claims came in at the very end of the claims process.
 The blended result, however, is that per-work recoveries converge on the floor: 
the high rate of claims means that forecasts about a bigger payout based on the March 19 report of 54% will not happen, and the actual payout will likely end up closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.


The attorney fee structure remains contested and is central to the final approval analysis. 
Authors urged the court to grant final approval along with an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 Co-lead counsel from Lieff Cabraser Heimann & Bernstein (Rachel Geman) and Susman Godfrey (Rohit Nath) negotiated the reduction. 
Separately, Anthropic argued that the $75 million in fees that non-class counsel requested is "far too high," with "scant justification" for the request.


The intraclass fairness objections presented at the May 14 hearing remain live until the approval order issues. Three structural fault lines dominate: first, 
objector Professor Lea Victoria Bishop argued that publishers could ultimately claim roughly 50% or more of the total settlement amount, a profound departure from an author-centric lawsuit.
 Second, the group-registration problem: 
author Pinder registered 40-plus independently published novels under a single group copyright registration; under the settlement, each copyright registration counts as one claimable Work, so she receives one share where a separately registered author with 40 books would receive 40 — and when she raised this with class counsel, she was told that counting her individually published books separately could constitute a "windfall."
 Third, the education-works split: 
objector James Sills reported that Elsevier proposed to take 88% of the per-work settlement amount for one of his books, leaving 12% for the author; the settlement's default split for non-education works is 50/50, but education works including textbooks are subject to negotiation and arbitration governed by the Special Master process.


The opt-out litigation overhang adds a further variable. 
Twenty-eight authors who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal., No. 3:26-cv-04482, demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works; a follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.
 Those cases will now proceed independently once the Bartz approval order becomes final, and they preserve the full § 504 statutory damages theory — up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement — that the class settlement did not litigate to judgment.

### *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* — Two Parallel Dockets in N.D. Cal.

There are now two distinct Concord actions in the Northern District, both assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen referred.

**Concord I** (*Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK, N.D. Cal., filed Oct. 18, 2023) is the original music-publisher action. 
The complaint was filed by Concord Music Group, Universal Music Corp., Songs of Universal, ABKCO Music, Polygram Publishing, Universal Music-MGB NA LLC, Universal Music-Z Tunes LLC, and Capitol CMG.
 In Concord I, the court found in favor of Anthropic on two consecutive motions: 
the court denied plaintiffs' motion for a preliminary injunction as overbroad and denied Anthropic's motion to dismiss as to secondary infringement and CMI claims; the court also found that plaintiffs did not sufficiently allege that Anthropic acted intentionally as to the removal of CMI, given plaintiffs' acknowledgment that CMI was not removed across the board throughout Claude's training process.
 As of the June 22, 2026 docket update, Concord I remains active with cross-motions for summary judgment on the fair-use question pending.

**Concord II** (*Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK, N.D. Cal., filed Jan. 28, 2026) is the torrenting action. 
The complaint alleges that Anthropic concealed during discovery in Concord I that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books — including hundreds or more books that plainly contain publishers' musical compositions — from pirate library websites, and that publishers did not learn of this torrenting until July 2025, when Judge Alsup issued a ruling in *Bartz* that publicly revealed the conduct for the first time.
 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay Concord II, with a motion hearing set for May 20, 2026, before Judge Lee in San Jose, Courtroom 7, 4th Floor.
 The CourtListener docket for Concord II was last updated May 31, 2026; the stay ruling has not been confirmed in available public data.

The Concord system is the most consequential active fair-use battleground in this docket cluster because it presents a music-specific question that Bartz's training-on-pirated-books analysis did not resolve: whether AI output reproduction of song lyrics — rather than mere storage of books — constitutes a separate and independently cognizable infringement. 
Plaintiffs, led by Concord and Universal Music Corp., claim Anthropic infringes their exclusive rights to musical lyrics both by using their works to train Claude and by distributing the lyrics in Claude's outputs when Claude is prompted.

### *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein; Mag. Judge Ona T. Wang) — Discovery Volume Escalates

The OpenAI MDL docket was updated July 2, 2026, the eve of this advisory. 
The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein, with referral to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, S.D.N.Y., case No. 1:25-md-03143.
 Discovery is the current pressure point. 
As of March 9, 2026, the court granted plaintiffs' motion to compel and ordered OpenAI to produce "the reservoirs of 78 million and 10 million logs," on top of the 20 million log sample already ordered to be produced — bringing the total compelled production to approximately 108 million output logs.


The January 5, 2026 20-million-log order anchors that posture. 
Judge Stein affirmed Magistrate Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20-million-log sample, not just the cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' works that OpenAI wanted to hand over.
 The court's reasoning on relevance is significant for § 107(4) market-harm analysis: 
Judge Wang found that even output logs without reproductions of plaintiffs' works are discoverable because they bear on OpenAI's fair-use defense — fair use analysis examines how the challenged use affects the market for the original works, and logs showing what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries could reveal patterns relevant to whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works.


The *NYT v. OpenAI* component of the MDL (1:23-cv-11195, also assigned to Judge Stein) has reached the discovery sanction phase. 
On April 8, 2026, Magistrate Judge Wang found that OpenAI's corporate designee was unprepared on noticed topics and ordered OpenAI to re-designate and produce a properly prepared 30(b)(6) witness.
 
The Times also seeks a permanent injunction and "destruction" of GPT models trained on Times works under 17 U.S.C. § 503(b) — a remedy that, if granted, would have industry-wide implications for deployed LLMs.


Within the MDL, the Ziff Davis sub-action (1:25-cv-04315-SHS-OTW) produced a notable ruling in December 2025 on model-scope: 
Judge Stein granted OpenAI's motion to dismiss claims 4 and 5, denied the motion as to claims 3, 6, and 7, and granted a stay as to models not already in the MDL — specifically the o1, o1 mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 models.
 The stay rationale matters: 
the burden on OpenAI of potentially expansive discovery into these models — which are not otherwise at issue in this MDL — would involve additional deponents and documents and would be contrary to the interests of the court and public in a speedy resolution of the core copyright issues.
 That ruling limits, at least temporarily, the models within the MDL's substantive discovery scope — a significant constraint on plaintiffs' fact-gathering on OpenAI's newest and most commercially significant products.

### Cross-System Framework: Fair-Use Divergence Between N.D. Cal. and S.D.N.Y.

The most durable structural tension in this docket cluster is now a circuit-level divergence in the making. 
Two federal judges in separate cases have found that training AI models is highly transformative and protected by fair use — the *Bartz* order (June 23, 2025) and the *Kadrey v. Meta* order (June 25, 2025) — with both courts noting that each AI model's use of copyrighted material was highly transformative, a key element of satisfying the fair use doctrine.
 Those two N.D. Cal. rulings represent fair use under § 107(1) for training on legitimately acquired copies.

The S.D.N.Y. posture, by contrast, is running a different track. 
Judge Stein's rulings allow The Times to continue pursuing claims that OpenAI's outputs included copyrighted content and resulted in user infringement, which diverges from the stance of California judges who have dismissed similar allegations.
 The divergence between the two jurisdictions on output-based liability will ultimately require resolution either by settlement, by Ninth Circuit review of any California appeals, or by Second Circuit review of the MDL's eventual summary judgment rulings. Rights-holders with claims in both jurisdictions should be treating S.D.N.Y. as the more hospitable forum on output-infringement theories.

One additional data point on the supremacy of human authorship: 
on March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming the principle that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable
 — a ruling that forecloses the AI-authorship defense and, incidentally, confirms that every AI output plaintiffs point to as infringing was necessarily produced by a human-authored model. That does not help defendants on liability.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and claims-recovery counsel should treat the Bartz docket's July 1, 2026 filings as the immediate priority: pull those entries from PACER to determine whether Judge Martínez-Olguín has entered final approval, issued a supplemental order on late opt-outs, or set a further hearing date — the answer governs whether the September 25, 2026 second installment payment ($375 million or equivalent tranche) is on track or delayed pending appeal. Claims investors holding assignments from class members should model a late-2026 to early-2027 payment timeline consistent with a fall approval scenario, factoring in the risk of a Ninth Circuit objector appeal that could delay distribution further under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)'s statutory damage release mechanism. On the opt-out litigation track, *Cruz v. Anthropic* (3:26-cv-04482) and *Kwon v. Anthropic* are the vehicles to watch for the full willful-infringement § 504 theory; any early ruling on the permanent-injunction ask under § 503(b) — mirroring the NYT's demand in S.D.N.Y. — would be the most consequential new precedent since *Bartz*'s summary judgment order. For Concord I and Concord II claimants and music publishers tracking those matters, Anthropic's stay motion in Concord II and the unresolved cross-MSJ in Concord I both need PACER verification against the June 22 and May 31 docket cut-offs before advising on litigation posture.
