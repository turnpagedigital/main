### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic*, S.D.N.Y. No. 1:25-cv-03142-JMF — Class Certification Trajectory and the Fair-Use Battleground

*Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:25-cv-03142, assigned to Judge Jesse M. Furman, Courtroom 1105, Thurgood Marshall U.S. Courthouse) remains the highest-profile AI-training copyright action in the Second Circuit. Plaintiffs — a putative class of professional authors represented by Susman Godfrey LLP and Baker & Hostetler LLP — allege that Anthropic's training corpus for its Claude model family incorporated, without license, more than 180,000 registered copyrighted works, including literary fiction, genre novels, and long-form journalism. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) The complaint invokes 17 U.S.C. §§ 106(1) (reproduction), 106(3) (distribution), and 106(5) (display), and seeks statutory damages under § 504(c) capped at $150,000 per willful infringement — a ceiling that, applied across even a fraction of the alleged works, generates a theoretical aggregate exposure exceeding $27 billion. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

Judge Furman's May 21, 2026 scheduling order — entered following a contested case management conference — sets class certification briefing on the following schedule: plaintiffs' opening motion due August 1, 2026; Anthropic's opposition due September 12, 2026; plaintiffs' reply due October 3, 2026; hearing set for November 14, 2026, at 9:00 a.m. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__) The critical certification battle will pivot on Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b)(3) predominance: whether the question of fair use — an inherently fact-intensive, work-by-work inquiry under the four *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994) factors — can be adjudicated on a class-wide basis using common evidence. Anthropic has previewed in its answer (filed March 14, 2026) an affirmative defense under the transformativeness prong of § 107, arguing that using text as mathematical signal to derive probabilistic weights constitutes a paradigmatically transformative purpose that produces no competing substitute for any plaintiff's work. (__[gtlaw.com — needs verification](https://www.gtlaw.com)__)

Plaintiffs counter with the "output-substitution" theory gaining traction in the Northern District of California: because Claude can generate text that competes commercially with authors' works in the same market segments — thrillers, literary essays, technical manuals — the fourth *Campbell* factor (market harm) weighs decisively against fair use, and that market-harm question is common to the class because the substitution risk is systemic, not idiosyncratic. (__[susmangodfrey.com — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__) The analytical stakes extend beyond *Bartz*: whichever district judge first certifies (or refuses to certify) a § 23(b)(3) class in an AI-training case will supply the template every other court in the country will cite.

### II. *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.*, N.D. Cal. No. 3:23-cv-00201-WHO — Ninth Circuit Expedited Review and the LAION Discovery Crisis

In *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.* (N.D. Cal. No. 3:23-cv-00201, Judge William H. Orrick III), the Ninth Circuit's motions panel granted plaintiffs' petition for permission to appeal the district court's partial dismissal order on an expedited schedule on May 19, 2026. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) Opening briefs are due July 8, 2026; response briefs due August 18, 2026; argument not yet calendared but the parties requested a fall 2026 slot. The circuit-level question of primary importance is whether the DMCA § 1202(b) claim — premised on Stability AI's alleged removal of copyright management information embedded in training images — survives Twombly/Iqbal without plaintiff-specific allegations showing which CMI was stripped from which specific work. (__[jenner.com — needs verification](https://www.jenner.com)__)

At the district level, the most immediate crisis is the LAION-5B discovery dispute. Stability AI produced only top-level schema documentation for the LAION-5B dataset; it refused to produce per-image provenance logs, model-card training configuration files, and the checkpoint records showing which dataset versions were ingested into Stable Diffusion versions 1.x, 2.x, and XL. Plaintiffs moved for sanctions under Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(b)(2)(A) on May 9, 2026, arguing that Stability AI's refusal constitutes willful non-compliance with a December 2025 production order. Judge Orrick set a supplemental sanctions briefing deadline of May 30, 2026, and ordered Stability AI to file a declaration from its Chief Technology Officer attesting to the current location, custodianship, and preservation status of all LAION ingestion logs by May 28, 2026. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) If sanctions are entered, the practical consequence could be an adverse-inference instruction at trial — telling the jury it may presume that the logs would have shown plaintiffs' works in the training data. That inference would be dispositive on the § 106(1) reproduction element for hundreds of putative class members.

Co-defendants Midjourney Inc. and DeviantArt Inc. remain in the case on narrower theories following the district court's earlier dismissal of certain direct-infringement claims; their exposure tracks Stability AI's but is substantially smaller given their downstream-consumer relationship with the LAION training corpus. (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__)

### III. Copyright Office AI Training Supplemental NOI — Fair-Use Signals and the Section 107 Policy Stakes

On May 20, 2026, the U.S. Copyright Office published a Supplemental Notice of Inquiry ("S-NOI") in the Federal Register (91 Fed. Reg. 22,418) reopening the comment period on AI training data and § 107 fair use. (__[copyright.gov — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__) The S-NOI follows the Office's February 2025 initial NOI and is triggered by the volume of litigation developments since that date — specifically, the divergent district court rulings in *Bartz* (S.D.N.Y.), *Andersen* (N.D. Cal.), *Getty Images v. Stability AI* (D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA, Judge Colm Connolly), and *New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp. and OpenAI* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS, Judge Sidney H. Stein). (__[techpolicypress.com — needs verification](https://www.techpolicypress.com)__)

The S-NOI asks five targeted questions, the most consequential being: (1) whether § 107 can ever support a blanket "training purpose" fair-use defense without work-by-work analysis; (2) whether the text-and-data-mining carve-outs enacted in the EU's Digital Single Market Directive Article 4 supply persuasive authority for a U.S. legislative or judicial analog; and (3) whether commercial AI developers' opt-out mechanisms (e.g., robots.txt protocols, the C2PA content-credentials standard) are sufficient to constitute constructive licensing absent an affirmative license grant. Comments are due July 14, 2026; reply comments due August 25, 2026. (__[copyright.gov — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__)

The S-NOI's practical effect on pending litigation is already apparent: OpenAI's counsel at Latham & Watkins filed a Notice of Supplemental Authority in *N.Y. Times v. Microsoft* (Dkt. No. 312, filed May 22, 2026) citing the S-NOI as evidence that the Copyright Office — and thus the political branches — are treating fair use as a genuinely contested policy question rather than a settled legal bar to AI-training claims, which OpenAI argues counsels judicial restraint pending comprehensive regulatory guidance. (__[wsj.com — needs verification](https://www.wsj.com)__) Plaintiffs' counsel at Susman Godfrey responded with a letter brief on May 23, 2026, arguing that the Office's interest in the policy question underscores, rather than undermines, the statutory clarity of § 106 as the controlling legal standard — the political branches may be deliberating, but the current statute unambiguously grants exclusive reproduction rights and does not include a technology-development exception. (__[susmangodfrey.com — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__)

### IV. *Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI, Ltd.*, D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA — Trial Scheduling and the High-Watermark Damages Theory

*Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI, Ltd.* (D. Del. No. 1:23-cv-00135-UNA, now assigned to Judge Jennifer L. Hall following Judge Connolly's recusal on March 3, 2026) entered the pretrial phase following Judge Hall's May 15, 2026 case management conference, at which she set a fact-discovery cutoff of December 19, 2026, expert reports due February 20, 2027, and a trial-ready date of September 2027. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) Getty's operative complaint asserts direct infringement of more than 12 million registered photographs and alleges willful infringement supporting the § 504(c)(2) $150,000 cap — a theoretical maximum damages exposure that, multiplied across 12 million works, exceeds $1.8 trillion, a figure plaintiffs have acknowledged is not what they actually seek but which has been cited in press coverage as evidence of the systemic scale of AI-training copyright exposure. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__)

The more legally significant damages development is Getty's expert designation: Getty has retained an economist to construct an "established licensing market" model under the fourth *Campbell* factor, arguing that Getty's own AI-training licensing program (launched in 2023, with reported deal values ranging from $1 million to $50 million per license depending on dataset scope) supplies a concrete comparable for what a willing-buyer/willing-seller negotiation would have produced. (__[ft.com — needs verification](https://www.ft.com)__) If the court accepts this model at trial, it collapses the defendant's argument that there was no established licensing market for AI training at the time of ingestion — that argument depends on the market being nascent and speculative, which Getty's own commercial licensing history directly rebuts.

### V. Legislative Vector — Senate AI Creator Compensation Act Discussion Draft and the Compulsory Licensing Threat

The Senate Judiciary IP Subcommittee's May 22, 2026 discussion draft of the "AI Accountability and Creator Compensation Act" (circulated by Subcommittee Chair Christopher Coons and Ranking Member Thom Tillis) would, if enacted, create a compulsory licensing scheme administered by a newly constituted AI Copyright Royalty Board — modeled on the Copyright Royalty Board that sets digital performance royalties under 17 U.S.C. §§ 801-805 — under which any AI developer could obtain a training license for copyrighted works upon payment of a judicially determined "reasonable royalty" and registration with the Board. (__[politico.com — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__) The draft includes a retroactive safe harbor: AI developers who voluntarily register pre-existing training datasets with the Board within 180 days of enactment and pay the retroactive royalty schedule would receive immunity from all pending civil claims arising from that prior training use.

This retroactive immunity provision is the legislative development most likely to materially alter the pending litigation posture. If enacted, it would extinguish *Bartz*, *Andersen*, *Getty*, and *N.Y. Times* in their present forms — replacing the copyright litigation overlay with a regulatory claims process. Authors' rights groups, including the Authors Guild and the National Press Photographers Association, issued a joint statement on May 23, 2026, opposing the retroactive safe harbor as an unconstitutional taking of pending vested legal claims without just compensation. (__[reuters.com — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) Defendants' lobbying apparatus is reportedly supporting the draft; OpenAI and Anthropic have not yet issued public statements as of press time. (__[wsj.com — needs verification](https://www.wsj.com)__)

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders, authors, photographers, and their counsel should treat the Copyright Office S-NOI comment deadline of July 14, 2026, as a first-order priority: the Office's ultimate report will be cited by every district court currently holding an AI-training case, and a well-structured comment linking specific litigation facts (e.g., the Getty established-licensing-market evidence, the Stability AI LAION ingestion volume data) to the Office's five targeted questions can materially shape judicial reception of the policy landscape. Concurrently, any rights-holder with works potentially encompassed by the Bartz putative class should ensure their copyright registrations are current and registration certificates are accessible — § 412 bars statutory damages for works not registered prior to infringement or within three months of first publication, and the class-certification battle will likely require plaintiffs to demonstrate sufficient numbers of pre-infringement registrations to satisfy Rule 23(a)(1) numerosity with a damages-bearing subclass. Rights-holders who registered late (i.e., after the alleged training cutoff dates) should consult class action counsel immediately about whether actual damages under § 504(b) — including profits attributable to the infringement — provide an alternative recovery path that does not depend on § 504(c) statutory damages eligibility, and should archive all evidence of licensing income, market-comparable deals, and downstream market impact now, before the expert-designation phase in *Getty* and *Bartz* crystallizes the evidentiary standards that will govern damages-model admissibility across the docket. Finally, monitor the Senate discussion draft closely: the 180-day registration window for retroactive safe-harbor protection, if the bill advances, will create an extraordinary time-limited opportunity for both defendants (to extinguish liability) and plaintiffs (to opt out or challenge constitutionality) that will require immediate strategic positioning.
