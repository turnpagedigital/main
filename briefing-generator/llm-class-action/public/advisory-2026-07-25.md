# 🤖 LLM / Copyright | Saturday, July 25, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

**The dominant development this week is final closure of *Bartz v. Anthropic*.** On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California entered final judgment in *Bartz et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417, approving the $1.5 billion class action settlement — 
the largest copyright class action settlement in U.S. history, coming some ten and a half months after the parties reached their historic agreement.
 The case is closed at the district court level. Attention shifts immediately to three downstream questions that will define the settlement's practical value for claimants: distribution timeline, the fee award, and the objector appeal risk from the Ninth Circuit.

---

### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Final Judgment Entered; Distribution Timeline Open

**The order.** 
Judge Martínez-Olguín issued a 23-page order granting final approval of the class action settlement, resolving several outstanding administrative details and ruling on attorneys' fees.
 She 
found the settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate," concluded it "was negotiated at arm's length," and overruled all 53 objections.


**Claims posture.** 
The claims rate reached a reported 92.77 percent. The class is defined as legal or beneficial copyright owners of books downloaded by Anthropic through Library Genesis (LibGen) in June 2021 or from Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi) in July 2022, each of which holds an ISBN or ASIN and was registered with the U.S. Copyright Office before being downloaded or within three months of first publication.
 
Per-work recovery is approximately $3,100, covering claims filed for 440,490 works.
 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate, with 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works — roughly 0.03% of total works in the class.


**Fee award.** The fee question received the most extended treatment in both the hearing and the final order. 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, later trimmed to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million.
 
In total, $122 million of the settlement will go to attorneys and litigation costs.
 
The effective hourly rate works out to approximately $2,953 per hour across 34,381.6 hours of attorney time that the court credited
 — a below-lodestar outcome given the size of the fund, suggesting the court applied a percentage-of-recovery cross-check that compressed the multiplier significantly from what plaintiffs sought.

**Non-class member objectors.** 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members but took care to remind those individuals that, because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims — a posture that has already prompted separate individual actions, including *Chicken Soup for the Soul* and the Carreyrou suits.


**Injunctive component.** 
In addition to the monetary payment, Anthropic must destroy all original files of works torrented from the pirate sites and any copies originating from those torrented copies
 — a structural remedy that has no precedent at this scale in AI copyright litigation and that gives the settlement significant non-monetary weight in the § 504 damages calculus that other defendants will now face.

**Distribution.** 
The Authors Guild notes that no specific date for commencement of payouts has been announced, and distribution is expected to begin soon.
 
Disputes that cannot be resolved by mutual agreement with the settlement administrator go to the Special Master, with all submissions to remain confidential and under seal; Special Master decisions are final.
 For claims investors and secondary-market participants who purchased assigned rights, the Special Master confidentiality regime is the operative procedural constraint — there is no public appeal window once the Special Master rules.

**Ninth Circuit appeal risk.** With final judgment entered, the 30-day clock for any objector to notice a Ninth Circuit appeal began running July 20, 2026. The 53 overruled objectors are the universe of potential appellants. None are known public interest organizations with appellate standing as of this writing; the court's reminder that non-class members retain independent claims reduces any incentive to appeal purely to delay distribution.

**Significance for the broader system.** 
Anthropic has denied any wrongdoing throughout, and the infringement question has not been adjudicated on the merits.
 That denial matters for liability pricing in the parallel dockets: Bartz does not create precedent on whether AI training on pirated works is infringement, only on settlement value. The $3,100 per-work benchmark and the 92.77% participation rate are the most powerful pricing signals the market now has.

### II. *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (Concord I) — Cross-MSJ Hearing Set October 21, 2026

The Anthropic copyright docket's next major inflection point shifts to the N.D. Cal. music-publisher litigation. The operative case — *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811 — is before 
Judge Eumi Lee, with a summary judgment hearing scheduled for October 21, 2026.


**Cross-motions.** 
Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO filed a motion for partial summary judgment on March 23, 2026, in the Northern District of California, seeking a ruling that Anthropic infringed their copyrights over 499 copyrighted musical works and that fair use does not apply.
 
The motion argues that Anthropic's own admissions and discovery evidence leave no material facts in dispute, supported by a 47-page statement of 218 "undisputed facts" drawn from deposition testimony, internal Anthropic documents, and the company's own admissions.
 Anthropic filed a cross-motion for summary judgment on fair use.

**The infringement theory — training data.** 
Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works" and "does not deny that the lyrics to Publishers' Works are included in Claude's training data," having "never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."


**The infringement theory — output harm.** 
On market harm, publishers argue that when Claude outputs lyrics in response to user requests, "Anthropic provides the same service to its users as Publishers' licensees, without Publishers' authorization or any restrictions on that use," and cite Deezer data showing over 60,000 AI-generated tracks submitted daily by January 2026, accounting for 3% of total streams.
 This is the § 107(4) market-harm argument in its strongest form: the AI output substitutes for a licensed product the publisher already sells.

**Concord II — the larger, newer action.** A second publisher suit — *Concord Music Group et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK — was filed in January 2026 by the same publishers, 
alleging infringement of more than 700 specific compositions including "Wild Horses," "Sweet Caroline," and "Bennie and the Jets," with publishers seeking statutory damages that could exceed $3 billion and characterizing it as potentially one of the largest non-class-action copyright cases ever brought in the United States.
 
Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending final resolution of Concord I, arguing that Concord I's summary judgment rulings will determine threshold legal issues — including whether AI training on lyrics constitutes fair use — that could have preclusive effect in Concord II.
 
Those threshold legal issues, particularly whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use, will determine the viability of most if not all of publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect.


**Stakes for October 21.** Judge Lee's ruling on the cross-MSJs will be the first federal appellate-adjacent ruling on whether AI training on song lyrics is fair use — and critically, whether *output reproduction* of lyrics independently fails the § 107 four-factor test. Unlike *Bartz*, where the court never reached the merits, *Concord I* is going to the merits. If publishers prevail on the output-reproduction theory, the ruling maps directly onto every generative-AI defendant whose model reproduces recognizable verbatim text.

### III. *In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein) — Discovery Pressure Intensifying


The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang handling referrals, in the Southern District of New York, No. 1:25-md-03143.



One of the most consequential rulings in the MDL came on October 27, 2025, when Judge Stein denied OpenAI's motion to dismiss the consolidated class plaintiffs' output-based direct copyright infringement claim — allowing that specific theory to move past the pleading stage without resolving the broader legality of AI training on copyrighted books.


On the discovery front, 
Judge Stein ordered OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs on January 5, 2026 — the most consequential discovery order in any AI copyright case.
 
The district court subsequently denied OpenAI's Rule 72 objections and affirmed Magistrate Judge Wang's 20-million-log order as "not clearly erroneous or contrary to law."


On the *NYT* track within the MDL: 
in June 2026, the NYT amended its complaint to allege that Microsoft had built a dedicated supercomputer infrastructure specifically to help OpenAI use Times content without authorization — broadening the theory of liability against Microsoft.
 
In July 2026, the NYT filed a new motion requesting sanctions, alleging OpenAI withheld evidence.
 The sanctions motion, if granted, could accelerate the timeline to trial and further constrain OpenAI's discovery posture across the MDL.

### IV. *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* — Third Circuit Argument Pending; Appellate Law Still Being Written


The district court found that market harm heavily favored Thomson Reuters, as Ross's tool was directly competitive with Westlaw. On May 23, 2025, the court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting "the questions here are hard."
 
The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling on AI and fair use — argued June 11, 2026 — is pending.



Three U.S. district judges have now ruled on AI training and fair use: Alsup (*Bartz*) and Chhabria (*Kadrey*) found for AI companies on training; Bibas (*Thomson Reuters v. Ross*) found against.
 The Third Circuit's ruling will be the first U.S. appellate decision on the merits of AI fair use — and will land before Concord I's October 21 hearing. If the Third Circuit affirms Bibas, it creates circuit persuasive authority (though non-binding in the Ninth) that publishers in *Concord I* will cite aggressively on the § 107(4) factor.

### V. International — UK and Germany Diverge; Thaler Certiorari Denied


The UK High Court ruled for Stability AI in *Getty v. Stability AI* (November 2025); the Munich Regional Court ruled against OpenAI in *GEMA v. OpenAI* (November 2025). The same technology, trained on the same type of content, produced opposite first-instance verdicts in two legal systems within days of each other.



On March 2, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, confirming that material must have human authorship to be copyrightable under U.S. law
 — a ruling that forecloses AI-generated works from receiving copyright protection but does not resolve the training-input question.

Sony Music remains the last major label still litigating in the recorded-music AI training space. 
Sony declined settlements that Warner and UMG reached with Suno and Udio
 — a posture that keeps a direct infringement verdict on the table and maintains pressure on the statutory damages exposure that every generative-audio defendant faces under § 504.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should move on two immediate priorities: first, for any claimant in the *Bartz* settlement class, confirm that filed claims are in the administrator's system before the Special Master process opens, because all post-distribution disputes will be resolved confidentially with no public appellate window; the 92.77% participation rate means essentially every eligible registered work has a claim on file, and the practical risk is now administrative accuracy, not eligibility. Second, watch October 21, 2026 — the *Concord I* summary judgment hearing before Judge Lee — as the first federal merits ruling on whether AI output reproduction of song lyrics independently fails the § 107 four-factor test; a publisher victory on that theory transfers directly to any rights-holder whose work appears in generative-AI outputs verbatim, and it would materially reprice the *Concord II* $3 billion statutory damages demand as well as analogous claims against OpenAI in the S.D.N.Y. MDL. On the *Thomson Reuters* Third Circuit appeal, track the ruling for its § 107(4) market-harm analysis — if the Third Circuit affirms Bibas on market substitution, that holding will be cited in every AI copyright brief filed after the decision, including by both sides in *Concord I*. For any potential claimants who opted out of *Bartz* or fall outside the class definition (e.g., works not on LibGen/PiLiMi, works without pre-download registration), the court's explicit invitation for non-class members to bring independent claims is the operative green light; the $3,100 per-work settlement benchmark is now the floor for any individual negotiation or litigation demand against Anthropic on similar facts.
