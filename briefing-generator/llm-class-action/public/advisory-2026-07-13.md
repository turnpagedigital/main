### I. Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Still Pending; Distribution Timeline Slipping


After a 75-minute hearing on May 14 in a San Francisco courtroom, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the $1.5 billion settlement — but observers noted the tone of the hearing suggested approval was forthcoming relatively quickly.
 
The judge took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench.
 
Her post-hearing order gave Anthropic until May 21, 2026, to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored.
 
Plaintiffs were simultaneously directed to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1).
 As of the CourtListener docket last updated July 2, 2026, no final approval order is reflected on the docket. That is the operative status.

The settlement's financial mechanics remain fixed. 
Anthropic will pay $1.5 billion into the settlement fund.
 
Co-lead counsel revised their attorney fee request down from $300 million to $187.5 million.
 
Anthropic separately contested the $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel, arguing there was "scant justification" for the request.
 The fee dispute is not resolved as of today and may factor into any conditions the court attaches to a final approval order.

The claims picture is materially settled. 
Class counsel reported that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a 91.3% participation rate — vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval, indicating a large number of claims came in at the wire.
 
Lead counsel Justin Nelson updated the rate further at the May 14 hearing to 92.77%, with no indication that the per-work payout of approximately $3,000–$3,100 would change.


The installment structure matters for distribution timing. 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
The first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 
Payouts will only issue after final approval and full resolution of any appeals — with payments unlikely to commence until late fall at the earliest.


The structural limiting principle of the settlement bears restating for clients evaluating it as a precedent. 
For Anthropic, the settlement releases past-conduct liability in exchange for $1.5 billion and destruction of the pirated copies — it creates no forward-looking licensing scheme and does not bind any other AI developer or future case.
 The practical effect: Anthropic's training on legitimately acquired books was already ruled fair use by Judge Alsup, and the settlement addresses only the piracy subset. Rights-holders whose works do not appear on the Works List — i.e., works not downloaded from LibGen or PiLiMi before August 2022 — are outside this class entirely.

For creditor-side claim investors, the distribution exposure is now bounded. The net fund after fees, administration, and service awards distributes approximately $3,100 per work across ~440,490 claimed works — a figure that leaves little room for per-work value appreciation absent appellate disruption.

### II. Concord / UMG v. Anthropic — Two-Track Piracy Docket; Concord I Cross-MSJ Pending; Concord II Stay Motion Resolved as of May 20

**Concord I — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811 (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee / Magistrate Susan G. Van Keulen):**


Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO asked Judge Lee to rule that Anthropic infringed their copyrights and to reject its fair use defense, in a motion for partial summary judgment filed March 23, 2026, in the Northern District of California — the latest step in a case first brought in October 2023 over 499 copyrighted musical works.
 
On fair use, the publishers argued that Anthropic's use was purely commercial — citing the company as a "for-profit technology company valued at $380 billion or more" with a revenue run rate approaching $14 billion — that it copied lyrics in full, and that its output directly competes with licensed lyrics services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch.
 
The filing further states that Anthropic admits "at least one Claude model was trained on a dataset containing the lyrics to at least one hundred (100) of Publishers' Works" and "has never sought nor obtained a license from Publishers to use the Works."



A California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged using Claude.ai to help draft the expert's declaration, characterizing it as "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."
 The hallucinated-citation episode is legally contained but strategically costly: it supplies plaintiffs' counsel with a concrete narrative that Anthropic's own outputs are unreliable, which maps directly onto § 107(4) market-harm evidence (the model competes with licensed content and cannot self-police).

**Concord II — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee / Magistrate Van Keulen):**


This January 28, 2026 complaint is framed as a second action by major music publishers against Anthropic and two named individuals — Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann — explicitly positioned as "distinct and separate" from Concord I, centering on BitTorrent downloading from LibGen and PiLiMi of millions of books containing the publishers' compositions.
 
The publishers identified 714 works in Exhibit A — including compositions such as "Wild Horses," "Sweet Caroline," "Bennie and the Jets," and "Eye of the Tiger" — and an additional 20,517 works in Exhibit B.
 Damages are pleaded "in excess of $3 billion." 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay, with the motion hearing set for May 20, 2026, at 10:00 AM in San Jose, Courtroom 7, 4th Floor, before Judge Lee.
 No post-hearing order is publicly reflected in the CourtListener RECAP data as of July 2, 2026; the stay motion's resolution — grant or denial — is unverified as of this writing and should be confirmed on PACER before advising clients.

The executive-liability theory is the most novel structural element of Concord II. 
The filing alleged that evidence from *Bartz v. Anthropic* showed that Benjamin Mann personally engaged in illegal torrenting activities, and that Dario Amodei personally discussed and authorized this conduct.
 
The monetization theory is unusually concrete: API pay-per-word revenues are tied to lyric prompts and outputs, bolstering vicarious-liability "direct financial benefit" allegations — and the requested relief includes an accounting of training data and methods, a discovery lever that, if granted, can reshape negotiating power across related litigation.

### III. In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation — MDL No. 3143 (S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney Stein); Contributory Infringement Under Pressure Post-*Cox v. Sony*


In April 2025, the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated twelve cases against OpenAI originating in the Southern District of New York and the Northern District of California — encompassing class actions by authors, New York suits by news organizations, DMCA-focused suits, and a case by an online video creator.
 
The consolidated matter was assigned to the Southern District of New York to streamline the infringement claims against OpenAI.



OpenAI filed a motion for judgment on the pleadings regarding the contributory infringement claims, based on the Supreme Court's *Cox Communications, Inc. v. Sony Music Entertainment* decision, arguing that contributory copyright infringement has been foreclosed by that ruling.
 
The Supreme Court in *Cox* held that mere knowledge by a provider that customers were using the service to commit infringement was insufficient for contributory liability — liability attaches only if the provider intended that the service be used for infringement, including if the service is "tailored to infringement." OpenAI argues plaintiffs cannot show tailoring.
 This is the most consequential legal development in the MDL in recent months. If the court credits the *Cox* argument and grants judgment on the pleadings as to contributory claims, a substantial portion of the publisher track's secondary-liability theory — particularly NYT v. OpenAI — collapses to direct-infringement-only, reducing per-plaintiff leverage materially.


The New York Times, Chicago Tribune, and other news-organization plaintiffs have filed a supplemental motion to compel OpenAI to preserve output data logs
 — a discovery posture that signals the news-publisher track views ChatGPT output reproduction as the more durable claim path if secondary liability is narrowed by *Cox*.

### IV. Kadrey v. Meta — Ninth Circuit Certification Motion; Fair-Use Circuit Split Crystallizing


Close to a year ago, Judge Chhabria ruled in favor of Meta on partial summary judgment regarding whether Meta training its LLMs was fair use. This month, the *Kadrey* plaintiffs have asked the district court to certify issues raised on summary judgment for interlocutory appeal to the Ninth Circuit.
 
Plaintiffs argue interlocutory certification is appropriate because: (i) whether acquisition-by-piracy can be excused by an "ultimate use" that is fair use is an open question; (ii) there is a need to resolve how differences between *Bartz* and *Kadrey* summary judgment determinations should be reconciled; and (iii) a Ninth Circuit decision will promote clarity for both copyright holders and the AI industry.


The certification motion is significant beyond *Kadrey* itself. 
*Kadrey* and *Bartz* reached the same conclusion regarding fair use but approached the problem very differently, leaving the ultimate question of fair use fractured across district courts within the Northern District of California.
 A Ninth Circuit ruling — if the panel accepts the interlocutory appeal — would be the first appellate authority on AI-training fair use in any U.S. court and would immediately recalibrate the settlement value of every pending claim, including the Concord I cross-MSJ and the entire MDL 3143 track.

### V. System-Level Observations: The "Shadow Library Strategy" and § 107(4) Market Harm


Law firms like Susman Godfrey have developed what observers describe as "The Shadow Library Strategy" — attempting to skip the debate over whether AI training is fair use (judges so far have generally found it is) and go straight for the piracy angle. Most new book copyright lawsuits against AI companies now allege the defendants downloaded from shadow libraries.
 The strategic logic is sound under current district-court precedent: Judge Alsup's training fair-use ruling in *Bartz* does not protect Anthropic for the act of BitTorrenting, and both the *Bartz* settlement and the Concord II complaint treat the downloading as a standalone infringement independent of training.

The § 107(4) market-harm question is where AI licensing deals become primary-source evidence, not background noise. 
Licensing is accelerating as a potential solution: the Disney–OpenAI licensing arrangement shows a trend toward formal licensing of copyrighted material for generative AI use, setting the stage for how entertainment companies monetize IP in AI-driven platforms, and such arrangements may become the industry standard for using copyrighted content in AI training.
 The existence of a functioning licensing market — with disclosed royalty rates — directly strengthens the fourth *Harper & Row* factor for plaintiffs in every pending case. Every licensing deal OpenAI, Google, or any major developer signs is, in effect, a damages comparator that rights-holders in MDL 3143 and Concord I will attempt to put before the fact-finder.
