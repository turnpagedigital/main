### I. Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Order, Settlement Administration, and Distribution Timeline


On July 20, 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and entered final judgment in the case.
 
The court issued a 23-page order granting final approval, resolving several outstanding administrative details and ruling on attorneys' fees.


The order answers three questions that had been open since the May fairness hearing: the fee award, the handling of objectors, and the treatment of non-class-member claimants.

**Fee award.** 
Class counsel had initially sought $300 million, later trimmed to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million.
 
Total attorneys' fees and litigation costs come to $122 million of the settlement fund.
 That figure — roughly 8.1% of the $1.5 billion corpus — is materially below the 25% benchmark plaintiffs' counsel argued for and reflects the court's application of a lodestar cross-check under Ninth Circuit precedent. Rights-holders who have been tracking fee exposure should note that the ~$286 million reduction from the original request directly enlarges the per-work recovery pool.

**Claims posture and per-work yield.** 
Class counsel reported a 91.3% claims rate, with 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works, or 0.03% of the total number of Works.
 
The reported claims rate of 92.77% makes this the largest class action copyright settlement in history by that measure as well.
 
Bartz v. Anthropic is paying approximately $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works, with claims filed for 440,490 works downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror by Anthropic.


**Objectors.** 
Judge Martínez-Olguín overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds, finding it "fair, reasonable, and adequate," "negotiated at arm's length," and treating class members "equitably relative to each other."
 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but expressly reminded those individuals that, because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims.


**Scope of release — what remains actionable.** This is the most consequential structural point for claims investors and output-litigation counsel. 
Under the settlement, class members release only claims relating to Anthropic's past acquisition and copying of their works — the "inputs" side — through August 25, 2025; claims based on AI outputs are not released, and neither are any claims of any kind about future conduct.
 
The Chicken Soup for the Soul and Carreyrou suits are already proceeding as separate, individual actions, and the court's order may prompt additional individual plaintiffs to follow.


**Distribution timeline.** 
The official settlement FAQ estimates initial payments by August 10, 2026, though payments cannot begin until the judgment becomes effective and any appeals are resolved.
 The installment schedule — embedded in the settlement agreement — is: 
$450 million on October 2, 2025 (already paid); $300 million on April 30, 2026 (already paid); $450 million on September 25, 2026; and the final $300 million on September 25, 2027.
 The practical implication: first-wave checks will issue only after the 30-day objector-appeal window runs from July 20, 2026 — i.e., no earlier than August 19, 2026, and subject to any Ninth Circuit stay motion. 
Any disputes that cannot be resolved by mutual agreement with the settlement administrator will be submitted to the Special Master, and all such submissions will remain confidential and under seal.

### II. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Cross-MSJ Track, Concord I and II

The music-publisher litigation is structurally more complex than Bartz: it bifurcates into two separately docketed actions, both before Judge Eumi Lee in the Northern District of California.

**Concord I (5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK)** is the lead vehicle. 
The case bears docket number 5:24-cv-03811, is assigned to Judge Eumi Lee, and carries a summary judgment hearing scheduled for October 21, 2026.
 
Plaintiffs filed their Motion for Partial Summary Judgment on March 23, 2026, with a proposed amicus brief in support filed March 30, 2026.
 The cross-questions presented are whether partial summary judgment on copyright infringement should be granted to plaintiffs, or whether summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement should be granted to Anthropic — a clean binary that will produce the first judicial ruling on whether generative AI training on licensed (rather than pirated) song lyrics constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

**Concord II (5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK)** is a follow-on action filed in early 2026. 
Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending final resolution of Concord I — or in the alternative, pending resolution of the MSJ and any subsequent appeal in Concord I — with a hearing noticed for May 20, 2026 in Courtroom 7, San Jose Division.
 
Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice; the threshold fair-use questions, including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use, will determine the viability of most if not all of the publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect.


The October 21 MSJ hearing in Concord I is now the most important scheduled event in the AI copyright docket for the back half of 2026. A finding of infringement would create direct precedent — from the same district that produced Bartz — that licensed but unlicensed training use is actionable, sharply narrowing the "legitimately acquired" carve-out that Judge Alsup's 2024 ruling left open.

### III. Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir.) — First Federal Appellate Argument on AI Training Fair Use


On June 11, 2026, the Third Circuit heard oral argument in *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence Inc.*, No. 25-2153, the first federal appellate case to squarely address whether using copyrighted works to train an AI model qualifies as fair use; a ruling is expected in late 2026.


The district court record (D. Del., Judge Stephanos Bibas) is clear: 
the court granted summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding the headnotes were original and protected and that Ross Intelligence's use of the headnotes to train its AI legal research tool was not fair use.
 
The court granted Ross's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard"; in its appeal brief filed September 2025, Ross argued that if the decision stands, it will have "sweeping consequences for innovation" in both legal research and artificial intelligence.



The appeal turns on two core questions: whether Westlaw's headnotes are copyrightable at all — they summarize judicial holdings, which are public documents — and whether internal use of those headnotes as training data might qualify as transformative use despite the district court's finding.
 The § 107(4) market-harm dimension is particularly sharp here: Judge Bibas found that harm to a *potential* market for AI training data is sufficient injury even where the rights-holder has not yet entered that market.


A ruling that reverses the district court would open training pipelines considerably, potentially allowing AI developers to use proprietary databases without licensing agreements provided training is classified as transformative; a ruling that affirms could force widespread changes to how AI companies source training data and accelerate the ongoing wave of licensing negotiations between publishers and labs.
 The Third Circuit's opinion — expected by year-end — is the single most watched ruling in this space for the remainder of 2026. Its transformativeness analysis will travel directly into the Concord I briefing and the OpenAI MDL, and its market-harm framing will be cited as § 107(4) comparator evidence in any subsequent licensing-damages proceeding.

### IV. In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation, No. 1:25-md-03143-SHS-OTW (S.D.N.Y.) — MDL Discovery Posture


The district court has denied OpenAI's motion to dismiss plaintiffs' claim of direct copyright infringement based on AI outputs, holding that plaintiffs adequately pled that OpenAI actually copied plaintiffs' text and that a reasonable jury could find that allegedly infringing outputs are substantially similar to plaintiffs' works.
 
Plaintiffs are authors and copyright holders of fiction and nonfiction books who brought copyright infringement claims against OpenAI Inc. and Microsoft Corp.; the action resulted from the consolidation of multiple putative class actions into this MDL.



A prior ruling found that OpenAI waived attorney-client privilege in the MDL over copyright infringement claims, granting plaintiffs' motion to compel OpenAI to produce privileged documents.
 That privilege waiver, if it encompasses internal training-data sourcing decisions, is potentially the most damaging discovery event in the MDL to date — the kind of production that tends to drive settlements by exposing decisional-level knowledge of infringement risk. 
A separate order granted OpenAI's motion to stay discovery as to models not already in the MDL, specifically the o1, o1 mini, o1-pro, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, and GPT-5 models.
 The stay limits near-term discovery burden on next-generation products but leaves the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 era training pipeline fully exposed.

### V. Broader System Watch — Precedent Transfer and § 107 Framework

The Bartz order and the Thomson Reuters Third Circuit argument together define the current § 107 framework as follows: (a) piracy-sourced training data is categorically indefensible — the Bartz pirate-library provenance finding, now reduced to a $1.5 billion consent judgment, is as close to hard precedent as a settlement gets; (b) the status of *licensed but unlicensed* training data — the Concord I question — remains open; and (c) the appellate resolution of *transformativeness* as applied to training (not output) will arrive from the Third Circuit first, likely before the Ninth Circuit gets there.


The AI copyright landscape in 2026 is resolving around a market-competition standard: when AI outputs compete with the market for training data, courts are finding infringement; when they don't, fair use arguments survive.
 For claimants and claims investors, the § 107(4) market-harm factor is now the litigation's gravitational center: every licensing deal struck between an AI company and a major publisher (whether with the AP, the Associated Press, or any news organization) is admissible as comparator evidence of the market that existed — and that the defendant chose not to enter.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and claims investors with positions in the Bartz class should monitor the August 19 appeal window closely — any Ninth Circuit stay motion filed within 30 days of the July 20 order would delay first-wave disbursements and reset the September 25 Anthropic installment calculus; absent an objector appeal, distribution should commence against the $300 million April 30 tranche and the $450 million September 25 installment on a schedule the settlement administrator has estimated at August 10, 2026 for initial checks. Output-claims counsel who preserved opt-out rights, or who represent the 350 opt-out class members, should be building § 107(4) licensing-market evidence now, before the Third Circuit ruling hardens (or softens) the transformativeness framework. Concord I's October 21 MSJ hearing date is a hard briefing deadline: amicus participation windows are typically 21 days before the hearing, putting the submission deadline at approximately September 30, 2026 — any industry coalition wishing to weigh in on the licensed-lyrics fair-use question must begin drafting now. For litigation financiers with exposure to the OpenAI MDL, the privilege-waiver production order is the development most likely to produce a settlement-forcing revelation in the near term; track the joint dispute chart submissions before Judge Swain at each discovery conference.
