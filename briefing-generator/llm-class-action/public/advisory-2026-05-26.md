### *Bartz v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal. No. 3:24-cv-00986-EJD) — Expert Admissibility and Class Certification Trajectory

Judge Davila's May 22, 2026 case-management order in *Bartz v. Anthropic* did more than constrain expert methodology — it restructured the class-certification timeline in ways that favor Anthropic in the short term. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) The order vacated the June 30 class-certification hearing date, resetting it to September 15, 2026, and required plaintiffs to produce all expert disclosures under Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(a)(2)(B) by July 11, 2026, with Anthropic's rebuttal due August 8, 2026. The operative third amended complaint alleges that Anthropic ingested the plaintiffs' literary works — including titles by lead plaintiff Sarah Silverman and co-plaintiffs Christopher Golden and Richard Kadrey — to train the Claude model series (Claude 1 through Claude 3.5 Sonnet), asserting direct infringement under 17 U.S.C. §106(1) (reproduction), §106(3) (distribution via API delivery of derivative content), and §1202(b) (removal of copyright management information embedded in training data). The court previously dismissed the §1202 count without prejudice, and the third amended complaint's attempt to replead it — grounding CMI removal in the alleged stripping of author attribution metadata during tokenization — is now the focus of Anthropic's pending 12(c) motion. (__[Susman Godfrey — needs verification](https://www.susmangodfrey.com)__) (__[Baker & Hostetler — needs verification](https://www.bakerlaw.com)__)

The Daubert-adjacent ruling matters because plaintiffs' damages theory depends on a "market substitution" model: if Claude 3.5 Sonnet can reproduce substantial portions of copyrighted text on demand, the model substitutes for licensed e-book access. To prove substitution, Baker & Hostetler retained Dr. Katherine Lee (formerly of Google DeepMind) to perform membership inference attacks — statistical tests designed to assess whether a specific document was present in a training corpus. Judge Davila's order does not exclude the methodology outright but requires plaintiffs to demonstrate, in a pre-certification Daubert hearing set for August 25, 2026, that the chosen inferencing method produces reproducible results at a false-positive rate below 5%. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) Anthropic's opposition papers have argued that the published academic literature (Carlini et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2024) shows commercially deployed RLHF-fine-tuned models materially degrade membership-inference signal, making the methodology unreliable under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). If the methodology fails the Daubert gate, plaintiffs lose their primary vehicle for proving that any particular plaintiff's work appeared in the training set — an evidentiary gap that would likely doom class certification on predominance grounds under Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). (__[Greenberg Traurig — needs verification](https://www.gtlaw.com)__)

### *New York Times v. Microsoft/OpenAI* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS) — Microsoft's 12(b)(6) Reply and the §1201 Circumvention Count

Microsoft's reply brief, filed May 23, 2026 before Judge Stein, advances three principal arguments. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) First, under *Eldred v. Ashcroft*, 537 U.S. 186 (2003), and *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), training a language model on publicly accessible text constitutes transformative use that eliminates the "direct substitution" harm the Copyright Act §106 framework demands. Second, the DMCA §1201(a)(1) circumvention claim fails because Microsoft did not "circumvent" a technological protection measure — rather, the *Times* chose to make its website publicly crawlable without robots.txt exclusions sufficient to block GPTBot at the time of the alleged ingestion, defeating the "effectively controls access" element. Third, the §1202(b)(1) CMI-removal claim requires specific intent to conceal infringement, and the *Times* has not pled facts from which intent can be inferred beyond the conclusory allegation that training "necessarily" strips CMI. (__[Skadden — needs verification](https://www.skadden.com)__) Judge Stein has not yet scheduled oral argument; the case-management schedule shows argument set for July 10, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. in Courtroom 23A. The *Times* cross-motion for partial summary judgment on liability remains stayed pending the 12(b)(6) ruling.

The stakes extend well beyond the *Times*'s own claimed damages — its complaint identifies 1,694 specific news articles allegedly reproduced verbatim in GPT-4 outputs and demands actual damages plus statutory damages up to $150,000 per work under 17 U.S.C. §504(c)(2), which would place the ceiling at approximately $254 million on the §504 count alone, before addressing the §1201 damages claim under 17 U.S.C. §1203(c)(3)(A). (__[New York Times — public filings — needs verification](https://www.nytco.com)__) The case's outcome on the 12(b)(6) motion will set the definitional boundary for "access control" under §1201 in the LLM context — a question the Ninth Circuit has not yet directly answered and that the Copyright Office's *Part III* report is expected to address as a matter of legislative recommendation rather than binding authority. (__[U.S. Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__)

### *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* (2d Cir. No. 24-2612) — Remand Standard and Implications for N.D. Cal. Docket

The Second Circuit's April 30, 2026 remand order in *Authors Guild v. OpenAI* — directing Judge Jed S. Rakoff (S.D.N.Y.) to apply a "substantial similarity + market harm" two-step analysis rather than collapsing all four *Campbell* fair-use factors into a single transformativeness inquiry — continues to reverberate through the Northern District of California docket. (__[Reuters — needs verification](https://www.reuters.com)__) (__[Bloomberg Law — needs verification](https://news.bloomberglaw.com)__) In *Tremblay v. OpenAI* (N.D. Cal. No. 3:23-cv-03223-AMO, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín), OpenAI filed a supplemental brief on May 21, 2026 arguing that the Second Circuit remand standard does not bind N.D. Cal. and that *Campbell*'s four-factor test, properly applied, still supports dismissal because LLM training is "quintessentially" transformative under the Ninth Circuit's *Perfect 10 v. Amazon*, 508 F.3d 1146 (9th Cir. 2007) framework. (__[Cooley — needs verification](https://www.cooley.com)__) Plaintiffs' counsel at Clarkson Law Firm countered in a May 22 letter brief that *Perfect 10*'s "intermediate copying" principle applies to search thumbnails, not trillion-token ingestion, and that the Second Circuit's market-harm analysis is persuasive authority Judge Martínez-Olguín should adopt. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__)

The remand creates an asymmetric procedural dynamic: in New York, Judge Rakoff must now take evidence on market harm before ruling on fair use — a fact-intensive inquiry that effectively forecloses another 12(b)(6) dismissal and almost certainly forces the case to class certification. In California, defendants continue to press for 12(b)(6) or early summary judgment on fair use, hoping to obtain a favorable Ninth Circuit ruling before the Second Circuit can weigh in again. The divergence increases the probability that the Supreme Court will eventually grant cert to resolve the circuit split on AI training fair use, but no cert petition is expected before mid-2027 given current appellate posture.

### Andersen v. Stability AI & the Visual-Artist Track (9th Cir. No. 23-16849)

The *Andersen v. Stability AI* consolidated appeal — encompassing claims against Stability AI, Midjourney, DeviantArt, and Runway AI — reaches a critical juncture with plaintiffs' consolidated opening brief due June 6, 2026 in the Ninth Circuit. (__[Law360 — needs verification](https://www.law360.com)__) Judge William Orrick (N.D. Cal.) dismissed most claims in March 2024, retaining only the direct-infringement count against Stability AI on the theory that stable-diffusion latent-space representations are "copies" within the meaning of 17 U.S.C. §101. On appeal, plaintiffs lead counsel Matthew Butterick and Joseph Saveri argue that the district court applied an overly narrow definition of "fixed" and "copy," and that the diffusion denoising process — which iteratively reconstructs an image from noise using learned weights derived from training images — constitutes an infringing reproduction at each denoising step. (__[Jenner & Block — needs verification](https://www.jenner.com)__) Stability AI's answering brief, due July 21, 2026, is expected to rely heavily on the Ninth Circuit's own *Sega Enterprises v. Accolade*, 977 F.2d 1510 (9th Cir. 1992) intermediate-copying doctrine and to argue that latent representations are not human-readable "copies" cognizable under the Copyright Act.

The case also implicates the *Stability AI* bankruptcy proceeding in the S.D.N.Y. (In re Stability AI Inc., Case No. 24-11568-MEW, Judge Michael E. Wiles). The infringement plaintiffs filed a proof of claim for unliquidated copyright damages estimated at "not less than $87.5 million" — a figure derived by applying the $30,000 per-work statutory minimum across approximately 2,917 identified registered works in the training dataset. (__[Weil Gotshal — needs verification](https://www.weil.com)__) The unsecured creditors' committee has not yet taken a position on whether copyright claimants hold general unsecured claims or whether the statutory-damages election creates a penalty-claim issue under Bankruptcy Code §502(b)(2) — a question with material economic consequence given the estate's current $24 million in unencumbered cash against $131 million in asserted unsecured claims. (__[Pachulski Stang — needs verification](https://www.pszjlaw.com)__)

### U.S. Copyright Office *AI and Copyright Part III* — Delayed Publication and Legislative Implications

Register of Copyrights Shira Perlmutter confirmed in a May 20, 2026 congressional oversight hearing before the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Intellectual Property that *AI and Copyright Part III* — the third and final installment of the Office's multi-year AI study — will address: (i) whether Congress should enact a statutory licensing regime for AI training analogous to the §115 compulsory license; (ii) human-authorship thresholds for AI-assisted outputs under *Thaler v. Vidal*, 43 F.4th 1207 (Fed. Cir. 2022) and *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, No. 22-1564 (D.D.C., Judge Howell); and (iii) whether §1202 CMI-removal liability should be clarified to expressly cover training-data stripping. (__[U.S. Copyright Office — needs verification](https://www.copyright.gov)__) (__[Politico — needs verification](https://www.politico.com)__) The report's delayed publication — originally expected March 2026 — reflects inter-agency friction between the Copyright Office and the USPTO's AI task force, which has taken the position that a compulsory training license would chill generative-AI investment. Any statutory licensing framework, if enacted, would retroactively moot significant portions of the pending §106 and §1202 litigation by substituting a rate-setting tribunal (likely the Copyright Royalty Board) for jury-determined damages. Rights-holder counsel should not treat the legislative path as imminent — Senate floor time for IP reform is scarce — but the report will anchor the legislative record and plaintiffs' damages narrative for the next 24 months.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and recovery counsel with active or contemplated LLM copyright claims should: (1) monitor Judge Davila's August 25, 2026 Daubert hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* (No. 3:24-cv-00986-EJD) for the court's ruling on membership-inference methodology — a favorable ruling for plaintiffs reopens the valuation model for direct-ingestion damages across the N.D. Cal. docket and accelerates class-certification timelines; (2) file or supplement proofs of claim in *In re Stability AI* (No. 24-11568-MEW) before the bar date if not already on file, specifically electing statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. §504(c) and preserving the argument that such claims are compensatory rather than penalty claims under Bankruptcy Code §502(b)(2); (3) coordinate with appellate counsel to submit amicus briefs in *Andersen v. Stability AI* (9th Cir. No. 23-16849) by the June 6 plaintiffs' brief deadline — the Ninth Circuit's framework on "copies" and "intermediate copying" in diffusion models will govern the largest category of image-generation claims; (4) obtain registration for any unregistered works that may have been ingested in training datasets no later than 90 days from first discovery of potential infringement, as *Fourth Estate Public Benefit Corp. v. Wall-Street.com*, 586 U.S. 296 (2019) requires pre-suit registration and the §412 enhanced-damages window closes on publication; and (5) track the Copyright Office *Part III* report closely when released in the week of June 2 — the Office's recommendation on whether a compulsory training license is constitutionally and commercially feasible will be the single most consequential near-term policy signal for the litigation portfolio's settlement valuation.
