## Analysis & Developments

**Bartz final approval order: still outstanding, distribution clock ticking.** As of this writing, the most consequential near-term development in the AI copyright docket is a gap, not a filing: Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín has not yet entered the final approval order in *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.). 
The May 14, 2026 fairness hearing concluded with the judge taking the matter under submission; as of May 18, 2026, the final approval order had not yet been entered.
 That gap has meaningful downstream consequences. 
Per the settlement administration timeline, the Settlement Administrator was scheduled to calculate per-work and per-claimant distributions by June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement expected to begin in June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed.
 If the order has not entered by today, the June 11 distribution-calculation milestone is slipping, and initial payments — already contingent on any objector's appeal resolving — will be pushed further into the summer.

The hearing itself produced one important post-hearing procedural directive. 
Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered that, by May 21, 2026, Anthropic could file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and that by the same date, plaintiffs were to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1).
 The late-opt-out question is not trivial: individuals who missed the January 29, 2026 deadline but submitted untimely exclusion requests are arguing they should be permitted to pursue independent claims; 
class counsel urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.

### Settlement Economics: Participation Rates, Fee Compression, and the Per-Work Benchmark

The claims participation picture is historically anomalous and has direct pricing implications for every parallel proceeding. 
Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3% rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed March 19, 2026.
 By comparison, 
the typical class action claim rate is around 10 percent.
 The practical consequence flows directly from the per-work allocation structure: 
the high rate of claim means that forecasts of a larger payout based on the March 19 report of 54% will not materialize, and the actual payout will likely end up closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.
 Class counsel Justin Nelson's updated calculation put the number at $3,100 per work. (__[Chat GPT Is Eating the World, May 15, 2026](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/05/15/final-approval-of-class-settlement-hearing-in-bartz-v-anthropic-recap/)__)

The fee trajectory is worth tracking separately. 
After Judge Alsup's December 2025 memorandum questioning the original fee request, class counsel reduced its petition from $300 million (20% of the fund) to $187.5 million (12.5%).
 
The settlement fund must also cover JND Legal Administration's administration costs, $50,000 service awards per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives), and Special Master fees for resolving disputes between class members.
 The net recovery per work after fees and administration is therefore below the headline $3,100 figure. Rights-holder counsel pricing future recovery claims should model net distributions in the $2,600–$2,800 range pending final fee order.

The structural precedent this sets is now being absorbed across the litigation system. 
The $1.5 billion settlement establishes the first concrete per-work pricing benchmark ($3,113/book) for AI training on pirated content; Norton Rose Fulbright's March 2026 analysis notes that statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504 can reach $150,000 per work, meaning the settlement resolved at approximately 2.1% of theoretical maximum exposure — giving plaintiff counsel in other AI copyright cases a credible floor for settlement negotiations.


The payment structure is also important for recovery planning. 
Anthropic will fund the settlement fund in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved, and payments will likely commence in the late fall at the earliest; Anthropic is paying into the $1.5 billion settlement fund over time, so not all funds will be dispersed at once.
 The installment structure means even a clean final approval order today does not produce immediate liquidity for claimants.

### What Bartz Does and Does Not Settle

A recurring analytical error in coverage of this settlement warrants restating. 
The scope of this settlement has been widely misunderstood in press coverage: Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training.
 The fair use question on training was decided at summary judgment — in Anthropic's favor as to legally-acquired corpora. 
In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled on summary judgment that using books without permission to train AI was fair use if they were acquired legally, but denied Anthropic's request for summary judgment related to piracy — finding that the piracy was not fair use.
 
Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy, not the act of AI training, and as such the fair use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers.
 Bartz's $1.5 billion settles the piracy-based liability; the broader AI training fair use question remains open and will be litigated across the parallel docket. 
For many authors implicated in Bartz, the litigation around AI training and book piracy is not ending; it is shifting to other cases — including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft, and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others.

### Concord / UMG v. Anthropic: The $3.1B Music-Publisher Follow-On

The most immediate structural successor to the Bartz piracy theory is the music-publisher action now pending before Judge Eumi K. Lee (Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen referred) in the Northern District of California. 
The case — *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal.) — was filed January 28, 2026 and is assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee.
 This is a second-generation complaint: plaintiffs Concord Music Group and Capitol CMG refiled after the earlier Tennessee/California litigation produced unfavorable early rulings on secondary infringement.


Plaintiffs in the prior action — a group of music publishers led by Concord Music Group and Universal Music Corp. — brought claims for direct and secondary copyright infringement and removal of copyright management information against Anthropic, alleging infringement of exclusive rights in musical lyrics through Claude's training and outputs.
 Those secondary infringement claims were dismissed. 
The court found in favor of Anthropic in two consecutive decisions: first, denying the motion for a preliminary injunction as overbroad and failing to establish irreparable harm; then granting Anthropic's motion to dismiss the contributory and vicarious infringement claims and the CMI removal claims.


The January 2026 refiling pivots. The new complaint more precisely targets direct infringement, incorporating the Bartz fair-use framework as a structural guide for what Anthropic can and cannot shelter under § 107. 
Anthropic filed a Motion to Stay with a hearing set for May 20, 2026, at 10:00 AM in San Jose, Courtroom 7, 4th Floor, before Judge Lee.
 The outcome of that stay motion — sought presumably to await Bartz final approval and any Third Circuit guidance — is not yet confirmed in the available docket materials as of this writing. 
Music publishers' $3.1 billion lawsuit against Anthropic using the same piracy theory suggests plaintiff attorneys view Bartz as a replicable playbook.

### Thomson Reuters v. Ross: Third Circuit Oral Argument — The First Appellate Fair-Use Test

The most consequential near-term event for the AI copyright docket system is not a trial court filing — it is the Third Circuit. 
The first appellate ruling on fair use in an AI training case is scheduled for oral argument on June 11, 2026, in the Third Circuit. The case, *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence*, involves non-generative AI — Ross's system used Westlaw headnotes to train a legal search tool — but the fair use analysis it will produce is expected to influence every generative AI training case in the pipeline.


The procedural posture is layered. 
On February 11, 2025, the district court granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and that Ross Intelligence's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use.
 
On May 23, 2025, the court granted Ross Intelligence's motion for interlocutory appeal and stay pending appeal, noting that "the questions here are hard."
 The certified questions — (1) whether Westlaw headnotes are original, and (2) whether Ross's use was fair use — are now before the Third Circuit. 
The Third Circuit's June 11, 2026 argument will be the first appellate consideration of whether training an AI on copyrighted works is fair use — making it the most consequential AI copyright hearing of 2026.
 A ruling adverse to Ross would force defendants in generative AI cases to rely exclusively on the "transformative" character of generative outputs to distinguish the result — a factual showing that the Thomson Reuters district court found insufficient at summary judgment under factor four (market harm).

### In Re OpenAI MDL (No. 3143, S.D.N.Y.) and NYT v. OpenAI: Discovery Driving Settlement Leverage


The consolidated MDL in the Southern District of New York brings together 16 copyright lawsuits against OpenAI, with lead cases including the New York Times (filed December 27, 2023), the New York Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting.
 The most significant 2026 development in the MDL is a discovery order with settlement-leverage implications. 
On January 5, 2026, Judge Sidney H. Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang's order compelling OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs to plaintiffs; OpenAI had attempted to narrow the production to only logs mentioning plaintiffs' specific works, and the court rejected this approach. Legal analysts suggest these logs, once analyzed, will effectively dictate settlement terms for the remainder of the consolidated litigation.
 The preservation order underlying this production — issued May 13, 2025 — was itself notable: 
Magistrate Judge Wang's order required OpenAI to retain all ChatGPT conversation logs affecting over 400 million users worldwide, the first time a court has mandated mass preservation of AI-generated content on such a scale.


The 20 million conversation logs represent a § 107(4) market-harm data set of unusual breadth. If plaintiffs' experts can quantify the share of log traffic that displaces subscription or licensing revenue — newspaper articles reproduced verbatim, paywalled content surfaced without attribution — factor four analysis shifts dramatically against OpenAI. The NYT case, No. 1:23-CV-11195 (S.D.N.Y.), is the bellwether; a settlement in that matter would almost certainly anchor per-article licensing valuations for the remainder of the MDL.

### Thaler v. Perlmutter: Human Authorship Requirement Confirmed at Supreme Court Level

One open question in this docket system has now been definitively resolved. 
On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari of Dr. Stephen Thaler's appeal of the lower court's decision granting summary judgment in favor of the U.S. Copyright Office, confirming the principle that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable.
 
This ruling does not necessarily prohibit copyright protection for works created with AI assistance — human contributions with AI assistive use may be protectable — but because Thaler explicitly and repeatedly disclaimed any human creative input, the Court did not address how much human involvement is necessary to support copyright registration for AI-assisted works.
 That question remains open and is increasingly material as rights-holders seek to register AI-assisted works and as defendants challenge registration validity under 17 U.S.C. § 411.

Finally, watch the Third Circuit after June 11 oral argument in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross*: a written decision endorsing the district court's factor-four analysis would materially narrow the fair-use defense available to generative AI defendants, change settlement math across every pending case, and provide the first circuit-level pricing signal for AI training licenses.
