### Copyright Office AI Study Part 3: Licensing Frameworks and the Opt-Out Debate

The Copyright Office's May 22, 2026 Part 3 report — the capstone of a three-part statutory study commissioned under Section 108 of the Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. § 108, following the Register's notice of inquiry in August 2023 — runs to 214 pages and makes four principal findings that rights-holders and their counsel must internalize immediately (__[U.S. Copyright Office — needs verification](https://copyright.gov/ai/ai-study-part3.pdf)__).

First, the Office declines to recommend a mandatory statutory licensing regime for AI training ingestion, concluding that existing Section 106 exclusive rights — reproduction, preparation of derivative works, and distribution under 17 U.S.C. §§ 106(1), (2), (3) — remain the operative legal framework absent Congressional action. This conclusion reinforces the posture of plaintiffs across the pending litigation docket: if no statutory license exists, any ingestion without authorization is presumptively infringing absent a successful fair-use defense under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

Second, the Office finds that an opt-out regime — under which rights-holders would affirmatively exclude their works from training datasets — would be constitutionally and administratively feasible but endorses it only as a "second-best" solution. The practical implication: Congress may legislate an opt-out framework on a faster timeline than a licensing framework, and AI developers are already lobbying for exactly that outcome. Rights-holder counsel should begin advising clients to document their datasets and register works now under 17 U.S.C. § 412 to preserve the right to seek statutory damages and attorney's fees for any infringement occurring after a registration date, regardless of how the legislative debate resolves.

Third — and most contested — the Office declines to opine definitively on whether large-scale scraping for AI training constitutes fair use under § 107, deferring to the courts. It does, however, characterize the *Authors Guild v. Google* (Books) precedent, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), as "distinguishable on the commercial-purpose and market-substitution axes" from generative-AI training, language that will be quoted in every plaintiff's opposition brief filed before Judge Orrick, Judge Stein, and Judge Trauger for the foreseeable future (__[U.S. Copyright Office — needs verification](https://copyright.gov/ai/ai-study-part3.pdf)__).

Fourth, the Office recommends that Congress authorize the Office itself to convene a series of multi-stakeholder licensing roundtables — modeled on the Section 115 music licensing reform that ultimately produced the Music Modernization Act, Pub. L. 115-264 (2018) — to negotiate voluntary licensing terms for training-data use. The report explicitly references the collective licensing structures of the Authors Licensing and Collecting Society (ALCS) in the United Kingdom and the German VG Wort as comparative models. Rights-holders with international footprints should coordinate with ALCS and VG Wort counsel immediately if they have not already done so.

### *Bartz v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal. No. 5:23-cv-03223): Discovery Disruption and Schedule Risk

Judge William Orrick's May 22, 2026 order in *Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:23-cv-03223-WHO (N.D. Cal.), denying plaintiffs' motion to re-open fact discovery without prejudice is a tactical setback that warrants careful unpacking (__[PACER — needs verification](https://pacer.gov)__). Plaintiffs — represented by Joseph Saveri Law Firm and Susman Godfrey — sought to supplement their existing document requests with targeted subpoenas to Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud, arguing that Anthropic's representation that its training-data corpus resided "primarily on AWS infrastructure" was materially incomplete given recently produced internal engineering memos suggesting that portions of the Constitutional AI dataset were processed through Google's TPU Research Cloud circa Q3 2022.

Judge Orrick's order — 11 pages — declines the request on ripeness grounds, finding that plaintiffs must first exhaust their existing custodial-log requests before seeking third-party subpoenas. The order is notable in two respects. First, it reaffirms the court's earlier ruling, at ECF No. 312 (March 14, 2026), that Anthropic must produce its "model cards, red-teaming reports, and training-run provenance logs" for Claude 1.0 through Claude 3.5 — a ruling that remains operative and that Anthropic has now partially complied with, having produced approximately 11,400 pages of responsive materials in the May 6 rolling production. Second, the order sets a May 30 deadline for Anthropic to produce a complete custodial log naming every individual with access to the training corpus, including contractors, annotators, and infrastructure personnel — a list that could materially expand the scope of deposition practice.

Class certification briefing remains on track: plaintiffs' motion is due July 18, 2026; Anthropic's opposition is due September 12; reply due October 10. The certification hearing before Judge Orrick is calendared for November 4, 2026, Courtroom 2, 17th Floor, San Francisco Courthouse (__[PACER — needs verification](https://pacer.gov)__). The statutory-damages exposure if a class is certified under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) — $750 to $150,000 per registered work per infringer — makes this the most financially consequential certification hearing in the current litigation cycle. Even a conservative estimate of 50,000 registered works at the $750 floor yields $37.5 million; at willfulness premiums the arithmetic scales to the billions.

### *Andersen v. Stability AI* (N.D. Cal. → 9th Cir.): En Banc Argument Set for September 16

The Ninth Circuit's en banc scheduling order, entered May 21, 2026, in *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.*, No. 23-15440 (9th Cir.), sets oral argument for September 16, 2026 at 9:00 a.m. in Courtroom 1, the James R. Browning United States Courthouse, San Francisco (__[U.S. Court of Appeals, Ninth Circuit — needs verification](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov)__). The en banc panel — eleven judges drawn from a pool including Chief Judge Mary Murguia — will consider two certified questions: (1) whether the direct-copying required to train a diffusion model satisfies the fixation and volitional-conduct elements of direct infringement under 17 U.S.C. §§ 106(1) and 501, and (2) whether compressed latent-space embeddings in a trained model constitute an infringing "copy" or "derivative work" within the meaning of 17 U.S.C. §§ 101 and 106(2).

Both questions were left unresolved in the three-judge panel's February 2026 opinion, which affirmed the district court's dismissal of the DMCA § 1202 claims (Digital Millennium Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. § 1202(b)) while certifying the direct and vicarious infringement claims for en banc review. The February panel found that plaintiffs had not adequately pled that Stability AI "intentionally removed or altered" copyright management information, as required by § 1202(b)(1), because the training pipeline's statistical transformation process was not equivalent to the manual removal contemplated by the statute's drafting history.

The September 16 argument is the first appellate argument on the merits of AI copyright infringement in any U.S. circuit. The outcome will constrain — or liberate — the fair-use analysis in every district court below. Amicus briefing deadlines: supporting petitioners (artists/rights-holders) due July 25; supporting respondents (AI developers) due August 22. The Copyright Office's Part 3 report released Thursday will almost certainly be cited in every amicus brief; practitioners should anticipate the Office filing its own amicus brief given its explicit policy views on the derivative-works question (__[U.S. Court of Appeals, Ninth Circuit — needs verification](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov)__).

### *New York Times v. Microsoft* (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195): Bifurcation Denied; Damages Discovery Accelerates

Judge Sidney Stein's May 20, 2026 order in *The New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp. and OpenAI LP*, No. 1:23-cv-11195-SHS (S.D.N.Y.), denying defendants' motion to bifurcate liability and damages discovery is a significant plaintiff-side victory that reshapes the litigation's timeline (__[PACER — needs verification](https://pacer.gov)__). Microsoft and OpenAI argued that running damages and liability discovery concurrently would impose disproportionate burden — their motion characterized the damages universe as potentially requiring review of 40+ million tokens of GPT-3 and GPT-4 training data and associated revenue-allocation models — and asked the court to sequence liability first with a 90-day lag before damages discovery began.

Judge Stein's four-page order rejected that framing, finding that "the liability and damages questions are sufficiently intertwined — particularly as to the fair-use fourth-factor market-harm analysis under 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) — that bifurcation would create redundancy rather than efficiency." The ruling means that The Times's damages experts, including its retained valuation firms, will receive access to Microsoft's Azure cost-allocation data and OpenAI's GPT-4 training-run expenditure logs in parallel with fact witness depositions. The parties' joint proposed discovery schedule is due June 6, 2026.

The Times's complaint, as amended, seeks actual damages, disgorgement of profits attributable to infringement, and statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504 for registered works. OpenAI has produced in prior submissions evidence that GPT-4 training involved ingestion of approximately 13 trillion tokens; The Times contends a non-trivial fraction derived from Times-owned content accessible through Common Crawl snapshots and direct news-archive scraping. At any disgorgement theory, the revenue base — OpenAI's annualized API and consumer revenue reportedly exceeding $3.4 billion as of Q1 2026 — would yield enormous potential exposure even after apportionment (__[Bloomberg — needs verification](https://bloomberg.com)__).

### *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (M.D. Tenn. No. 3:23-cv-01092): Consolidation Order and Discovery Calendar

Judge Aleta Trauger's May 19, 2026 consolidation order in *Concord Music Group, Inc. et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:23-cv-01092 (M.D. Tenn.), brings together for discovery purposes *Concord Music Group* (filed October 2023), *Universal Music Group Recordings, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-00527 (M.D. Tenn.) (filed May 2024), and *ABKCO Music & Records, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-01161 (M.D. Tenn.) (filed October 2024) (__[PACER — needs verification](https://pacer.gov)__). The three cases, brought by the major music publishing and recording houses, allege that Claude's training corpus incorporated lyrics and compositions without license, and that Claude's outputs — when prompted for song lyrics — reproduce substantially similar protected expression in violation of 17 U.S.C. §§ 106(1) and (2).

Judge Trauger's order does not formally consolidate the cases for trial — she explicitly preserves each plaintiffs' group's right to separate trial — but establishes a single discovery master (Magistrate Judge Alistair Newberne, appointed May 19) and a unified document-production protocol. The consolidated discovery schedule: fact discovery closes November 14, 2026; expert reports due January 16, 2027; expert discovery closes March 13, 2027. The consolidation materially reduces Anthropic's ability to run inconsistent factual positions across three separate dockets, which had been a concern flagged in *Concord*'s May 5, 2026 status conference before Judge Trauger.

The music cases are strategically distinct from the *Bartz* and *Times* litigations in one important respect: plaintiffs hold registered copyrights with pre-infringement registration dates for virtually their entire catalogs, meaning statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) are available as of right, without the predicate-registration timing disputes that complicate some of the authors' cases. With the three publishing/recording groups collectively owning millions of registered works, the ceiling on statutory-damages exposure — even at the $750 per-work floor — is effectively unlimited as a practical matter.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holders and their counsel should take the following immediate steps in light of this week's developments: (1) **Registration audit** — confirm that all works for which AI ingestion claims are contemplated carry pre-infringement copyright registrations under 17 U.S.C. § 412, as this is the threshold predicate for statutory damages and attorney's fees in every pending and prospective case; works not yet registered should be submitted to the Copyright Office under the group registration option (GRO) for published photographs, short online literary works (SOLO), or other applicable group programs to reduce per-work cost; (2) **Copyright Office comment period** — the Part 3 report will trigger a public comment period (expected to open within 30 days per the Register's standard practice) in which rights-holders can submit formal positions on licensing-framework design; coordinated submissions from music publishers, book authors, visual artists, and news organizations will carry greater administrative weight than fragmented filings; (3) **Ninth Circuit amicus coordination** — rights-holder trade groups, including Authors Guild, Association of American Publishers, and NMPA, should begin coordinating amicus briefs in *Andersen v. Stability AI* now, with the July 25 filing deadline approximately nine weeks out; the en banc panel's resolution of the derivative-works and fixation questions will govern every district-court proceeding in the circuit, and a well-developed amicus record can materially shape the analytical framework; (4) **Litigation-hold notices for AI companies** — counsel pursuing or contemplating claims against any generative-AI developer should serve litigation-hold notices and preservation letters cross-referencing the *Bartz* custodial-log order, which establishes that cloud-vendor infrastructure logs constitute responsive ESI; and (5) **Monitor Tennessee consolidation closely** — the unified discovery master in the *Concord/UMG/ABKCO* consolidation will set document-production protocols applicable to all three cases; any music-industry rights-holder with related claims should consult with plaintiff-side counsel about whether permissive joinder or intervention is procedurally available before the November 14 discovery close.
