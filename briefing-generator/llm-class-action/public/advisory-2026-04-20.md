# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 20, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The Bartz v. Anthropic settlement enters its final pre-hearing phase with twenty-four days remaining until the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín and Anthropic's second installment of $300 million due in just ten days on April 30, bringing total funded commitments to approximately $600 million of the $1.5 billion settlement fund — a procedural posture that places simultaneous pressure on class counsel to demonstrate Rule 23(e)(2) adequacy and on Anthropic to demonstrate financial capacity for the remaining $900 million in installments running through September 2027. The cross-industry alignment against Anthropic's training practices deepened this month when the Association of American Publishers, News/Media Alliance, the International Association of Scientific, Technical & Medical Publishers, and the Authors Guild filed a second amicus brief in the original Concord Music action (No. 5:24-cv-03811), joining the RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, and allied organizations that filed on March 30 — together constituting the broadest coalition of rights-holder organizations yet assembled in any AI copyright case, spanning music, book, news, and academic publishing and arguing that Anthropic's unlicensed copying undermines the nascent licensed market for AI training data. ([AAP](https://publishers.org/news/book-news-and-journal-publishers-join-with-authors-in-amicus-brief-in-support-of-music-publishers-in-concord-v-anthropic/)) ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) The practical significance of this coalition extends beyond the Concord docket: the amicus arguments — particularly the contention that a functioning licensing market already exists and that AI-generated outputs act as direct market substitutes for human-created works — provide the analytical foundation for Judge Martínez-Olguín to evaluate the Bartz settlement's per-title valuation under the fourth fair-use factor and the Rule 23(e)(2) adequacy standard, where objectors like Professor Lea Victoria Bishop have challenged the $3,000 per-title payout as a substantial discount against the $150,000 statutory ceiling under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c).

The music-industry litigation landscape continues to evolve in ways that directly inform settlement valuation across all AI copyright dockets. Warner Music Group's landmark settlement with Suno — the first major-label licensing deal with an AI music generator — established a precedent in which Suno will deprecate its current unlicensed models and launch new licensed models in 2026, with downloading restricted to paid accounts and subject to caps, while WMG artists retain full control over name, image, likeness, and voice usage. ([Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/suno-warner-music-group-ai-music-settlement-lawsuit-1235472868/)) This structural framework, combined with the earlier UMG and WMG settlements with Udio, establishes a clear pattern: AI music generators that trained on copyrighted content without authorization are being required to transition to licensed models as a condition of settlement. The UMG case against Suno remains active before Judge Saylor in Boston, where a fair-use ruling is expected by summer 2026, and the outcome will have direct implications for the Concord v. Anthropic partial summary judgment motion pending in the Northern District of California, where plaintiffs argue that Anthropic's copying of 499 songs constitutes per se infringement. ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026))

The In re OpenAI MDL (No. 3143) continues its intensive discovery phase following Judge Stein's March 9, 2026 order compelling OpenAI to produce the expanded reservoir of 78 million and 10 million ChatGPT logs beyond the original 20-million-log sample — an order that represents the most significant discovery ruling in any AI copyright case and will generate the granular output-level evidence needed to test the "transformative use" framework at scale when fair-use motions are briefed later this year. The SCOTUS's unanimous Cox v. Sony decision of March 25, 2026, narrowing contributory-infringement liability to cases of active inducement or services "not capable of substantial or commercially significant noninfringing uses," has already prompted strategic pleading adjustments across the docket, most notably in the Concord action where plaintiffs amended to redirect their intentional-contributory-infringement claim from Anthropic PBC to CEO Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann personally, relying on unsealed Bartz filings as evidence of intent. ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/04/supreme-court-narrows-secondary-copyright-liability-in-cox)) Internationally, the UK Court of Appeal proceedings in Getty Images v. Stability AI — the first appellate consideration of whether intangible AI model weights constitute "infringing copies" under CDPA § 27 — are expected to be heard during 2026, while the CJEU's deliberation in Like Company v. Google on generative AI and copyright under EU law remains under advisement following the March 10 hearing. ([Chambers and Partners](https://chambers.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-remain-in-focus-for-2026-with-getty-appeal-given-the-green-light)) ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/next-steps-for-getty-v-stability-why-has-permission-to-appeal-been-granted))

On the legislative front, the bipartisan CLEAR Act (S. 3813) continues to gather industry support, with its retroactive disclosure mandate requiring AI developers to file detailed notices with the Register of Copyrights enumerating every copyrighted work in their training datasets within 30 days before commercial release, backed by civil penalties of $5,000 per unreported work — a framework that, if enacted, would create an unprecedented public database of training-data composition and expose AI companies to potentially billions in penalties for historical omissions. ([IP Watchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/02/11/clear-act-establish-notice-requirements-copyrighted-works-ai-training-data/)) ([Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/legislation-watch-for-ai-developers-and-registered-copyright-owners-the-federal-clear-act/)) The legislative posture reinforces the litigation trajectory: whether through judicial rulings on fair use, congressional mandates on disclosure, or the market-pricing precedent being set by the growing number of industry licensing deals — Disney's $1 billion OpenAI investment, WMG-Suno, UMG-Udio, and the AP/Reuters/Reddit content partnerships — the legal and commercial environment is converging toward a framework in which unauthorized AI training on copyrighted works carries substantial financial consequences under 17 U.S.C. §§ 106 and 504.

## Recommended Actions

Class members who submitted Bartz claims by the March 30 deadline should verify their submission confirmations through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and preserve all correspondence from the settlement administrator, as the final claim count will be central to Judge Martínez-Olguín's Rule 23(e)(2) adequacy analysis at the May 14 hearing; rights-holders who opted out, including those joining the Carreyrou multi-defendant action against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Perplexity, should confirm that their separate counsel has preserved all evidence of Anthropic's torrenting activity from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror, given the strengthened per se infringement theory. AI developers with music-generation or character-generation capabilities should study both the WMG-Suno settlement structure — particularly its requirement to deprecate unlicensed models and transition to licensed alternatives — and the Disney-OpenAI deal framework as licensing templates, given that the existence of functioning willing-licensor markets substantially weakens fourth-factor fair-use defenses in pending cases. Companies subject to the proposed CLEAR Act should begin internal audits of training-data provenance and copyright status now, as the bill's retroactive disclosure requirements and $5,000-per-work civil penalties create substantial exposure for historical omissions, and early compliance preparation may reduce both litigation risk and regulatory penalties. Practitioners tracking the Concord v. Anthropic docket should monitor for the court's response to the partial summary judgment motion and the now-consolidated amicus record from music, publishing, news, and academic industries — the breadth of this cross-industry coalition signals that any fair-use ruling in the Concord action will be treated as dispositive precedent across AI copyright litigation generally.
