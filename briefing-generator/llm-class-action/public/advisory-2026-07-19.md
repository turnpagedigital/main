### Bartz v. Anthropic — Settlement Administration and Distribution Timeline

The architecture of the $1.5 billion fund matters as much as the approval ruling itself for rights-holder counsel tracking recovery timing.


At the May 14 fairness hearing before Judge Martínez-Olguín, class counsel Justin Nelson reported a per-work payout of $3,100, with a 92.77% participation rate across the works list.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench and indicated that short supplemental orders may follow; the fairness hearing itself was one of the last major procedural steps before a final approval ruling.


The $3,100 per-work figure is a product of the high claim rate, not a fixed contractual term. 
Class counsel reported that 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a 91.3% rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the March 19, 2026 motion for final approval, 11 days before the March 30 claims deadline.
 
That high rate means forecasts of a larger payout predicated on the 54% figure will not materialize; the actual per-work amount is now closer to the originally projected ~$3,000 figure.


On the payment schedule: 
Anthropic funds the settlement in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
The $1.5 billion minimum is being funded in multiple installments; the first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.
 Per the distribution timeline, 
the settlement administrator is expected to issue a second wave of checks around October 9, 2026, with Anthropic's first scheduled payment of $450 million due September 25, 2026, 12 months after the issuance of the preliminary approval order.


Fee structure note: 
Class counsel urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorneys' fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Anthropic separately argued that the $75 million in fees requested by non-class counsel is excessive, with "scant justification" for that request.
 The fee disputes remain live before the court and will be resolved in the final approval order.

Scope caution for rights-holder counsel: 
the Bartz settlement is widely misunderstood — it is not a settlement about AI training.
 
Judge Alsup certified the class only for the piracy (shadow-library download) theory; the fair-use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers.
 
For Anthropic, the settlement releases liability for past conduct in exchange for the $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies; it does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme and does not bind any other AI developer or future case.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Two Parallel Dockets, One SJ Hearing in October

The Concord/Universal music-publisher litigation against Anthropic now operates on two simultaneous tracks in N.D. Cal., both before Judge Eumi K. Lee, with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen referred.

**Concord I** — 
*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.)
 — is the original October 2023 action brought by Concord, Universal Music Corp., Songs of Universal, ABKCO Music, Polygram Publishing, Capitol CMG, and affiliated entities, alleging infringement through Claude's training and lyric outputs. Cross-motions for summary judgment are fully briefed. 
The summary judgment hearing in No. 5:24-cv-03811 before Judge Eumi Lee is calendared for October 21, 2026. The questions presented are whether partial summary judgment on copyright infringement should be granted to plaintiffs, or whether summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement should be granted to Anthropic. Plaintiffs filed their motion for partial summary judgment on March 23, 2026.



Music publishers argue in their motion for partial summary judgment that fair use does not excuse Anthropic's conduct because Anthropic used the lyrics to build a competing commercial product.
 
Anthropic contends that its use is "transformative" fair use, analogizing Claude's learning to how humans learn from reading and internalizing themes.


A subsidiary litigation integrity issue warrants attention. 
A California federal magistrate judge partially struck an expert report filed by Anthropic after that report cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," though "not quite so grave as it first appeared."
 
A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic acknowledged using Claude.ai to help draft the expert declaration that included the erroneous citation, characterizing the error as "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority."


**Concord II** — 
*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.), filed January 28, 2026
 — is a separate action arising from Anthropic's alleged torrenting of pirated book files containing musical compositions. 
Publishers allege that Anthropic concealed during discovery in Concord I that it had separately torrented millions of copyrighted books — including works that contain publishers' musical compositions — from pirate library websites, and that publishers did not learn of this until July 2025 when Judge Alsup's ruling in Bartz publicly revealed the torrenting conduct for the first time.
 
Anthropic filed a motion to stay Concord II, with a hearing set for May 20, 2026, before Judge Lee in San Jose Courtroom 7, 4th Floor.
 The docket as of May 31, 2026 does not reflect a stay order; the matter appears to remain live.

The October 21 hearing in Concord I is the next major inflection point on the fair-use question for AI training of musical compositions — and the result will be the first substantive ruling by Judge Lee, whose decisions have no direct precedential binding on the Bartz/Alsup framework but will inform the emerging circuit-level split.

### NYT v. OpenAI / In re OpenAI MDL — Sanctions Motion and Discovery Escalation


*In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation*, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.), before Judge Sidney H. Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang referred
, remained active through at least July 10, 2026 (last CourtListener update). The most significant recent development is a sanctions motion.


On July 9, 2026, a group of newspapers including The New York Times and New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan to sanction OpenAI in their copyright dispute for allegedly lying to the court about OpenAI's ability to search its systems for proof that it misused millions of articles in AI training.
 
The newspapers told the court that OpenAI falsely represented it could not search its large language models for their copyrighted material, while hiding that it had done so "even before the first News Plaintiff filed suit."
 
The newspapers also alleged that OpenAI deleted billions of relevant ChatGPT conversations or made them unsearchable. They asked for sanctions including attorneys' fees and a court finding that OpenAI's chat logs showed misuse of copyrighted works.


This motion is consequential beyond the NYT case. 
Judge Sidney Stein had previously ordered OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs on January 5, 2026 — characterized as the most consequential discovery order in any AI copyright case.
 If the July 9 sanctions motion is granted — even partially — the court finding that OpenAI's logs affirmatively show infringement would shift the 17 U.S.C. § 504 damages calculus for every MDL plaintiff and structurally disadvantage any settlement posture predicated on liability uncertainty.

Separately, 
in June 2026, the NYT amended its complaint to allege that Microsoft had built a dedicated supercomputer infrastructure specifically to help OpenAI use Times content without authorization — broadening the theory of liability to encompass Microsoft's infrastructure role.

### Thomson Reuters v. Ross — Third Circuit Appeal Argued; Ruling Pending


On February 11, 2025, in *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence*, (D. Del., No. 1:20-cv-00613, Judge Stephanos Bibas), the court granted partial summary judgment for Thomson Reuters, finding that Ross Intelligence's copying of Westlaw headnotes to train its AI legal-research tool was not fair use.
 
The fourth fair-use factor under 17 U.S.C. § 107 — effect on the market — heavily favored Thomson Reuters, as Ross's tool was directly competitive with Westlaw and undercut its market.



The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling — argued June 11, 2026 — is pending.
 
Three U.S. district judges have now weighed in on AI training and fair use: Alsup (Bartz) and Chhabria (Kadrey) found for AI companies on training; Bibas (Thomson Reuters v. Ross) found against.
 The Third Circuit decision will be the first U.S. appellate ruling on AI-training fair use and the most important fair-use development since Bibas. A ruling affirming Bibas creates a circuit-court precedent favorable to plaintiffs on the market-harm factor and materially strengthens the § 107(4) argument in every pending case — including both Concord I and the MDL.

### International: UK Getty Appeal, GEMA/OpenAI, and EU TDM Framework


The UK High Court found in November 2025 that Stable Diffusion model weights are not "infringing copies" of Getty's photographs — meaning the model itself does not reproduce the images. Getty had abandoned its primary training-phase and output-infringement claims before judgment, leaving only secondary claims, which the court also rejected. Getty won narrow trademark findings tied to early versions of Stable Diffusion that produced distorted watermarks.



The Munich Regional Court ruled against OpenAI in *GEMA v. OpenAI* in November 2025
 — a result directly opposite to the UK High Court's finding on Stability AI. 
OpenAI is appealing; the appeal will be heard by the Munich Court of Appeal (OLG München), and a CJEU referral remains possible that could produce harmonized EU standards.
 
Until a higher court rules, every AI provider operating in the EU faces the risk that a model capable of reproducing training-data content on demand may be liable for each reproduction.


For context on the divergence: 
the same technology, trained on the same type of content, produced opposite first-instance verdicts in two legal systems within days of each other.
 The EU AI Act / DSM Directive Article 4 TDM exception does not resolve the output-reproduction question, which is the operative liability hook in both GEMA and Concord I.

### Human-Authorship Floor — Thaler v. Perlmutter Closed


The U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* on March 2, 2026, reaffirming human authorship as a foundational requirement of U.S. copyright law even in the context of rapid AI advancement.
 
This does not prohibit copyright protection for works created with AI assistance — human contributions with AI assistive use may be protectable — but because Dr. Thaler explicitly and repeatedly disclaimed any human creative input, the Court did not address how much human involvement is necessary to support copyright registration for AI-assisted works.
 That question remains open and directly affects derivative-work and training-output claims in every AI copyright case where the defendant argues its outputs lack protectable human authorship.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should press the settlement administrator on the distribution timeline for the Bartz first wave, which is contingent on final approval — still outstanding as of today — and note that Anthropic's second installment of $450 million is not due until September 25, 2026, meaning no meaningful distribution is likely before Q4 2026 at the earliest; 
payouts will only issue after Judge Martínez-Olguín grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved, with payments commencing in the late fall at the earliest.
 On the Concord I track, the October 21, 2026 summary judgment hearing before Judge Lee is the next actionable date; parties with exposure across both the training-data and output-reproduction theories should monitor whether Judge Lee bifurcates fair-use analysis by use type, as the two-part framework from Alsup's Bartz opinion and Bibas's Ross opinion point in opposite directions on transformativeness. In the MDL, the July 9 sanctions motion against OpenAI is the most immediate litigation risk for defendants and the most immediate potential leverage event for publisher plaintiffs — if granted, any court finding of spoliation or misrepresentation has § 504 and settlement-valuation implications across the entire S.D.N.Y. docket. Watch the Third Circuit for the first appellate word on § 107(4) market harm; an affirmance of Bibas would be the most plaintiff-favorable legal development in the AI copyright cycle to date.
