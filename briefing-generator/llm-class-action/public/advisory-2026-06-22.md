### Bartz v. Anthropic — Post-Hearing, No Final Order Yet


The fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* took place May 14, 2026, before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in the Northern District of California, addressing approval of the $1.5 billion settlement.
 
After a 75-minute hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín declined, for now, to approve the settlement, though the tone of the hearing led most observers to expect final approval relatively quickly.


The post-hearing order tells you where the lingering risk sits. 
The judge took the motions under submission and issued an order directing Anthropic, by no later than May 21, 2026, to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored; Plaintiffs were simultaneously directed to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1); the Court stated it would not require anything more from objectors nor consider further submissions from them.


The economics of the settlement are now fixed. 
Class counsel Justin Nelson reported that the payout per work would be $3,100, and the class had a 92.77% participation rate for works on the works list.
 
A total of 440,490 of 482,460 eligible works were claimed — a 91.3% participation rate at the time of the earlier filing — far surpassing the 54% rate reported in counsel's Motion for Final Approval filed March 19, 2026, indicating a surge of claims in the final days before the March 30 deadline.
 For comparison, typical class action participation runs approximately 10%.

The fee structure remains a potential appellate hook. 
Plaintiffs urged the court to grant final approval and an attorney fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
According to the Authors Alliance summary, Judge Martínez-Olguín's questions at the hearing focused on attorneys' fees and the structure of the cost reserve, rather than the merits of the deal itself.
 The fee-multiplier framing and the cost-reserve structure — not the underlying liability question — are the live risk vectors between now and final order entry.

The structural constraint for claimants: 
payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.
 
Anthropic is funding the settlement in four installments — October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 The April 30 installment has already been funded; the September 25, 2026 tranche is the next near-term event regardless of when the order issues.

**Systems-level note:** 
The Bartz settlement has been treated by the industry as a template — a number other AI labs and rights holders are quietly using as a benchmark in their own negotiations. If Judge Martínez-Olguín approves the deal as written, expect a wave of similar class settlements in the OpenAI, Meta, and Microsoft cases; if she requires material changes — higher per-work payments, broader injunctive relief, or stronger deletion provisions — the leverage in every pending AI training case shifts toward plaintiffs.



The $1.5 billion settlement establishes the first concrete per-work pricing benchmark ($3,113/book) for AI training on pirated content; a Norton Rose Fulbright March 2026 analysis notes that statutory damages can reach $150,000 per work under 17 U.S.C. § 504, meaning the settlement resolved at approximately 2.1% of theoretical maximum exposure — a ratio that gives plaintiff attorneys in other AI copyright cases a credible floor for settlement negotiations.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Two Active Dockets, One Approaching Decision

The music-publisher track against Anthropic now runs on parallel rails that require tracking separately.

**Concord I** — 
*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.), assigned to Judge Eumi K. Lee with Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen, was last updated on CourtListener on June 10, 2026.
 This is the first-filed action and the one that controls the timeline for the entire Concord dispute. 
Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues governing the claims.
 
Per Anthropic's March 2026 briefing, dispositive motions in Concord I were scheduled to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026.
 That deadline has now passed — the cross-MSJ briefing in Concord I is complete as of this writing.

The three questions posed to Judge Lee at summary judgment are consequential beyond this case: 
whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use under § 107 — the same question that will determine the viability of most if not all of the publishers' claims in Concord II — with preclusive effect in the later-filed case.
 The output-infringement theory (Claude reproducing verbatim lyrics in response to user prompts) sits alongside the training-data theory; the two prongs track § 107(1) (purpose and character) and § 107(4) (market harm) differently. 
Music lyrics aggregator sites have already obtained licenses to display song lyrics, creating an established market that the publishers argue Anthropic is infringing — a clean § 107(4) market-substitution argument.


**Concord II** — 
*Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal.), assigned to the same judge, was filed January 28, 2026 and last updated May 31, 2026.
 
Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending final resolution of Concord I, or alternatively pending resolution of the parties' Concord I summary judgment motions, with the stay motion heard May 20, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. in Courtroom 7 of the N.D. Cal. San Jose Division (280 South First Street).
 The outcome of that stay motion is not yet confirmed in verified sources as of this writing. If the stay is granted, Concord II sits dormant until Judge Lee rules on cross-MSJs in Concord I. If denied, Anthropic faces simultaneous active discovery obligations across both dockets.

The practical significance: Concord I's summary judgment ruling — expected sometime this summer given the June 8 briefing close — will be the **first district court ruling on fair use in an AI training case involving output reproduction** rather than just ingestion. That distinguishes it from the Alsup ruling in Bartz, which addressed piracy, not training-as-fair-use on its own terms.

### Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence — Third Circuit Oral Argument Completed June 11


The first appellate ruling on fair use in an AI training case is now in deliberation at the Third Circuit, following oral argument June 11, 2026; the case, *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence*, involves non-generative AI — Ross's system used Westlaw headnotes to train a legal search tool — but the fair use analysis it produces is expected to influence every generative AI training case in the pipeline.



At the district level, on February 11, 2025, the court granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Westlaw's headnotes and Key Number System were original and protected by copyright, and that Ross Intelligence's actual copying of the headnotes was not fair use.
 
The § 107(4) market-harm factor — "undoubtedly the single most important element of fair use" — favored Thomson Reuters because of the potential impact on Thomson Reuters' ability to sell its own data for AI training if Ross's use were permissible; the court flatly rejected the fair use defense as a matter of law.


The Third Circuit's decision will answer two certified questions: whether Westlaw headnotes are sufficiently original to be copyrightable, and whether training an AI on those headnotes is fair use. 
Its ruling is expected to influence every generative AI copyright case in the pipeline, even though Ross involved non-generative AI.
 A ruling against Ross on the fair use question — applying the § 107(4) market-substitution logic to training data — would materially strengthen publishers' and authors' positions in Concord I, the OpenAI MDL, and the NYT case. A ruling for Ross would not resolve the GenAI training question but would create meaningful friction for rights-holders seeking summary judgment on factor one and four.

### In re OpenAI Copyright MDL (S.D.N.Y., No. 1:24-md-03143) — Discovery in Progress


The consolidated MDL in the Southern District of New York brings together 16 copyright lawsuits against OpenAI; the lead cases include *The New York Times* (filed December 27, 2023), the New York Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting.
 
Authors including John Grisham, George R.R. Martin, Jonathan Franzen, and Jodi Picoult are plaintiffs in consolidated author suits.



On January 5, 2026, U.S. District Judge Sidney Stein affirmed a magistrate judge's order compelling OpenAI to produce the entire 20 million-log sample of anonymized ChatGPT conversations — not just the cherry-picked conversations implicating plaintiffs' specific works — marking a significant discovery victory for the news organizations and authors suing OpenAI.
 
Legal analysts suggest these logs, once analyzed, will effectively dictate settlement terms for the remainder of the consolidated litigation.


The 20-million-log production is the most consequential near-term development in the MDL. Output-level evidence — whether ChatGPT reproduced plaintiffs' articles verbatim or near-verbatim in response to user prompts — speaks directly to both the § 107(1) transformation inquiry and the § 107(4) market harm question. If the logs show systematic reproduction of NYT content, the fair use defense weakens materially, and the Bartz $3,100/work figure becomes a floor rather than a ceiling for any eventual settlement calculus.

### Thaler v. Perlmutter — Human Authorship Requirement Confirmed at Supreme Court Level


Dr. Thaler sued the U.S. Copyright Office seeking judicial review of its refusal to register his AI-generated artwork; on March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari, confirming the principle that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable under 17 U.S.C. § 411.
 This closes the question at the Supreme Court level — for now. It has a direct downstream effect on rights-holder litigation strategy: any AI-generated content interpolated into a training dataset by the defendant's own model cannot itself support a copyright counterclaim; but the underlying human-authored works ingested for training remain fully protected.
