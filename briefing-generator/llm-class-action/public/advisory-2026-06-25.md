### Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 — Distribution Watch

The settlement architecture bears restating because distribution mechanics are now the operative concern for rights-holder counsel. 
Anthropic agreed to fund the $1.5 billion settlement in four installments: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027.
 
The fund is split among both authors and publishers of the approximately 500,000 titles that met the class definition — ISBN/ASIN registration within five years of publication and before Anthropic's download date — at a floor of approximately $3,000 per title, less costs and fees.
 At the May 14 hearing, 
class counsel Justin Nelson reported that the payout per work in the class will be $3,100.
 
The default allocation is 50% to the author side and 50% to the publisher side; parties may deviate by submitting the actual publishing contract.


The fee dimension is unresolved and consequential. 
After Judge Alsup's December 2025 memorandum questioning the original fee request, class counsel reduced its petition on March 19, 2026 from $300 million (20% of the fund) to $187.5 million (12.5%).
 
Plaintiffs urged the court to grant final approval along with the $187.5 million fee request, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections.
 
Judge Martínez-Olguín's questioning at the fairness hearing focused heavily on the fee request and on expense accounting
 — not on the merits of the settlement itself, which signals she is unlikely to derail the deal on substantive grounds.

The opt-out posture also has downstream consequences. 
Opt-outs remain modest: 350 valid opt-outs covering 1,802 works, with a handful of late opt-outs received after the deadline.
 Those 350 opt-outs are not inconsequential, however. 
Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal., No. 3:26-cv-04482, demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works.
 
A follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.
 Those cases will proceed on the piracy-liability theory that *Bartz* settled around, informed by Judge Alsup's fair-use ruling on lawful-acquisition training but unconstrained by the settlement release.

The structural limitation of the settlement deserves emphasis for any rights-holder counsel advising clients on scope. 
For Anthropic, the settlement releases liability for past conduct in exchange for the $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies; it does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme and does not bind any other AI developer or any future case.
 
Participating in the settlement does not give Anthropic a license to use works in the future; the release does not apply to anything Anthropic does after August 26, 2025, or to any claims related to infringing outputs from Anthropic's AI models.


On the due-process objection front: 
objector Chinonto Chakanga raised the argument that thousands of pages of sealed summary judgment records, class certification briefs, and expert declarations were not unsealed until January 21, 2026 — eight days before the original opt-out deadline and 19 days before the extended deadline — and, citing the Ninth Circuit's January 28, 2026 decision in *Avery v. TEKsystems*, argued that compressed timing combined with a sealed judicial record constitutes a substantive defect in the opt-out process.
 
Following the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín ordered that by May 21, 2026, Anthropic may file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and plaintiffs shall file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1).
 The final approval order has not appeared in the public docket as of the June 22 CourtListener sync. Rights-holders tracking disbursement should note that 
the settlement administrator is scheduled to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement expected to begin June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed.

### Jamendo v. Nvidia — A New Fault Line: Dataset Governance

The structurally most important new complaint filed this week is not a book case. 
Nvidia faces a new lawsuit claiming it illegally trained its AI music models Fugatto and Audio Flamingo on Jamendo's copyrighted trove of songs.
 
Jamendo is seeking an injunction, plus actual damages and Nvidia's profits of "no less than" EUR €17.8 million (approximately USD $20.3 million).
 The complaint was filed in the Northern District of California; a specific docket number has not yet been confirmed in the public record as of this writing and should be verified before citing in any filing.

The case sits at a distinct intersection from the shadow-library suits. 
The Jamendo case against Nvidia is not just another "AI company scraped copyrighted works" lawsuit — it is more precise and potentially more important for the future of AI data governance because it sits at the intersection of four unresolved fault lines: Creative Commons licensing, non-commercial research datasets, music training data, and the emerging market for curated AI-ready datasets.
 
Jamendo says it created and curated a large music database including metadata, tags, classification systems, playlists, and commercial licensing structures; a subset became the MTG-Jamendo Dataset, built with the Music Technology Group at Universitat Pompeu Fabra for academic and research use, reportedly containing more than 55,000 full audio tracks with genre, instrument, mood, and theme tags.


The registration posture is significant for § 504 statutory-damages eligibility. 
The complaint says the U.S. Copyright Office issued the MTG-Jamendo Dataset registration on June 17, 2026, shortly before the complaint was filed, while the alleged Nvidia use was discovered in or around March 2024.
 
That may not defeat the claim, but it could complicate remedies, especially statutory damages and attorneys' fees for alleged infringement that began before registration.
 Under 17 U.S.C. § 412, statutory damages under § 504(c) are unavailable for infringement commenced before the effective date of registration, unless registration was made within three months of first publication. 
Jamendo also says it has applied to register the broader Jamendo Database and expects to amend the complaint later.


The market-harm argument under § 107(4) — the fourth fair-use factor — is the most litigation-ready aspect of Jamendo's theory. 
In the book cases, defendants argue that training is transformative and that the market for books is not the market for AI models; in Jamendo, the plaintiff can say its business already includes licensing music and datasets for AI and machine-learning uses, and Nvidia used the dataset for exactly the kind of use Jamendo licenses — making the market-harm argument more concrete.
 
The closest analogy may be the *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS* line, not because the facts are identical, but because both concern curated, commercially valuable data infrastructure used to build a product in a related market.

### Concord Music Group v. Anthropic — Cross-MSJ Watch

The music-publisher front against Anthropic remains at the cross-summary-judgment stage. Per the tracked-case ground truth, the last known public filing is Anthropic's cross-motion for summary judgment on fair use, filed April 22, 2026. The docket is not yet synced to a confirmed CourtListener ID for this advisory system, so the case number and current judge assignment are unverified in this run. What is confirmed: the core question — whether Anthropic's reproduction of song lyrics during Claude's conversational outputs constitutes fair use — presents a materially different § 107 analysis than Alsup's June 2025 training-use ruling in *Bartz*. Output-reproduction is not training-ingestion; the transformative-use argument weakens considerably when a model produces near-verbatim lyric text in response to a user prompt, and the market-substitution case under § 107(4) is correspondingly stronger for the publishers. 
Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO Music filed a $3.1 billion lawsuit against Anthropic on January 28, 2026, alleging Anthropic built Claude AI on a foundation of torrented piracy.
 The cross-MSJ briefing, once completed, will present the first post-*Bartz* judicial test of whether the fair-use framework that cleared training-ingestion extends to model outputs — a question with far broader licensing implications across the entire AI industry.

### The Parallel Docket: In re OpenAI Copyright MDL and Opt-Out Follow-On Cases


The complaint against OpenAI in the multi-author suit has been severed and joined with the Multi-District Litigation against OpenAI under docket 1:26-cv-03625; the complaint against Meta has also been severed and is now under docket 5:26-cv-03725.
 
On June 8, 2026, the Court severed Google, xAI, Perplexity, Apple, and Nvidia from that complaint.
 
Microsoft filed a motion to strike certain allegations from the consolidated class action complaint relating to arguments that Microsoft infringed when training its own models and when training new models developed after plaintiffs filed their original lawsuits; OpenAI filed a motion to dismiss "output claims."


The severed-defendant posture matters for litigation strategy. The June 8 severance order means Google, xAI, Perplexity, Apple, and Nvidia are now on separate tracks, each requiring independent pleading and service. Rights-holders or claims investors with cross-defendant exposure need to monitor each severed docket individually rather than relying on the MDL for consolidated status updates. 
There are dozens of other AI copyright suits at varying stages — including consolidated actions against OpenAI and Microsoft and active putative class actions against Meta, Apple, Salesforce, Adobe, and others — and several of those cases appear to be adopting the strategy that proved effective in *Bartz*: rather than challenging AI training as such, they focus on the underlying piracy and on infringing copies.


The fair-use framework also carries through to the Third Circuit. 
The Third Circuit's June 11, 2026 argument in *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS* was the first appellate consideration of whether training an AI on copyrighted works is fair use — making it the most consequential AI copyright hearing of 2026.
 No ruling has been issued as of this advisory. A Third Circuit decision affirming infringement would directly pressure the "transformative use" defense in every pending district court case; one affirming fair use would accelerate settlement pressure on plaintiffs with weaker market-harm records.

## Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims investors should treat the next two to four weeks as the decisive window for *Bartz* distribution positioning: verify that claims were submitted against all eligible ISBN/ASIN registrations before the March 30 claims deadline, confirm the publisher-author split reflects the actual publishing contract (deviating from the 50/50 default requires a contract submission), and flag the education-works Special Master process for any textbook claimants, where publisher-side allocations have reached as high as 88% per-work per objector testimony. 
Payment disbursement is expected to begin June 2026 or later, subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed
 — and any Ninth Circuit objector appeal would stay distributions, so monitoring the docket for a notice of appeal in the 30 days following final approval is essential. On the Jamendo-Nvidia front, rights-holders with curated AI-ready datasets subject to restricted Creative Commons licenses should evaluate whether their factual record mirrors Jamendo's § 107(4) market-harm theory: if the licensor was already selling machine-learning rights, the argument that a defendant's unauthorized training use substituted for a licensing transaction is substantially more concrete than in a general web-scraping case, and the *Thomson Reuters v. ROSS* analogy may survive a motion-to-dismiss challenge on that narrower theory. Finally, the June 8 severance of Google, xAI, Perplexity, Apple, and Nvidia from the Carreyrou-led multi-defendant complaint means five independent litigation tracks now require separate docket monitoring — claims investors with portfolio exposure across defendants should update their docket-watch subscriptions accordingly.
