### I. Bartz v. Anthropic (No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Hanging; Distribution Clock Ticking

Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín held the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and, following the 75-minute session, issued a post-hearing order requiring Anthropic to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages by May 21 addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and requiring plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections with a redline to the April 30 proposed order. Judge Martínez-Olguín took the matter under submission rather than ruling from the bench, and indicated that short orders might follow as soon as the next day. The final fairness hearing was heard before Judge Martínez-Olguín, who took the matter under submission; as of May 18, 2026, the final approval order had not yet been entered. The CourtListener docket for No. 3:24-cv-05417 reflects a last update of June 12, 2026, with the most recent filing dated June 4, 2026 — an order granting a motion to withdraw as attorney — and zero new entries in the 72-hour scan window as of this run.

The financial stakes of approval are large and unusually well-documented. Class counsel filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3% participation rate, vastly surpassing the 54% rate reported in the motion for final approval filed on March 19, 2026, eleven days before the claims deadline of March 30, 2026. Class counsel Justin Nelson represented that the payout per work in the class will be approximately $3,100. Authors' lawyers urged the court to grant final approval alongside an attorney fee request revised downward from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections. The original fee request had been $375 million — 25% of the $1.5 billion fund — with service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff ($150,000 total for the three class representatives), and settlement administration by JND Legal Administration expected to cost several million dollars additionally.

The legal architecture of the settlement demands clarity for future litigation strategy. The settlement halted a trial that might have tested the boundaries of copyright statutory damages; because statutory copyright damages can reach $150,000 per infringed work under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c), Anthropic's theoretical exposure extended into the hundreds of billions of dollars, raising existential risk for the company. The $1.5 billion settlement therefore establishes a per-work pricing benchmark of approximately $3,113/book — roughly 2.1% of theoretical maximum statutory exposure — giving plaintiffs' attorneys in other AI copyright cases a credible floor for settlement negotiations. Critically, Judge Alsup certified the class only for piracy, not for the act of AI training itself, meaning the fair-use ruling on training applies only with respect to the three named plaintiffs, not the certified class authors and publishers. Per the settlement schedule, JND was to calculate distributions by June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement expected to begin June 2026 or later — both subject to change depending on whether appeals are filed.

### II. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic (No. 5:26-cv-00880, N.D. Cal., Judge Van Keulen) — Piracy Playbook Escalates to $3.1 Billion

On January 28, 2026, Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord Music Group, and ABKCO Music filed an amended complaint against Anthropic claiming $3.1 billion in damages, advancing the theory that Anthropic acquired its music training data by torrenting — the same piracy theory that drove the Bartz settlement. The plaintiffs accuse Anthropic of downloading more than 20,000 protected compositions through BitTorrent and shadow libraries, and demand statutory damages that could surpass $3 billion. The case is assigned to Judge Susan G. Van Keulen in the Northern District of California (No. 5:26-cv-00880) and is tracked as active on the CourtListener docket, with an awaiting-sync flag as of this run.

Four organizations representing publishers and content creators — including the Association of American Publishers and the Authors Guild — filed an amicus brief supporting Concord, arguing that copyright law does not permit Anthropic to systematically copy human-authored works without permission and that the unauthorized training fails the fair use doctrine on at least two grounds: it is not transformative and it interferes with copyright holders who are signing licensing agreements with technology companies.

The procedural posture of the earlier music-publisher track is instructive: Following Anthropic's $1.5 billion settlement with authors, the music publishers attempted to add piracy claims to their preexisting lawsuit; however, the court denied the request, ruling that they had failed to investigate the piracy claims earlier and that adding them at that late stage would unfairly delay the case. The January 28 amended complaint in the consolidated 5:26-cv-00880 proceeding appears to represent the publishers' response to that denial — a fresh, re-engineered pleading built piracy-first rather than grafted onto an existing fair-use complaint. Norton Rose Fulbright's March 2026 analysis notes that the music publishers moved to amend their complaint specifically to add piracy claims similar to Bartz, after that settlement demonstrated the theory's financial potency: if training is fair use but torrenting is not, music publishers have a viable path to recovery independent of the fair-use debate.

### III. In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation (No. 1:25-md-03143-SHS-OTW, S.D.N.Y.) — Discovery Deep Dive; 20 Million Logs in Production

The OpenAI MDL in the Southern District of New York consolidates 16 separate suits covering music, books, news, and visual art. The docket (last updated June 12, 2026) is assigned to District Judge Sidney H. Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang handling discovery.

Judge Stein compelled OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymized ChatGPT logs. News plaintiffs had initially requested 120 million logs from tens of billions preserved; OpenAI countered with 20 million — 0.5% of its logs — arguing that was "surely more than enough," and plaintiffs agreed. OpenAI then changed course in October 2025, proposing to run keyword searches and produce only conversations implicating plaintiffs' specific works. Magistrate Judge Wang rejected that approach in November 2025. District Judge Stein affirmed Magistrate Judge Wang's ruling in full. Plaintiffs include The New York Times Company, the Chicago Tribune, and additional regional publishers in the consolidated action; OpenAI's defense includes Latham & Watkins, Keker Van Nest & Peters, and Morrison & Foerster.

The consolidated MDL complaint includes allegations from authors David Baldacci, Michael Chabon, and others that OpenAI "captured, downloaded, and copied copyrighted written works" without permission; OpenAI moved to strike certain model-specific allegations, arguing the consolidated complaint improperly introduced a new legal theory by separating download allegations from training-based claims. The court granted OpenAI partial relief, striking allegations about GPT-4V, GPT-4.5, GPT-5, and any "derivatives" or "successors," on the ground that an earlier May order confined the case to seven models (GPT-3 through GPT-4o Mini).

### IV. Kadrey v. Meta / In re Mosaic LLM Litigation — Torrenting Claims Live; Fair-Use Runway Ahead

In *Kadrey v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (N.D. Cal.), plaintiffs alleged that Meta infringed authors' copyrights when training its Llama LLM on their books; the court granted a partial motion to dismiss on fair-use grounds as to training the LLM, but claims related to alleged reproduction of pirated works during "seeding" in the torrenting process remain active in the Northern District of California. On June 25, 2025, Judge Vince Chhabria issued the summary judgment decision largely favoring Meta on the fair-use issue.

In *In re Mosaic LLM Litigation* (No. 3:24-cv-01451, N.D. Cal.), the court on January 20, 2026 granted plaintiffs' motion for leave to file a Second Amended Consolidated Complaint, which plaintiffs filed the next day; the SACC added a direct infringement claim regarding defendants' development of the DBRX models as well as claims for contributory infringement and inducement of infringement. Per the Copyright Alliance's January 2026 year-in-review analysis, *In re Mosaic LLM Litigation*, along with *In re Google Generative AI* and *Concord v. Anthropic*, is among the next cases expected to produce fair-use decisions — but those decisions are not likely to begin emerging until summer 2026 at the earliest.

### V. SCOTUS and the AI-Authorship Bookend

The Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* on March 2, 2026, confirming the lower courts' ruling that AI-generated works without human authorship are not eligible for copyright protection under U.S. law. The denial forecloses any near-term route for AI developers to assert copyright in model outputs — reinforcing the asymmetry in the current litigation posture: LLM training inputs may warrant fair-use protection under *Bartz* and *Kadrey*, but the outputs themselves generate no copyright accruing to developers, leaving the rights-holder monetization question unresolved by any existing IP framework.
