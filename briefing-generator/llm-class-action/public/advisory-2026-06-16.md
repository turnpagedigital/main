### I. *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Settlement Mechanics, Distribution Timeline, and the Opt-Out Satellite Litigation

After a 75-minute fairness hearing held May 14, 2026, in a San Francisco courtroom, presiding Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín declined to approve the $1.5 billion settlement from the bench; however, given the tone of the hearing, most observers expect the judge will give final approval relatively quickly.

The settlement fund total — $1.5 billion to be paid by Anthropic — represents the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history. The key procedural posture as of today: the judge ordered supplemental briefing by May 21, 2026, including a filing from Anthropic of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honored, and required plaintiffs to file a revised proposed order substantively addressing objections, with a redline to the proposed order filed April 30, 2026 (Dkt. 646-1).

Class participation metrics are extraordinary by any measure. Class counsel filed papers reporting that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a 91.3 percent rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10 percent). At the May 14 hearing itself, that figure was updated: the claims rate was confirmed as 92.77% (447,576 of 482,460 works), with roughly 53 objections logged. Class counsel Justin Nelson stated the payout per work in the class will be $3,100. The high rate of claims means that forecasts of a bigger payout based on the March 19 report of 54 percent participation will not be realized.

On attorneys' fees, class counsel urged final approval alongside an attorneys' fee request revised down from $300 million to $187.5 million, arguing the deal is fair despite multiple objections. The settlement fund must also cover administrative costs (settlement administration by JND) and service awards of $50,000 per named plaintiff (totaling $150,000 for the three class representatives).

The distribution schedule tracks closely to the current advisory window: the settlement administrator was scheduled to calculate distributions on June 11, 2026, with payment disbursement projected to begin June 2026 or later, subject to change. Payouts to class members will only be issued after the judge grants final approval and any subsequent appeals are fully resolved.

Rights-holders should note a consequential satellite development. Twenty-eight authors and rights-holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. (No. 3:26-cv-04482), demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works. A follow-on suit, *Kwon v. Anthropic*, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors. Statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) can reach $150,000 per work for willful infringement; across multiple registered works per opt-out plaintiff, aggregate exposure could dwarf the per-work settlement figure.

The liability architecture underlying the settlement bears repeating for practitioners advising downstream defendants. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup held at summary judgment that Anthropic's use of copyrighted books to train Claude was "spectacularly" transformative fair use, but refused to grant summary judgment as to copies obtained from "pirate" websites and maintained in a permanent, general-purpose library. The settlement thus resolves the piracy channel exclusively — not AI training as such — a distinction that shapes every subsequent filing in this space.

### II. *Elsevier Inc. et al. v. Meta Platforms, Inc. et al.*, No. 1:26-cv-03689 (S.D.N.Y.) — The Post-*Bartz* Piracy-Theory Wave Crests

The most significant new case in the LLM copyright landscape is now fully docketed. On May 5, 2026, five major publishing houses — Elsevier Inc., Cengage Learning, Inc., Hachette Book Group, Inc., Macmillan Publishing Group LLC, and McGraw Hill LLC — along with bestselling author Scott Turow, filed a putative class action against Meta Platforms Inc. and its CEO, Mark Zuckerberg, for willful infringement of millions of textual works, including literature, educational works, and scholarly articles, to develop Meta's Llama large language models.

The case was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York as Case No. 1:26-cv-03689. The complaint advances two principal theories of liability: willful copyright infringement, alleging that Meta committed willful infringement by torrenting millions of copyrighted books and journal articles from pirate sites to train its Llama AI LLMs. The second theory is CMI violations: plaintiffs claim that Meta violated copyright law by removing copyright management information (CMI) from the works to conceal their origins. The CMI theory invokes 17 U.S.C. § 1202(b), which carries statutory damages of $2,500–$25,000 per violation — potentially multiplied across hundreds of thousands of works.

In their complaint, the plaintiffs argue Meta knowingly copied copyrighted materials from notorious pirate websites such as LibGen and Anna's Archive to train various iterations of its Llama language model — with Zuckerberg's personal authorization to do so. The personal-liability allegation against Zuckerberg is tactically significant: it targets corporate-veil protections and is designed to establish individual scienter for the willfulness finding that unlocks maximum statutory damages under § 504(c)(2).

The Elsevier litigation is explicitly shaped around issues that proved dispositive in recent decisions favoring AI developers: in *Bartz v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal. June 2025), Judge Alsup held that using lawfully acquired books to train an LLM was "spectacularly" transformative and qualified as fair use, but the fair use defense did not apply to retention of pirated copies sourced from shadow libraries. By focusing exclusively on pirated-source training data — and by alleging more than 267 terabytes of copyrighted material was ingested from pirate sites — plaintiffs have deliberately designed the complaint to survive the Alsup fair-use holding that would otherwise doom claims based on lawfully sourced works.

Practitioners should note that the Kadrey v. Meta line of litigation (N.D. Cal.) presents a parallel risk vector for Meta. Similar to Bartz, plaintiffs in Kadrey sued Meta alleging copyright infringement in training its Llama LLM; the court granted a partial motion to dismiss on fair use grounds as to the LLM training itself, but claims related to alleged reproduction of pirated works during "seeding" in the torrenting process remain active in the Northern District of California. Meta now faces both the surviving Kadrey piracy claims in N.D. Cal. and the new Elsevier class action in S.D.N.Y. — a two-front litigation exposure with compounding reputational and settlement leverage.

### III. *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 5:26-cv-00880 (N.D. Cal.) — Lyrics-Reproduction Theory, Assigned to Mag. Judge Van Keulen

Anthropic faces a copyright lawsuit filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California by major music publishers, including Universal Music Group, Concord, and ABKCO, alleging the company used thousands of protected songs without permission to train its AI models. The lawsuit states that collections of allegedly pirated books used in the training process contained copyrighted sheet music and lyrics associated with at least 714 songs controlled by the publishers; plaintiffs argue the reproduction and use of these materials violated their exclusive rights under U.S. copyright law. Legal representation for the publishers includes attorneys from Oppenheim + Zebrak, Coblentz Patch Duffy & Bass, and Cowan, Liebowitz & Latman.

The case caption in the tracked-case ground truth reflects a January 2026 re-filing (No. 5:26-cv-00880), assigned to Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen. The docket shows no new activity in the past 72 hours, consistent with its early-proceedings posture. This matter is distinct from the earlier Middle District of Tennessee action by UMG and Concord (filed October 18, 2023) that concerned live lyric generation by Claude — a vicarious-reproduction claim rather than a training-data claim. The newly filed lawsuit follows and expands upon that separate case initiated by the same group of music publishers against Anthropic in 2023, which also centered on allegations that the company used their copyrighted material to train its Claude LLM. Rights-holders in music publishing should monitor whether the N.D. Cal. case is coordinated with, or severed from, the older Tennessee proceeding, as the choice of forum and theory will determine whether the *Bartz* fair-use ruling (authored by Judge Alsup in the same district) supplies a direct fair-use defense.

### IV. *In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation* — SDNY MDL Discovery Obligations and Output-Log Production

In April 2025, the U.S. Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation consolidated twelve cases against OpenAI originating in S.D.N.Y. and N.D. Cal. The MDL encompasses class actions filed by authors, New York suits by news organizations, DMCA-focused suits, and a case brought by an online video creator; the panel found that these cases involve common questions of fact regarding defendants' use of copyrighted works without consent to train LLMs. The consolidated matter was assigned to the Southern District of New York.

A significant discovery ruling shapes the current posture: on January 5, 2026, the court ordered OpenAI to produce all 20 million output logs, noting that "OpenAI identifies no caselaw requiring a court" to excuse that production. That order — producing the largest-ever compelled disclosure of generative AI output data — is now being executed, and the contents of those logs represent the evidentiary linchpin for plaintiffs seeking to demonstrate that ChatGPT outputs constitute substantially similar copies of ingested training works. Claims in the SDNY MDL (which includes *Alter et al. v. OpenAI*, No. 1:23-cv-10211) have survived motions to dismiss, with the court holding that "it is sufficient to defeat OpenAI's motion to dismiss that plaintiffs have alleged some outputs that a reasonable jury could find are substantially similar to plaintiffs' works."

The MDL's discovery schedule is now running in earnest. Summary judgment briefing in the Kadrey-adjacent Chhabria-supervised cases was set to conclude on approximately April 2, 2026; rulings, if any, would represent the next major inflection point after *Bartz* and *Kadrey* at the summary judgment phase.

### V. Supreme Court Human-Authorship Requirement and the *Thaler v. Perlmutter* Closure

For completeness: the constitutional/statutory backdrop governing AI-generated copyright claims was settled on March 2, 2026. On that date, the Supreme Court denied certiorari of Dr. Stephen Thaler's appeal, confirming the principle that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable. The cert. denial forecloses any near-term avenue for AI developers to seek affirmative copyright protection in their own LLM-generated outputs — a consideration that cuts against AI companies arguing that model outputs independently qualify for protection separate from the underlying training data question.
