### *Bartz v. Anthropic* — Distribution Phase Underway; August 21 Non-Filing Claimant Deadline Approaches

**Case:** *Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 3:24-cv-05417-AMO (N.D. Cal.) | **Judge:** Hon. Araceli Martínez-Olguín | **Status:** Final judgment entered July 20, 2026


On July 20, 2026, Judge Martínez-Olguín granted final approval of the $1.5 billion class action settlement and entered final judgment.
 
The court issued a 23-page order granting final approval, resolving several outstanding administrative details, and ruling on attorneys' fees.


The settlement's mechanics are now operative. 
Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion plus interest to a class of copyright owners whose works were torrented from Library Genesis (LibGen) and Pirate Library Mirror (PiLiMi); the certified class encompasses all beneficial or legal copyright owners of books on a Works List containing 482,460 works, each possessing an ISBN or ASIN and a U.S. Copyright Office registration within five years of first publication.
 
The court confirmed that the estimated per-work payment of approximately $3,000 is four times the statutory minimum for willful copyright infringement under 17 U.S.C. § 504, and noted that the 91.3% claims rate — as of April 16, 2026 — was "magnitudes above the average for class action cases."


**Fee award.** 
Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then trimmed to $187.5 million; the court approved approximately $101.5 million in fees.
 
By the court's accounting, that works out to approximately $2,953 per hour across 34,381.6 hours of credited attorney time.


**Objections.** 
The court overruled all 53 objections filed opposing the settlement.
 
The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but noted those individuals remain free to bring their own claims.
 
The order devoted substantial attention to the 54 objections and comments filed, and despite class counsel's arguments that many objections were procedurally improper, the court declined to overrule any docketed objection on procedural grounds, addressing each category on the merits.


**Scope of release — what survives.** This is the operative point for rights-holder counsel advising on residual claims. 
The settlement grants a past-only release for conduct up to August 25, 2025, and explicitly does not release claims related to AI outputs or future conduct.
 
The settlement resolves only claims about Anthropic's past conduct in acquiring and copying pirated books; it sets no ceiling on what authors can pursue going forward — against Anthropic for any future conduct or output-based claims, or in the many other pending cases against AI companies.


**Payment schedule — next milestone August 21.** The IPG's post-final-approval timeline, drafted against the actual July 20 order date, shows the following verified schedule:

- **August 21, 2026:** Settlement Administrator sends additional checks to non-filing claimants (Plan of Allocation ¶ 3(c)(ii)(5), 120 days after Final Approval Order). 
Per the Plan of Allocation, 120 days after issuance of the Final Approval Order, the Settlement Administrator sends an additional check to non-filing claimants; Anthropic then pays the first $450 million scheduled payment on September 25, 2026 (12 months after preliminary approval), followed by a second wave of checks on October 9, 2026.

- **September 25, 2027:** Second $450 million installment (24 months after preliminary approval).

No announcement of first-wave distribution commencement has issued as of this writing. 
The Authors Guild confirmed it looks forward to the distribution process commencing so authors can begin receiving payment, though a specific payout date had not been announced as of the final approval order date.


**Opt-out litigation tail.** 
The *Chicken Soup for the Soul* and Carreyrou suits are already separate individual actions, and the court's final approval order may prompt other non-class members to file.
 
The court reported 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works — approximately 0.03% of total works on the list.
 Rights-holder counsel with opt-out clients should note that the output-claims carve-out and the post-August 25, 2025 conduct carve-out give those plaintiffs meaningful surviving theories under 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) market-harm analysis, particularly given the absence of any licensing structure in the settlement.

### *Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC* — Last Activity July 29; Discovery Running

**Case:** No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL (N.D. Cal.) | **Judge:** Hon. Eumi K. Lee | **Referred to:** Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen | **Status:** Active

Per the ground truth docket (last filing July 29, 2026; no new filings in the 72-hour scan), the case is in active litigation with no new public docket entries today. The substantive posture: 
several large music publishers — Concord, Capitol CMG, Universal, and ABKCO — brought copyright infringement claims against Anthropic, alleging it infringed their rights by using song lyrics catalogues to train its Claude AI product.
 
On March 25, 2025, Judge Lee denied the plaintiffs' application for a preliminary injunction restraining Anthropic's use of the lyrics in AI training.


The case has since moved deeper into discovery. A notable prior skirmish worth tracking for evidentiary posture: 
in a 2025 discovery ruling, the court denied without prejudice Anthropic's request to compel production of publishers' prompts, outputs, and settings, and instead ordered production of a sample of 5 million prompt-output pairs.
 That order shapes the evidentiary record on the § 107(4) market-harm factor — the extent to which Claude's lyric outputs substitute for licensed usage. 
A separate incident involved Anthropic's attestation that a "manual citation check" had been performed but failed to catch what appeared to be an AI-hallucinated citation; the court noted it was not clear how such an error — including a complete change in article title — could have escaped correction during a manual cite-check by a human being.
 The court struck that declaration in part.

### Broader Docket — Pending Inflection Points (No New Filings Today)

Three matters across the system carry near-term ruling risk that this desk will lead with when they break:

**Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence — Third Circuit.** 
Three U.S. judges have now ruled on AI training and fair use at the district level — Alsup (*Bartz*) and Chhabria (*Kadrey*) found for AI companies on training; Judge Bibas (*Thomson Reuters v. Ross*) found against. The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling on AI copyright — argued June 11, 2026 — remains pending.
 That ruling is the most consequential near-term development in the system; a circuit-level pronouncement on the § 107 fair-use framework for training-data ingestion will immediately affect valuation of residual claims left open by the *Bartz* release.

**NYT v. OpenAI / In re OpenAI Copyright MDL, No. 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.), Judge Sidney H. Stein.** 
The court denied OpenAI's motion to dismiss, rejecting OpenAI's argument that AI training is inherently transformative, and the case is in discovery as of April 2026, with trial expected in late 2026 or early 2027.
 
In June 2026, the NYT amended its complaint to allege Microsoft had built a dedicated supercomputer infrastructure specifically to help OpenAI use Times content without authorization — broadening the theory of liability.


**Disney/Universal v. Midjourney (C.D. Cal.).** 
In the Rule 26(f) report, Disney requested a non-expert discovery cut-off of August 17, 2026 with all motions filed by December 21, 2026; Midjourney did not agree and requested dates approximately three months later.
 That scheduling dispute is unresolved. The August 17 date — two weeks out — may produce a court ruling or stipulated extension worth tracking.

**Getty v. Stability AI (D. Del. / UK High Court).** 
As of the Getty-OpenAI licensing deal announced in June 2026, Getty's lawsuit against Stability AI remains active — never settled, never concluded — even as Getty now licenses images to OpenAI under a separate commercial arrangement.
 The divergence between licensing posture and litigation posture is directly relevant as § 107(4) market-harm evidence: the existence of a functioning licensing market cuts against a fair-use defense.
