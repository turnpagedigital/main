### I. NYT v. OpenAI / Microsoft — Sanctions Motion (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195)


A group of newspapers including the New York Times and New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan on Thursday to sanction OpenAI in their high-stakes copyright dispute for allegedly lying to the court about its ability to search its systems for proof that it misused millions of their articles in AI training.
 The filing, styled as a sanctions motion under the court's inherent authority and Fed. R. Civ. P. 37, goes well beyond the ordinary discovery-dispute posture that characterized the log-production skirmish resolved by Judge Stein on January 5, 2026.

The factual predicate is substantial. 
According to the filing, OpenAI data privacy engineer Vinnie Monaco testified in a court-ordered deposition this April that the company had "performed multiple searches for News Plaintiffs' content" — including searches conducted before any of the news organizations had even filed their lawsuits.
 That admission directly contradicts the position OpenAI maintained throughout discovery: 
throughout the case, OpenAI argued that it lacked the ability to search its own training corpus, and also argued that searching or producing its massive collection of ChatGPT conversations would be technically burdensome and would raise user-privacy concerns because the logs would need to be retrieved, processed, and de-identified.


The deposition produced two additional disclosures that compound the problem for OpenAI. 
Monaco's deposition allegedly revealed that, beginning before the NYT filed its lawsuit, OpenAI had already amassed a database of about 78 million de-identified ChatGPT conversations that it was using internally to determine how much it was infringing on others' works.
 On top of that, 
the deposition also reportedly disclosed the existence of "Project Giraffe," a set of internal tools that included a so-called "Bloom" filter designed to detect and log instances of content regurgitation in ChatGPT outputs — a system allegedly implemented shortly after the lawsuit was filed.


The relief requested is structured to punish both the misconduct and the evidentiary prejudice it caused. 
The plaintiffs are asking the court to prevent OpenAI from using the 20 million chat log sample as evidence, claiming it is unreliable; to accept as fact that ChatGPT logs would have shown major regurgitation and grounding of the plaintiffs' content; to prevent OpenAI from arguing that its provided chat logs don't demonstrate substantial regurgitation; and to make OpenAI pay legal fees for having to chase down this evidence.
 The deletion allegation is particularly pointed: 
the newspapers told the court in a filing that OpenAI falsely told the court it could not search its large language models for their copyrighted material while hiding that it had done so "even before the first News Plaintiff filed suit," and that OpenAI had also deleted billions of relevant ChatGPT conversations or made them unsearchable.


OpenAI's response, in a statement from spokesperson Drew Pusateri, framed the motion as a litigation tactic rather than a legitimate discovery dispute. 
OpenAI said: "As the Times' case weakens and they've been forced to drop claims against us, they're persisting with their efforts to invade the privacy of people who have nothing to do with this case, including by making these blatantly false allegations."


**Why this matters for the docket broadly.** The 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) market-harm factor is where the NYT's case differs structurally from the *Bartz* book-piracy track: the press-publisher theory turns on whether ChatGPT outputs reproduce journalism at scale in a way that substitutes for the original publication. 
The case has already produced significant rulings on motions to dismiss, a contentious battle over ChatGPT user logs, and a preservation order that briefly required OpenAI to retain billions of user conversations.
 If the court grants even the lesser sanctions — accepting as established fact that the logs would have shown substantial regurgitation, and barring OpenAI from contesting that inference — OpenAI's § 107 fair-use defense becomes substantially harder to advance at summary judgment. The log-production battle that Magistrate Judge Wang resolved in November 2025, and that Judge Stein affirmed January 5, 2026, was predicated on OpenAI's representation that the corpus was difficult to search. The Monaco deposition undermines that representation at its foundation.

The § 504 statutory-damages exposure here is not bounded the way it was in *Bartz*. 
Damages exposure is existential. Statutory damages for willful infringement run up to $150,000 per work. Millions of articles multiplied by that ceiling is not a rounding error, even for OpenAI and Microsoft.
 And it is worth flagging the divergence: 
Judge Stein's ruling allows The Times to continue pursuing claims that OpenAI's outputs included copyrighted content and resulted in user infringement, which diverges from the stance of California judges who have dismissed similar allegations.
 That circuit-level split will matter if either side seeks an interlocutory certification or if a merits ruling reaches the Second Circuit.

### II. Bartz v. Anthropic — Settlement Administration: Fee Dispute and Disbursement Timeline (No. 3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.)

The May 14 fairness hearing before Judge Martínez-Olguín did not produce a final approval order; the matter remains pending. The active motion before the court is a fee allocation dispute that cuts directly into what class members will receive net of deductions.

The headline claim figures: 
in their March 19 motion, class counsel requested 12.5 percent of the settlement fund ($187,500,000.00) for attorneys' fees plus $2,779,950.26 for the reimbursement of litigation expenses.
 That is already a reduction — class counsel had initially asked for $300 million, or 20 percent, before revising down to the current ask. The separate controversy involves three firms — Edelson PC, Oppenheim + Zebrak, and Cowan DeBaets, Abrahams & Sheppard LLP — that sought an additional $75 million as "nonclass coordination counsel." 
Anthropic urged a federal judge to deny the $75 million in attorneys' fees sought by non-class counsel, arguing there is "scant justification or substantiation" for the "extraordinary" fees the three firms are seeking.
 
Anthropic alleged the add-on firms are requesting awards ranging from 15 to 95 times the fees they claim to have incurred and called their claimed hours "excessive."


The claims-rate picture is now settled and unfavorable for per-work recovery. 
Class counsel filed papers apprising the court that 440,490 of the 482,460 eligible works had been claimed — a remarkable 91.3 percent rate (the typical class action claim rate is around 10 percent).
 
The high rate of claim means that the forecasts about a bigger payout based on the March 19 report of 54 percent will not happen, and the actual payout will likely end up being closer to the original $3,000 per work figure.
 After deduction of the $187.5 million fee request, the $2.78 million expense reimbursement, the $18.22 million cost reserve, and the $150,000 in service awards, distributable principal per title will be materially below the headline $3,000. 
Anthropic is paying into the $1.5 billion settlement fund over time, so not all of the funds will be dispersed at once. The $1.5 billion minimum settlement is being funded by Anthropic in multiple installments — the first $300 million has already been deposited in an interest-bearing escrow account.


The structural limits of the settlement warrant a standing reminder for rights-holders watching this as a licensing benchmark. 
This settlement only releases Anthropic from liability for past conduct — specifically, its acquisition, retention, and use of the identified pirated works before August 25, 2025. It does not create any ongoing licensing scheme for future AI training, cover claims based on AI outputs that might infringe copyrights, affect Anthropic's ability to train on lawfully acquired materials, or set precedent for how AI training should be regulated globally.
 That narrow scope distinguishes it from a forward-looking licensing framework and is why the *Bartz* settlement figure, often cited by commentators as a floor for valuing press-publisher or music-publisher claims, does not straightforwardly transfer: those tracks involve ongoing reproduction of outputs, not one-time pirated-acquisition liability.

On the underlying jurisprudence: Judge Alsup's June 2025 ruling — that 
Anthropic's use of legally acquired books for AI training was "quintessentially transformative" and protected as fair use — but simultaneously holding that downloading and keeping pirated copies was not fair use
 — remains the primary fair-use data point for defense counsel in AI-training cases. The assignment of the case to Judge Martínez-Olguín following Judge Alsup's inactive status means the court inherits an unsettled fee docket and any post-approval appellate risk.

### III. Parallel Docket Watch — Concord Music Group v. Anthropic; In re OpenAI MDL No. 3143

**Concord / UMG v. Anthropic** (M.D. Tenn. → N.D. Cal., cross-MSJ pending, awaiting first docket sync): No new public filings have surfaced in the 72-hour scan. The cross-motions for summary judgment — including Anthropic's fair-use cross-motion filed April 22, 2026 — remain pending. The music-publisher track presents a fair-use analysis that diverges from the book-piracy claim in *Bartz* because the gravamen is lyrics reproduction in outputs, not shadow-library acquisition. The § 107(4) market-harm calculus for music is more adverse to defendants than for books: a functioning licensing market demonstrably exists (ASCAP/BMI, direct sync deals), and courts have consistently found substantial similarity in verbatim lyric reproduction at the phrase level. There is nothing further to report on this matter today beyond what appeared in yesterday's advisory.

**In re OpenAI Copyright MDL, No. 3143 (N.D. Cal.)**: No new filings surfaced in today's 72-hour scan. The MDL consolidation of author and publisher claims against OpenAI continues to develop against the backdrop of the NYT sanctions motion, which is proceeding in a separate court but under substantially overlapping discovery facts. Coordination counsel watching the MDL should note that the Monaco deposition — which underlies the NYT sanctions motion — occurred in a different case but involved OpenAI's institutional capability to search its corpus, a question that is live in the MDL as well.

### IV. Systemic Read-Across: What the NYT Sanctions Motion Changes

The Monaco deposition, if credited, transforms the AI-copyright discovery landscape in three ways. First, it forecloses the "technical impossibility" defense that has been OpenAI's principal basis for resisting corpus-search discovery in every pending matter. Courts in the MDL, in *Authors Guild v. OpenAI*, and in *Kadrey v. Meta* analogues may now view similar technical-burden representations with heightened skepticism. Second, the "Project Giraffe" / Bloom filter disclosure suggests that OpenAI had contemporaneous awareness of and tooling to measure regurgitation — which bears on the § 504 willfulness finding that determines whether statutory damages run at $750 or $150,000 per work. Third, the allegation that 
OpenAI deleted billions of ChatGPT outputs after the lawsuit was filed, potentially violating a court preservation order, and substituted millions of logs in the requested sample
 implicates spoliation doctrine independently of the § 107 merits. A spoliation adverse-inference instruction — the softer version of what the plaintiffs are requesting — would be available even if the court declines to impose terminating sanctions or fee shifting.


The Times has already spent more than $28 million on fighting AI companies in court, according to filings with financial regulators that disclose its litigation costs.
 That number, paired with the institutional commitment signaled by the sanctions motion, signals that the NYT is not positioning for a quiet settlement at a number reflecting OpenAI's current litigation exposure. It is building a record that increases that exposure.
