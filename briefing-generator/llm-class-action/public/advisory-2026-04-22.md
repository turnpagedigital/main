# LLM CLASS ACTION ADVISORY | April 22, 2026

## Analysis & Developments

The financial architecture of the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement has come into sharper focus twenty-two days before the May 14 fairness hearing, with Publishers Weekly reporting on April 20 that class counsel's filings now project a net settlement fund of approximately $1.29 billion after deducting roughly $208.6 million in attorneys' fees, settlement administration costs, and the nationwide notice program — yielding an estimated base payout of approximately $2,931 per work across the 440,490 titles claimed by the March 30 deadline. ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/digital/copyright/article/100208-anthropic-settlement-hearing-comes-into-focus.html)) The $2,931 figure sits modestly below the $3,000 estimate that class counsel originally projected, a gap attributable to administration costs that have exceeded initial projections, though the figure remains subject to adjustment depending on how Judge Martínez-Olguín resolves the publisher-versus-author allocation formula and whether any cy pres distribution is ordered for residual unclaimed funds. Anthropic's second installment of $300 million is due in eight days on April 30, which will bring total funded commitments to $600 million of the $1.5 billion fund — a payment milestone that, while administrative in nature, will test Anthropic's liquidity commitments and provide the court with concrete evidence of the defendant's capacity to fund the settlement at the Rule 23(e)(2) fairness hearing. The 91.3% claim rate continues to serve as the settlement's strongest adequacy metric: in the landscape of class action litigation, where claim rates of 5–15% are typical, near-universal participation by eligible claimants is a powerful signal under the third Rule 23(e)(2)(C) consideration that the relief is adequate in relation to the costs, risks, and delay of continued litigation. ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/04/14/bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-update-new-date-and-time-for-the-fairness-hearing-you-can-join-online-and-unsealed-objections/))

The Concord Music Group v. Anthropic litigation has entered a pivotal phase as Anthropic filed its opposition to the publishers' partial summary judgment motion, arguing that AI training constitutes transformative fair use and that Claude's outputs do not function as market substitutes for licensed lyrics services such as LyricFind and Musixmatch — a position that, if rejected, would establish that commercial AI reproduction of copyrighted works for output generation is per se infringement, collapsing both the first and fourth fair-use factors under 17 U.S.C. § 107. ([Reuters via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com,2026:newsml_L6N41418J:0-anthropic-seeks-pivotal-court-win-in-music-publisher-lawsuit-over-ai-training/)) The cross-industry amicus record now spans music, book, news, and academic publishing: the RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, and SoundExchange filed on March 30, and the AAP, News/Media Alliance, STM, and Authors Guild filed a separate brief supporting the publishers' motion, together arguing that a functioning licensing market already exists and that Anthropic's unlicensed copying undermines it at scale. ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/riaa-nmpa-and-more-file-amicus-brief-backing-music-publishers-against-anthropic-arguing-ai-companys-unlicensed-copying-is-inexcusable/)) ([AAP](https://publishers.org/news/book-news-and-journal-publishers-join-with-authors-in-amicus-brief-in-support-of-music-publishers-in-concord-v-anthropic/)) The parallel $3.1 billion Concord/UMG v. Anthropic II action covering 20,000+ songs, combined with the Carreyrou opt-out action seeking $150,000 per title per defendant, underscores the escalating multi-front litigation pressure facing AI developers who trained on copyrighted content without licenses. The PSJ ruling, expected before the summer 2026 trial window, will be the first federal court determination on whether AI training constitutes fair use in the music context — a decision with immediate implications for UMG v. Suno (Judge Saylor, D. Mass.) and the broader docket.

The legislative landscape has sharpened with two competing frameworks now on the table. Senator Marsha Blackburn's March 18 discussion draft of the "Trump America AI Act" — a 291-page omnibus bill — includes a provision in Title XV that would amend the Copyright Act to declare that unauthorized reproduction of copyrighted works for AI training "does not constitute fair use," effectively overriding pending judicial determinations and the Copyright Office's more measured position that "some uses of copyrighted works for generative AI training will qualify as fair use, and some will not." ([Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/03/ai-legislation-copyright-hollywood-trump-1236759660/)) ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/trump-administration-takes-major-steps-toward-comprehensive-federal-ai-regulation)) This clashes directly with the White House's March 20 National AI Policy Framework, which favors letting courts resolve fair-use questions case by case while encouraging collective licensing mechanisms — a tension that will shape any eventual legislative consensus. The bipartisan CLEAR Act (S. 3813), introduced by Senators Schiff and Curtis, takes a narrower approach focused solely on transparency: requiring AI companies to file detailed disclosures with the Copyright Office enumerating every copyrighted work in training datasets, backed by $5,000 per-work civil penalties for noncompliance. ([IP Watchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/02/11/clear-act-establish-notice-requirements-copyrighted-works-ai-training-data/)) Neither bill has advanced to markup, but together they bracket the range of congressional responses — from full fair-use abrogation to disclosure-only mandates — that will frame the regulatory environment for AI developers through the remainder of the 119th Congress.

Internationally, the Getty Images v. Stability AI appeal remains the most consequential pending matter, with the UK Court of Appeal set to consider whether AI model weights can constitute "infringing copies" under CDPA § 27 even absent storage of actual reproductions of copyrighted works — a question the trial judge, Mrs Justice Joanna Smith, acknowledged "has a real prospect of success" and "concerns a pure question of law on which the minds of reasonable lawyers may differ." ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/next-steps-for-getty-v-stability-why-has-permission-to-appeal-been-granted)) ([Chambers](https://chambers.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-remain-in-focus-for-2026-with-getty-appeal-given-the-green-light)) A reversal would create secondary copyright liability for any organization importing foreign-trained AI models into the UK, with immediate implications for cross-border AI deployment strategies and the EU AI Act's Article 4 TDM framework. The CJEU's Like Company v. Google referral, which held its first hearing on March 10, 2026, remains the first occasion the Court of Justice has directly addressed generative AI and copyright under EU law, and its advisory opinion could reshape the DSM Directive's text and data mining exception across all 27 member states. The Kluwer Copyright Blog's newly published analysis of the Bartz settlement offers comparative perspective, noting that it dwarfs all prior copyright settlements and establishes a pricing benchmark of roughly $3,000 per work that will inevitably influence negotiation dynamics in parallel dockets and voluntary licensing arrangements worldwide. ([Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-understanding-americas-largest-copyright-settlement/))

## Recommended Actions

Class members who submitted Bartz claims by the March 30 deadline should verify their submission confirmations through the settlement portal at anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com and note that the projected base payout of $2,931 per work reflects the net fund calculation after $208.6 million in deductions — a figure that could shift depending on the court's resolution of objections at the May 14 hearing, particularly regarding the publisher-versus-author allocation formula and whether Professor Bishop's challenge to per-title valuation under the $150,000 statutory ceiling prompts supplemental briefing. Rights-holders monitoring the Concord v. Anthropic PSJ motion should study Anthropic's opposition brief carefully, as its articulation of the transformative-use defense will preview the arguments AI developers deploy across all pending dockets; publishers and songwriters with works in the 499-song catalog should prepare for the possibility that a ruling against fair use on partial summary judgment would accelerate settlement negotiations in the $3.1 billion Concord/UMG v. Anthropic II action. AI developers subject to the proposed CLEAR Act's retroactive disclosure mandate should accelerate training-data provenance audits immediately, as Senator Blackburn's competing proposal to legislatively abrogate fair use for AI training signals that even the floor of congressional intervention will impose significant compliance obligations — early preparation positions companies favorably whether the eventual framework requires disclosure only or full licensing. Practitioners monitoring cross-border exposure should track the Getty v. Stability AI appeal timeline closely and prepare contingency assessments for the scenario in which model weights are deemed "infringing copies" under UK law, as that ruling would require urgent reassessment of AI model import and deployment strategies across all jurisdictions recognizing secondary copyright liability.
