Bartz fairness hearing held; first appellate AI fair-use argument lands June 11.

AI Copyright Desk10:00 AM ETWednesday, August 5, 2026

Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC — No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL (N.D. Cal.) — Concord I: Summary Judgment Pending

No new filings confirmed in the last 24 hours on this docket; last CourtListener sync was July 29, 2026. The matter is in the post-briefing interval before the October hearing.

The summary judgment hearing before Judge Eumi K. Lee is scheduled for October 21, 2026. The questions before the court are whether partial summary judgment on copyright infringement should be granted to plaintiffs, and whether summary judgment on fair use and non-infringement should be granted to Anthropic.

The fair-use frame in Concord I is materially narrower than what Judge Alsup resolved in Bartz. Plaintiffs' March 23, 2026 motion for partial summary judgment asks Judge Lee to rule that Anthropic's unlicensed copying of copyrighted song lyrics to train its Claude AI chatbot is not fair use.

The RIAA amicus brief, filed March 30, zeroes in on the fourth fair-use factor — market harm — arguing that AI-generated music already acts as a direct market substitute for human-created works, and that a functioning licensing market for AI training already exists — one that Anthropic has chosen not to participate in. The § 107(4) market-harm argument is the strongest vector for plaintiffs here; the Bartz precedent on pirated-source infringement is not directly transferable because Concord I centers on whether ingestion of lyrics — rather than wholesale downloading of pirated books — is itself transformative.

Concord II parallel. A separate, larger action — Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC, No. 5:26-cv-00880, N.D. Cal., filed January 28, 2026 — names Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann as individual defendants.

Anthropic moved to stay Concord II on the ground that Concord I has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — that could have preclusive effect in Concord II. That stay motion was still active as of the last confirmed filing; the October 21 ruling in Concord I is therefore the pivot point for both cases simultaneously.

---

Parallel Docket — Appellate and Structural Signals

Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence — Third Circuit. The Third Circuit's first appellate ruling on AI training and fair use is pending after oral argument on June 11, 2026. Three U.S. district judges have now ruled on AI training and fair use: Alsup (Bartz) and Chhabria (Kadrey) found for AI companies on training; Bibas (Thomson Reuters v. Ross) found against. The Third Circuit's ruling is the first appellate resolution of the question. The outcome matters directly to Concord I: if the Third Circuit affirms Bibas's rejection of fair use on the § 107(4) market-harm ground, it strengthens the RIAA's amicus framing and gives Judge Lee doctrinal cover to rule against Anthropic on the same factor.

NYT v. OpenAI — Sanctions posture. A group of newspapers including the New York Times and New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan to sanction OpenAI in their high-stakes copyright dispute for allegedly lying to the court about its ability to search its systems for proof that it misused millions of their articles in AI training.

In June 2026, the NYT amended its complaint to allege Microsoft had built a dedicated supercomputer infrastructure specifically to help OpenAI use Times content without authorization — broadening the theory of liability. The sanctions motion and the amended complaint together suggest the NYT litigation is entering a more adversarial, discovery-conflict phase — the kind of procedural friction that historically precedes either an aggressive damages push or a settlement overture from defendants.

Kadrey v. Meta — Continued active litigation. The Kadrey case remains active and is proceeding through further pleading and discovery. No new docket activity confirmed in the 72-hour window.

Human-authorship doctrine settled. On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in Thaler's appeal, confirming the principle that material must still have human authorship to be copyrightable. This is background law for all training-output claims — the registration prerequisite under 17 U.S.C. § 411 and § 504 statutory damages analysis both assume a human-authored work in the chain. No delta from yesterday.
