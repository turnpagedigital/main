Bartz v. Anthropic, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Pending; Order Awaited

No new docket entry has been substantively briefed since the May 14 fairness hearing and the May 21 supplemental filing deadline, consistent with the tracked-case ground truth showing entry 678 (filed July 10) with a description not yet synced. The case remains under submission before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín.

The settlement posture, as of this advisory: Preliminary approval was granted September 25, 2025; the final fairness hearing was held May 14, 2026, and taken under submission by Judge Martínez-Olguín. (AI Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026ABartz v. Anthropic: Case Status May 2026 (I Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026)) The 75-minute proceeding went smoothly, with only seven objectors appearing. The judge did not rule from the bench. She ordered Anthropic to file a supplemental brief by May 21, 2026, explaining why late opt-outs should not be honored, and indicated she would not require further objector submissions. (LegalClarity, mid-2026LAnthropic AI Settlement: Claims, Payouts, and Deadlines (egalClarity, mid-2026)) As of today, July 12, 2026, final approval has not been confirmed; observers expect the order soon, but payments remain contingent on that approval and the resolution of any appeals. (LegalClarity, mid-2026LAnthropic AI Settlement: Claims, Payouts, and Deadlines (egalClarity, mid-2026))

The fee dispute is the most docket-active sub-issue. After Judge Alsup's December 2025 memorandum questioning the original fee request, class counsel reduced its petition on March 19, 2026, from $300 million (20% of the fund) to $187.5 million (12.5%). (AI Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026ABartz v. Anthropic: Case Status May 2026 (I Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026)) Anthropic separately argued that the $75 million in fees that non-class counsel requested as part of the settlement is far too high, with "scant justification" for the request. (Law360law360.comLaw360) The net distributable fund, once fees and administrative costs are deducted, is approximately $1.29 billion. When all costs are added together, they come to roughly $208.6 million, leaving a net settlement fund of roughly $1.29 billion, translating to an estimated base payout of approximately $2,931 per work, with interest on Anthropic's already-deposited funds likely pushing the per-work figure slightly higher. (Publishers Weekly, April 2026PAnthropic Settlement Hearing Comes into Focus (ublishers Weekly, April 2026))

Claims participation remains the benchmark metric. Claims rate updated to 92.77% — 447,576 of 482,460 works — at the hearing, with roughly 53 objections logged. (AI Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026ABartz v. Anthropic: Case Status May 2026 (I Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026)) That participation rate is extraordinary by class-action standards; as of March 19, only 54% of claims had been reported — the spike to 91%+ reflects the effectiveness of the corrected notice process ordered by Judge Alsup following the ClaimsHero controversy. (Publishers Weekly, April 2026PAnthropic Settlement Hearing Comes into Focus (ublishers Weekly, April 2026))

The structural precedent set here is documented and consequential. The case bifurcates the fair-use question in a way that matters: training on licensed material may be fair use; training on pirated material is not. (AI Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026ABartz v. Anthropic: Case Status May 2026 (I Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026)) For Anthropic, the settlement releases them from liability for past conduct in exchange for a $1.5 billion payment and destruction of the pirated copies. It does not create any forward-looking licensing scheme, and it does not bind any other AI developer or any future case. (Words and Money / Authors Alliance, May 2026WWhat's at Stake—and What Isn't—as Bartz v. Anthropic Settlement Heads Toward Approval (ords and Money / Authors Alliance, May 15, 2026))

The opt-out litigation produced a meaningful downstream case: twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in N.D. Cal. (No. 3:26-cv-04482) demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works. (AI Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026ABartz v. Anthropic: Case Status May 2026 (I Lawsuit Tracker, May 2026)) A follow-on suit, Kwon v. Anthropic, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors. That cluster of opt-out cases now carries the statutory-damages-at-trial risk that the class settlement was meant to resolve for Anthropic.

Concord/UMG v. Anthropic, No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK (N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee) — Cross-MSJs at Submission; Concord II Stay Proceeding

The Concord music-publisher track is now two parallel dockets before Judge Lee in the San Jose Division. The original action — Concord I, No. 5:24-cv-03811 — has completed fact and expert discovery and is approaching summary judgment practice that will determine several threshold legal issues governing these claims. (Anthropic Mot. to Stay, Concord II, filed Mar. 2026NAnthropic's Motion to Stay — Concord II, filed Mar. 10, 2026 (.D. Cal. filing)) Per the briefing schedule, dispositive motions were to be fully briefed by June 8, 2026. (Concord I Dkt. 587, cited in Anthropic Mot. to StayNAnthropic's Motion to Stay — Concord II, filed Mar. 10, 2026 (.D. Cal. filing)) That means cross-MSJs are now fully submitted to Judge Lee; ruling is outstanding.

Anthropic has asked Judge Lee to find that its use of copyrighted materials to train Claude is "transformative" fair use under copyright law, comparing Claude's learning to how humans learn from reading and internalizing the themes of various works. (Law360LConcord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC — Law360 case page (aw360)) The publishers — Concord Music Group, Universal Music Corp., ABKCO Music, and affiliates — counter with a partial-MSJ arguing fair use does not excuse training on lyrics to build a competing commercial product.

The amicus constellation in Concord I is notably broad. RIAA, NMPA, A2IM, SoundExchange, SONA, BMAC, the Music Artists Coalition, and the Artist Rights Alliance filed an amicus brief urging the court to reject Anthropic's fair-use defense. (Music Business Worldwide, April 1, 2026MRIAA, NMPA, and more file amicus brief backing music publishers against Anthropic (usic Business Worldwide, April 1, 2026)) The publishing coalition's brief characterizes Anthropic as a "holdout" that has opted to scrape rather than license, and cites early-2026 academic research challenging AI companies' claims that training data is not retained in their models, pointing to studies showing LLMs memorize copyrighted texts and can reproduce them. (Music Business Worldwide, April 1, 2026MRIAA, NMPA, and more file amicus brief backing music publishers against Anthropic (usic Business Worldwide, April 1, 2026))

The Claude-hallucinated-citation episode continues to carry procedural weight. A California federal magistrate judge partially stricken an expert report filed by Anthropic in this copyright litigation that cited a nonexistent study — an error created by Anthropic's own Claude AI tool — calling the issue "serious," but "not quite so grave as it first appeared." (Law360LConcord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC — Law360 case page (aw360)) A Latham & Watkins associate representing Anthropic said she used Anthropic's own Claude.ai tool to help draft an expert's declaration that included an erroneous citation, but argued the error was "an honest citation mistake and not a fabrication of authority." (Law360LConcord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC — Law360 case page (aw360)) For claim-recovery counsel, this episode is useful: it demonstrates that Anthropic's own model failed to reliably process and accurately represent the very kind of textual material it argues it is merely "learning from."

The Concord II action — No. 5:26-cv-00880-EKL-SVK, filed January 28, 2026 — is the scale-up: the second, larger lawsuit covers more than 20,000 songs and seeks over $3 billion in statutory damages. (Music Business Worldwide, April 1, 2026MRIAA, NMPA, and more file amicus brief backing music publishers against Anthropic (usic Business Worldwide, April 1, 2026)) Anthropic moved to stay Concord II pending resolution of the Concord I cross-MSJs. The threshold legal issues — including whether Anthropic's use of lyrics as training data constitutes fair use — will determine the viability of most if not all of the publishers' claims in Concord II and could have preclusive effect. Concord II should be stayed so that the claims in both cases are resolved in an orderly and consistent manner, Anthropic argued. (Anthropic Mot. to Stay, filed Mar. 10, 2026NAnthropic's Motion to Stay — Concord II, filed Mar. 10, 2026 (.D. System-Level View: § 107 Framework Stress Points

Three developments this week, read together, expose the same fault line in every pending AI-copyright case: what did these companies know about infringement, and when did they know it?

In NYT v. OpenAI, the allegation is that OpenAI had an internal 78-million-conversation database quantifying its own copyright exposure before the suit was filed. In Bartz v. Anthropic, the liability pivot was Anthropic's maintenance of a "central library" of pirated books — not the training act itself, but the retention of the infringing copies. In Concord v. Anthropic, the expert-citation episode demonstrated, in real time, that Claude both ingested and can reproduce copyrighted text verbatim.

All three lines of evidence map directly to 17 U.S.C. § 107(4) — the fourth fair-use factor, "effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." The argument that AI training is non-substitutive becomes harder to sustain when internal tools confirm reproduction, when retention of pirated copies was affirmatively chosen, and when outputs demonstrably reproduce protected expression. The mounting litigation costs come as a growing number of media organizations have signed licensing deals with OpenAI and other AI companies, which typically pay the outlet a fee to train AI systems on their news feeds or archives — evidence that a licensing market exists, which is precisely the market-displacement evidence § 107(4) analysis requires. (Al Jazeera, July 9, 2026ANYT-led group asks court to sanction OpenAI in US copyright dispute (l Jazeera, July 9, 2026))

The NYT itself has spent more than $28 million fighting AI companies in court, including $4.2 million in this year's first quarter alone. (TheStreet, July 10, 2026TOpenAI faces sanctions bid as copyright case escalates (heStreet, July 10, 2026)) That investment signals institutional commitment to trial; the sanctions motion is not a pressure tactic but a litigation posture.

Recommended Actions

Rights-holder counsel and claims-recovery investors should treat three items as immediate. First, on the NYT/OpenAI sanctions motion: monitor the S.D.N.Y. docket for the opposition deadline and any hearing scheduling — an adverse ruling granting evidentiary preclusion or a spoliation-inference jury instruction would be the most significant pre-trial ruling in this litigation cycle, directly accelerating the § 107 analysis in every parallel publisher case including the In re OpenAI Copyright MDL No. 3143; counsel with books in both proceedings should file coordinated monitoring notices now. Second, on Bartz final approval: the order from Judge Martínez-Olguín remains outstanding and could issue any day; claims investors holding positions contingent on final approval should confirm their claims administrator contact at AnthropicCopyrightSettlement.com and note that the per-work net payout is approximately $2,931 (pre-interest), with distribution timing still dependent on approval and any Ninth Circuit objector appeal — the compressed timeline between approval and distribution should be modeled conservatively at 90–180 days post-order. Third, on Concord I: the cross-MSJ ruling from Judge Lee, fully briefed as of June 8, 2026, is the most consequential unissued ruling in the music-publisher AI-copyright track; a finding on whether lyrics-training is transformative under § 107(1) will cascade directly into Concord II (20,000+ works, $3B+ exposure), the BMG v. Anthropic proceeding, and analogous OpenAI music-publisher suits — rights-holder counsel should have supplemental briefing prepared for any court entertaining similar cross-motions, incorporating the RIAA/NMPA amicus arguments and the academic memorization-reproduction studies cited in their March 30 filing.
