I. Bartz v. Anthropic (3:24-cv-05417, N.D. Cal.) — Final Approval Entered; Distribution Timeline Unannounced

Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín has closed the Bartz v. Anthropic case, issuing a 23-page order that grants final approval of the class action settlement, resolves several outstanding administrative details, and rules on attorneys' fees.

The fee award. Class counsel initially sought $300 million, then later trimmed the request to $187.5 million. In the final order, the court approved an approximately $101.5 million payment to the attorneys.

According to Bloomberg, the settlement pays "about $3,100 per author of more than 480,000 works," with claims filed for 440,490 works downloaded from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror; $122 million of the settlement money will go toward attorneys and litigation costs.

Claims participation. In aggregate, this decision marks the largest class action copyright settlement in history, with a reported claims rate of 92.77 percent.

Class counsel separately reported a 91.3% claims rate, and 350 valid opt-outs representing 1,802 works — approximately 0.03% of total works covered. The near-complete participation rate limits dilution risk for claimants but also signals that the opt-out population — which retains independent claims against Anthropic — is numerically modest.

Class definition and § 411 registration prerequisite. As defined by the court, the class includes legal or beneficial copyright owners of any book downloaded by Anthropic through Library Genesis in June 2021 or from Pirate Library Mirror in July 2022, with an ISBN or Amazon ASIN, that was registered with the Copyright Office within five years of first publication, and was registered before being downloaded by Anthropic, or within three months of first publication. The registration-timing filter is the operative § 411 screen — works outside that window are not class members and remain available for individual suit.

Objector handling and non-class-member posture. Judge Martínez-Olguín overruled all 53 objections filed with the court opposing the settlement on various grounds.

The court declined to entertain objections from non-class members, but took care to remind those individuals that, precisely because the settlement does not cover them, they remain free to bring their own claims — which is already happening, as the Chicken Soup for the Soul and Carreyrou suits are separate individual actions, and the court's order may prompt others to follow.

Non-admission; fair-use posture preserved for Anthropic. Anthropic, in agreeing to the settlement, denied any wrongdoing, and the infringement question has not been adjudicated. Anthropic's public statement after final approval reinforced this: "Training AI on books is fair use under copyright law," said Aparna Sridhar, deputy general counsel for Anthropic, in a statement to NPR. That position — commercially understandable — is the crux of the strategic tension in this settlement for rights-holders in the parallel docket: a payment of $1.5 billion accompanied by a non-admission leaves the fair-use question unresolved for subsequent cases involving legally acquired works, not pirated corpora.

Distribution timeline. The Authors Guild stated it looks forward to the commencement of the distribution process so that authors can start getting paid, noting that while a specific date for the commencement of payouts has not been announced, they hope and expect it will happen quickly. No distribution date is confirmed as of this writing.

Author-side reaction and the structural critique. The overall settlement amount may seem large, but thousands of authors are involved and have to split the money with publishers; more than $100 million went to plaintiffs' lawyers; and perhaps more importantly, the case could be seen as more of a win for AI companies than for the publishing industry — because within the narrow terms of this particular lawsuit, the court did not find it illegal for Anthropic to train its AI algorithms on authors' copyrighted works, as long as the company pays for the books it uses. Named plaintiff Charles Graeber, whose The Good Nurse was among the works at issue, expressed ambivalence: Graeber said he is entitled to around $3,100 in compensation for each of the two books he wrote that were used to feed Claude. The practical implication for claims investors: the per-work recovery figure functions as a data point for § 107(4) market-harm evidence in downstream cases — it establishes a revealed market-clearing price for one AI company's access to one pirated corpus, which is a floor, not a ceiling, for licensed access to curated, high-quality training sets.

Ninth Circuit watch. The 23-page final-approval order has a 30-day objector-appeal window running from July 20. No notice of appeal has been confirmed in the tracked docket as of the July 30 data pull (date_last_filing: 2026-07-21, new_in_72h: 0). Monitor CourtListener docket 69058235 for any Rule 23(e)(5)(B) appeal by a named objector before approximately August 19, 2026.

II. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic (5:24-cv-03811, N.D. Cal., Judge Eumi K. Lee) — Discovery-Informed Amended Complaint Filed July 22

This is the active litigation that now carries the most near-term evidentiary significance on the AI-copyright docket.

The battle between the music publishing industry and AI developers intensified as Concord Music Group, Universal Music Publishing Group, and ABKCO Music filed an expanded complaint against Anthropic; the publishers, who first sued Anthropic in 2023, allege the company unlawfully copied and reproduced copyrighted song lyrics as part of the development and operation of its AI models. Their latest filing, submitted in federal court in California on July 22, builds on earlier claims and incorporates information obtained through the discovery process.

At the heart of the dispute is the publishers' claim that Claude was trained on copyrighted lyrics and was designed to provide those lyrics in response to user requests; according to the amended complaint, internal records show Anthropic employees and contractors prompted the system with requests for copyrighted lyrics during the model's development. That allegation — that reproduction was intentional and employee-directed — is significant for the § 107(1) fair-use factor analysis: it cuts against a transformative-use defense and toward willful infringement, which lifts the § 504(c)(2) statutory-damages ceiling to $150,000 per work.

The docket also reflects an administrative motion order signed by Judge Lee on July 29 (CourtListener docket 68889092, new_in_72h: 1), consistent with active case management running concurrent with the amended pleading. Magistrate Judge Susan G. Van Keulen is handling discovery referrals. The amended complaint is joined by: Concord Music Group, Inc.; Universal Music Corp.; Songs of Universal Inc.; ABKCO Music Inc.; Polygram Publishing Inc.; Universal Music – Z Tunes LLC; Capitol CMG, Inc.; and Universal Music – MGB NA LLC — a broader plaintiff roster than the original filing, which matters for the scope of the works-at-issue list.

The infringement allegedly occurred when lyrics were copied as part of the model training process, and when those lyrics were reproduced and distributed in response to prompts using Claude; the publishers argue that direct infringement took place at three stages: copying in initial data ingestion, additional reproduction in fine-tuning, and producing near-identical copies as outputs. The three-stage theory is structurally more aggressive than the Bartz framing, which centered on downloading from pirated book repositories. It may also be harder to settle on the same liability-agnostic terms because the reproduction-in-output claim is directly justiciable.

The parallel second Concord action (5:26-cv-00880, N.D. Cal., also before Judge Lee and Magistrate Van Keulen, filed January 28, 2026) addresses a separate set of works and remains in early proceedings.

III. In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation (1:25-md-03143, S.D.N.Y., Judge Sidney H. Stein) — Newspaper Coalition Seeks Sanctions Over Alleged Evidence Destruction

A group of newspapers including the New York Times and New York Daily News asked a federal court in Manhattan on July 9 to sanction OpenAI in their high-stakes copyright dispute, alleging that OpenAI falsely told the court it could not search its large language models for their copyrighted material while hiding that it had done so "even before the first News Plaintiff filed suit" — and that OpenAI also deleted billions of relevant ChatGPT conversations or made them unsearchable.

The newspapers asked the court for sanctions, including attorneys' fees, and a court finding that OpenAI's chat logs showed the company misused their copyrighted works. A finding of the latter would effectively create adverse-inference evidence at trial on the § 107(4) market-harm factor, the fourth and most dispositive fair-use consideration in the Second Circuit. Judge Stein, with Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang handling discovery, has not yet ruled on that motion as of this writing.

The MDL is assigned to Judge Sidney H. Stein and referred to Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang, captioned In Re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation, 1:25-md-03143 (S.D.N.Y.).

On April 4, 2025, Judge Stein largely denied the defendants' motion to dismiss, allowing the suit to proceed toward discovery and trial. No trial date has been set.

The sanctions motion is material for the broader AI-copyright system for two reasons. First, if granted, it could produce a de facto adverse inference that training data included copyrighted news articles at scale — collateral estoppel implications for related claims within the MDL are significant. Second, the alleged deletion of ChatGPT conversations, if proven, would accelerate pressure on other AI defendants in active discovery to demonstrate preservation. Counsel in Concord v. Anthropic and Kadrey v. Meta (3:23-cv-03223, N.D. Cal.) should be reviewing their own preservation compliance correspondingly.

IV. Structural Read: What the Bartz Settlement Means for the Remaining Docket

The Bartz outcome is a reference transaction, not a resolution of the underlying legal question. Three implications for rights-holder counsel and claims investors:

1. Piracy-premium vs. licensed-access pricing. The $3,100-per-work per-pirated-copy figure, applied across approximately 440,000 works, totals $1.5 billion without any adjudication of fair use. For works in legitimate curated datasets — higher provenance, higher quality — a willing-buyer/willing-seller market price should command a premium. That premium is the § 107(4) market-harm argument in every remaining case: if an AI company would pay $3,100 for a pirated copy to settle liability, the market value of a licensed copy is at minimum that figure.

2. The non-admission cuts both ways. Anthropic's denial that training on legally acquired books is infringement — backed by Sridhar's post-settlement statement to NPR — means the Bartz settlement creates no collateral estoppel on the fair-use question. The Concord publishers' output-reproduction theory is the vehicle most likely to generate an adjudicated ruling on fair use. Judge Lee's docket is the one to watch for the first substantive fair-use ruling in a music-publisher AI case.

3. Individual opt-out claims. The 350 opt-outs covering 1,802 works are a tiny fraction of the class, but they represent holders who elected to preserve individual damages claims — potentially including willfulness-enhanced statutory damages under § 504(c)(2). If any opted-out claimant holds a commercially prominent work (e.g., a major trade nonfiction title with documented commercial harm), that claim is more valuable post-settlement than before, because the per-work settlement figure now anchors the minimum reasonable recovery in any negotiated resolution.

For works outside the class (unregistered within the window, or not on the LibGen/PiLiMi download lists), document the chain of title and any evidence of actual AI-training use now — the Concord amended complaint's internal-document allegations suggest discovery in that case will generate model training records that could be relevant to independent infringement claims. Counsel in the OpenAI MDL should evaluate the July 9 newspaper sanctions motion carefully: a favorable ruling against OpenAI on the chat-log deletion issue would be the single most significant evidence-preservation ruling in AI-copyright litigation to date, and its adverse-inference implications would extend well beyond the Times track.
